# Voice

How the editions sound. Principles only, for now: the worked examples get
written after two real editions have been read, against sentences the
generator actually produced. Until then the templates are neutral, and
this file says what they are aiming at.

## The register

Dry. Nothing else. The reader is one person who already knows football
and does not need to be told a 5–0 was emphatic.

Four devices are in bounds. Everything else is out.

**Understatement and litotes.** Say less than the facts support and let
the gap do the work. Six straight defeats are "not their best month".
A 6–0 is "comfortable". The record itself carries the weight; the prose
stands slightly to one side of it.

**Bathos.** Build the sentence up and let the last clause bring it down.
Sixty-eight years of history, a founder member of the league, top-flight
champions twice – currently 19th in League Two. The database is built
for this: every club has a grand past and a present, and the distance
between them is the joke.

**Deadpan formality.** Civil-service prose applied to absurd situations.
"The club has now gone fourteen matches without a goal, which is noted."
Never wink. The formality *is* the wink.

**Self-deprecation.** The editions were wrong about something on Friday
and say so on Monday, plainly. "The case for this fixture rested on
Southend's form. Southend lost 3–0." Not a verdict label, not a tally –
the sentence names the expectation and what happened, and stops.

## Arrangement over adjectives

Most of the humour is in which facts sit next to which, not in the words
between them. A club's 89% share of its recorded seasons in the National
League, beside its LLLL form, is funnier than any adjective about the
form. The selection layer – what goes in, in what order – is where the
voice lives. The phrasing layer adds a dry connective and gets out of
the way.

Practical rule: if a sentence is trying to be funny on its own, cut the
attempt and check whether the facts beside it are already doing it.

## Where it applies

Everywhere: prose, section headers, fixture tags, subject lines. A tag
may be wry ("nothing at stake"). A subject line may be dry. Score grids
and tables stay straight – numbers are not a place for a voice.

## Suppression

Human events force a straight tone: deaths, disasters, tragedies, and
their anniversaries. When one applies to a club or a date, every mention
of that club in that edition is plain, subject line included.

`content/straight-dates.yml` holds them. It starts empty and is added to
as events arise; it is not seeded from a list. Club distress –
administration, points deductions, winding-up petitions – is not on it
and stays fair game.

## Banned

- Puns. Wordplay on club names, player names, or place names.
- Exclamation marks.
- "Cheeky", "banter", "bants", "lads", "scenes".
- Any sentence that explains its own joke, or signals one is coming.
- Sarcasm aimed at supporters. Clubs and boards are fair; fans are not.
- Mock outrage, capitals for emphasis, rhetorical questions.
- Referring to the newsletter as "we" with a personality. It is a
  process, and the self-deprecation is about the process being wrong.

## Sources to read before the examples are written

The devices above are well documented and the examples half of this
guide should be written against actual sources, not from recall:
Wodehouse and Pratchett for bathos; Private Eye and the civil-service
prose it parodies for deadpan formality; Partridge and Peep Show for
self-deprecation that does not beg for sympathy; the tradition of
football writing that already does this – Brian Glanville, the Football
Ramble in its plainer moments, the deadpan of a Sunday broadsheet's
League Two round-up.
