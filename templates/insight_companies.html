The grant bodies – the Football Stadia Improvement Fund, Sport England, the Football Foundation, the Premier League Stadium Fund – take a charge over a ground as security for a grant, so the money is repaid if the ground stops being used for football. A charge from them is a safeguard, not a debt in the usual sense. The banks and specialist lenders are the borrowing.

How it is worked out

From the Companies House public data API (Crown copyright, Open Government Licence), fetched monthly. Each club is matched to the company it plays as: by name for most, scored against every company of a similar name; for the rest, only with evidence from the register itself – a charge naming the ground, a director or controller matching the club's known owner, the operating company under a holding company, or a football-named company registered near the ground. A club with no such evidence is left out rather than guessed at, and many small clubs are community benefit societies, which file with the FCA and are not on the register at all. "Controller" is the furthest person with significant control the register reaches: anyone holding over a quarter of the shares or votes, or able to appoint the board, followed upwards through UK companies. A chain that reaches a company registered abroad ends there.
