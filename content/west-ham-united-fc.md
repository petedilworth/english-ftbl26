## Origins

West Ham United traces to Thames Ironworks F.C., founded in 1895 when foreman Dave Taylor proposed a works team to shipbuilding company chairman Arnold Hills, who had just endured a bitter industrial dispute with his own workforce and saw a football club as a way to rebuild morale. The company itself only sponsored the side for five years: Thames Ironworks became a public limited company in 1900 and could no longer fund a team as a corporate concern, so the club was formally wound up that June and refounded days later, independent and professional, as West Ham United. The "Irons" nickname is a direct carry-over from the ironworks name; "Hammers" comes from the crossed rivet hammers on the club crest, representing the tools of shipbuilding rather than a contraction of the club's own name.

The claret-and-blue colours are the one part of the story without a settled answer. The commonly repeated version involves a sprinting bet at a fair near Villa Park in 1899, in which Thames Ironworks player Charlie Dove's father beat several Aston Villa players and was given an Aston Villa shirt in lieu of payment, which the club is said to have then adopted — a story told consistently across fan and club sources but always as "the story goes," with no contemporary document, minute or press report ever produced to support it. Kit historians offer a more granular, photograph-traceable account instead: the side wore plain dark blue in 1895, switched to blue-and-white in 1897, added claret trim around 1899-1900 as what multiple sources describe as the ironworks' own commercial colour, then a claret hoop in 1901, with the settled claret body and light-blue sleeves visible in photographs from January 1904. That version doesn't fully explain the coincidence with Villa's own colours either — the honest position, on the evidence available, is that no account has been documented well enough to call settled.

## Trajectory

West Ham has no administration or insolvency in its history, and none of the financial-crisis pattern common to several other clubs in this database. Its one significant off-pitch disciplinary episode came in 2006, when West Ham signed Carlos Tevez and Javier Mascherano under undisclosed third-party ownership arrangements that breached Premier League rules. The League fined the club a then-record £5.5m in April 2007 but imposed no points deduction — and Tevez went on to score the winner away at Manchester United on the final day of that season, keeping West Ham up and sending Sheffield United down in their place. Sheffield United sued over the decision and the clubs settled out of court in 2009 for a reported figure around £20m, paid over five years — a rare case of a boardroom compliance failure directly deciding who stayed up.

The 2016 move from Upton Park to the London Stadium (detailed under Infrastructure) coincided with a run of unremarkable finishes — 11th, 13th, 10th, 16th across the following four seasons — before David Moyes's second spell brought sixth and seventh-place finishes and the UEFA Europa Conference League title in 2023, the club's first major trophy in 43 years, complicating any simple story that the move itself damaged the team. What has persisted regardless of results is dissatisfaction with the stadium's atmosphere, running alongside periodic organised protest against the Sullivan-Gold ownership. West Ham were relegated from the Premier League at the end of 2025-26, finishing 18th after three managerial changes in eighteen months — David Moyes's departure at contract expiry, Julen Lopetegui sacked in January 2025 after six wins in twenty league games, and Graham Potter sacked early into 2025-26 before Nuno Espírito Santo's appointment. Net summer transfer spend trailed both promoted clubs that year, and a defensive reinforcement wasn't signed until deadline day in January 2026. The honest reading is that this was substantially a results-and-recruitment failure sitting on top of real managerial instability, rather than a deeper financial mismanagement story of the kind that produced other clubs' relegations in this database.

## Ownership & Finance

West Ham's previous owner, Icelandic businessman Björgólfur Guðmundsson, bought the club in 2006 for a reported £85m financed through heavy borrowing from Icelandic bank Landsbanki. Iceland's 2008 banking collapse wiped out that structure: Landsbanki was nationalised that October, ownership passed to fellow Guðmundsson-linked lender Straumur in 2009, and Guðmundsson himself was declared bankrupt by Icelandic courts that July with debts near £500m — financial distress imported wholesale from a national banking crisis rather than caused by anything at the club. David Sullivan and David Gold, previously owners of Birmingham City, bought a controlling 50% stake in January 2010 for around £50m, against reported club debt of £110-120m, and increased their holding to 60% that May.

David Gold died in January 2023, with his shareholding passing to his daughter Vanessa Gold. Czech billionaire Daniel Křetínský bought a 27% stake in 2021 for around £170m, valuing the club at roughly £600m. The ownership structure has moved rapidly and remains unsettled as of this writing in August 2026: David Sullivan resigned as joint-chairman on 6 June 2026 over unrelated personal allegations he says he will contest, while remaining the largest individual shareholder at around 38.8%; Křetínský and Vanessa Gold then agreed a deal raising his stake to 43% with the two voting jointly; within weeks, however, reports indicated Vanessa Gold had instead agreed to sell the Gold family's 25.1% stake to a rival consortium fronted by Amanda Staveley and Mehrdad Ghodoussi, of the 2021 Newcastle United takeover, alongside Chicago's Ashland Forest Capital Partners, for a reported £150m — a deal still subject to pre-emption rights at time of writing. The final ownership split should be treated as unresolved rather than settled.

## Infrastructure & Environment

West Ham played at the Boleyn Ground, commonly known as Upton Park, from 1904 to 2016 — 112 years, ending with a 3-2 win over Manchester United in the final match there. The ground took its name from an adjoining property once associated with Anne Boleyn.

The move to the London Stadium, built as the Olympic Stadium for London 2012, produced one of English football's clearest public-subsidy controversies. Conversion to a football-ready configuration was originally estimated at £190m but an independent review found the actual cost had risen to around £323m; West Ham's own direct contribution was only £15m, with the remainder borne by the London Legacy Development Corporation, Newham Council and ultimately London taxpayers via the Mayor of London's office. The club signed a 99-year lease with no break clause on the public side, at reported rent around £2-2.5m a year, while the stadium operator continues to cover most matchday running costs on top of that; 2026 reporting put the stadium's ongoing loss to taxpayers at roughly £19m a year, a figure Mayor Sadiq Khan has publicly attributed to terms negotiated under his predecessor. The ground has never carried a sponsor's name — a prospective Allianz deal collapsed in 2024 when the insurer chose to sponsor Twickenham instead. The stadium's retained Olympic running track, pushing spectators back from the pitch, is consistently cited by fans and pundits as the structural cause of the atmosphere complaints that have followed the move since 2016, in direct contrast to Upton Park's tighter, older character.
