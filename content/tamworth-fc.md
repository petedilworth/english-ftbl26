## Origins

Tamworth FC exists because somebody wrote to the local paper.

**Michael Flowers**, a London businessman who had moved to the area, complained
in the *Tamworth Herald* that his new home town had no senior football club —
the previous one, **Tamworth Castle FC**, having ceased to exist. The club was
formed in **April 1933** in response. It is not a continuation of the Castle
club and does not claim to be: a town filling a hole, which is also how Burton
Albion came about down the road.

The first ground was the **Jolly Sailor**; the club moved to **The Lamb Ground**
in 1934 and has been there ever since. The nickname follows the ground, and the
ground follows a pub: **the Lamb Inn** stood next to the site, and in the early
years it served as the club's changing rooms and clubhouse. So "the Lambs" is a
pub name that reached the club through its address. Tamworth's famous animal is
the **Tamworth pig**, and it has nothing to do with any of this.

## Trajectory

Tamworth reached the fifth tier for the first time in **2003**, seventy years
after they were founded, as **Southern League Premier champions by thirteen
points**. What followed is the most in-and-out record on this site: **eleven
completed seasons at that level, and twelve missing.**

Four seasons up to 2007, then down. Two seasons below, then back in 2009. Five
seasons to 2014, then down again — and this time they kept going. Relegated
from National League North in **2018**, they spent five seasons in the seventh
tier, won it in **2022/23 on goal difference** over Coalville Town, and won
**National League North in 2023/24** conceding twenty-seven goals in
forty-four games. Back-to-back promotions returned them to the fifth tier after
**a ten-season absence** — long, but nowhere near the record; Accrington's is
forty-one and Wealdstone's thirty-two.

**In 2004/05 Tamworth were docked three points for fielding an ineligible
player.** That sentence could not have been written from this site's data until
this page was researched: the table recorded fourteenth place on fifty-three
points, which is what the results produced, and the published table has them
fifteenth on fifty. The gap was not a Tamworth quirk — the whole of that
Conference season had been loaded without its sanctions, including the ten
points Northwich Victoria lost for entering administration. Both are now in.

What is **not** in the record, anywhere, is a ground-grading refusal, a
financial sanction or a regulatory demotion. Every level Tamworth have changed,
they changed by results. For a club whose pitch has been a live regulatory
question since 2016, that is worth stating.

## Ownership & Finance

**Bob Andrews and Steve Lathbury ran Tamworth for around forty years**, and in
**2026 they sold to Dawn Group International**, the vehicle of a
twenty-four-year-old American commercial real-estate investor, who became
chairman. The stated ambition is the Football League, with investment in
infrastructure and the stadium.

Before that, the club's defining financial event was a cup run that lost money.

In **2024/25 Tamworth beat Huddersfield Town 1–0** in the first round of the FA
Cup and **knocked Burton Albion out on penalties** in the second — two League
One clubs — to draw **Tottenham Hotspur at home** in the third. On 12 January
2025 they held Spurs to **0–0 for ninety minutes** and lost 3–0 in extra time.

Under the rules of any previous season, 0–0 after ninety minutes would have
bought them a replay at the Tottenham Hotspur Stadium. **The FA had abolished
replays from the first round proper for 2024/25.** The football finance
academic Kieran Maguire put the forgone replay at around **£850,000 in gate
receipts plus £25,000 for television** — roughly **£875,000**. Against that,
the prize money for beating two League One clubs and drawing with a Premier
League side came to about **£145,000**: £45,000 for round one, £75,000 for round
two, £25,000 for losing in round three.

The comparison the coverage kept making is Marine, whose **£800,000** from
hosting Spurs in 2021 paid for a complete ground refurbishment. Running a
National League club was reported at around **£150,000 a month** — so the
replay would have covered close to half a season.

The bill landed on the supporters instead. Tamworth priced the Spurs tie at
**£42 for an adult seat and £38 on the terrace**, against a normal £20 and free
entry for under-tens; the supporters' club chairman called it unfair in public
and some regulars said they would watch on television. That is the documented
consequence of the rule change: a club with no replay to sell trying to extract
its value from its own crowd in one afternoon. The FA subsequently looked again
at the rule, prompted by this tie.

What the money was **for** is on the record — the chairman named better dressing
rooms and "a stand down the bottom end". Whether it was spent on them is not,
and this page does not claim it was.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**The Lamb Ground has an artificial pitch, and that is the most consequential
thing about this club.**

A FIFA two-star 3G surface was installed in **summer 2016**, while Tamworth were
in National League North, and it levelled out the slope the ground had always
had. The reasoning was explicitly financial and civic: more football on the
site, academy fixtures at the stadium, community hire — described by the
contractor as a development that would help sustain the club financially. It
was a survival measure, and it worked.

It is also a trap. **No EFL club may play on a fully artificial surface**, and
**National League rules provide that a club which wins promotion and does not
install grass is demoted to National League North or South, and may be fined.**
When Sutton United went up in 2021 the conversion cost them **around £500,000**;
Harrogate, the year before, paid "upwards of £200k" and had to groundshare at
Doncaster while it was laid. Both of those pages are on this site.

So the new owner's stated goal carries a six-figure pitch bill before anything
else, and the pitch in question was laid by the previous regime precisely
because the club needed the income. The two halves of the ownership story are in
direct conflict, and the conflict is written into the turf.

It reached the Premier League, briefly: **Tottenham's manager said publicly
that Tamworth's artificial surface would affect his team selection** for the cup
tie — a fifth-tier club's cost-saving decision shaping a Premier League team
sheet.

Who owns the ground is genuinely unresolved. One line of sources has the club
owning it outright; against that, **Tamworth Borough Council maintains a page
for the football club, and in 2026 was reported to be considering backing the
club to own the ground it plays on** — which only makes sense if the council
holds the freehold and the club a lease. Whether that transfer completed is not
established, and this page does not pick a side.

The rest is scale. Capacity is a little over four thousand with **only about 518
seats**, so the ground is roughly nine-tenths terrace — which is a cost decision
and also why a £42 seat for the Spurs tie hit as hard as it did. There were only
518 of them.

And the catchment. **No senior club sits within ten miles of Tamworth.** Within
fifteen there are eight, six of them in the top four tiers and two of them in
the Premier League: Aston Villa 11.7 miles, Birmingham City 12.9, Walsall 13.5,
Burton 13.7, West Bromwich Albion 14.2, Solihull Moors 14.3, Coventry 14.7.
The town is its own island, and the island sits inside four overlapping
big-club catchments at once.
