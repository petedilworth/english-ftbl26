## Origins

Hull City was founded in 1904 and admitted to the Football League a year
later, playing its first match in white shirts with black shorts. The amber
and black stripes arrived in that first League season, and the nickname
followed almost immediately: a columnist writing as "Athleo" in the *Hull
Daily Mail* had dubbed the side the Tigers on account of the strip by March
1905, with the reasoning set out plainly — "We have our 'All Blacks' and our
'Robins', so why should not the wearers of the yellow and black stripes be
'the Tigers'?" The stripes were worn continuously until the Second World War
apart from a single season, 1935-36, in sky blue.

That date matters more than it looks, because a century later the club's
owner would propose replacing "Hull City" with "Hull Tigers" on the grounds
that the second was the more marketable name. The nickname he wanted to
promote was 109 years older than the proposal, and had been given to the club
by a local journalist describing a shirt. It is the reason the campaign
against the change could be run on heritage rather than sentiment: both
halves of the name were old, and only one of them was being kept.

## Trajectory

Hull City is the clearest case in the recent English record of a governing
body deciding what a club may call itself — and the most instructive part is
that the club won its legal challenge and still did not get the name.

The sequence runs like this. In spring 2013 the corporate entity had the
"Association Football Club" element removed at Companies House, becoming Hull
City Tigers Ltd. On 9 August 2013 the owner, Assem Allam, announced the club
would be marketed as "Hull City Tigers"; supporters marched before the first
home game of the season behind a banner reading "Hull City AFC: a club not a
brand." In November he confirmed the intention to go further, to "Hull
Tigers", and in December the club formally applied to the FA to change its
playing name.

The mechanism the application ran into is worth stating, because it is the
whole story. Under FA rules an application to change a club's playing name
must reach the Association on or before 31 December of a playing season to be
considered for the following one, and the decision is taken at the governing
body's absolute discretion. The owner of the club is not the decision-maker.
In March 2014 the FA's Membership Committee voted unanimously to recommend
rejection. The club balloted its own season card holders that month and the
result settled nothing: 2,565 in favour, 2,517 against, and 792 who said they
were not too concerned, from 5,874 responses out of 15,033 holders. On 9
April 2014 the FA Council rejected the application, carried by 63.5% of
members.

The club's response was to unveil a crest for 2014-15 carrying no club name
at all — a tiger's head and the year 1904 — and to commence arbitration
under FA Rule K. It won. A panel of Sir Stanley Burnton, Tim Kerr QC and
Nicholas Stewart QC unanimously set the FA Council's decision aside as
procedurally unlawful. But quashing a decision for unfairness restores the
process, not the outcome: the FA confirmed in March 2015 that the club could
apply afresh for 2015-16, with the new application due by 1 April. Hull
reapplied, and on 10 July 2015 the FA Council rejected it again, this time by
69.9%.

The playing name never changed. Hull City AFC is what the club is called, and
what it has been called throughout. The accounts, however, are still filed by
Hull City Tigers Limited — the entity name attached to the club's financial
data in this database for both 2023/24 and 2024/25. The FA had jurisdiction
over one name and none over the other, and eleven years later the two are
still different.

Underneath the naming fight, the footballing trajectory is unusually
compressed. Hull spent 1996/97 to 2003/04 in the fourth tier — eight
consecutive seasons — and reached the top flight for the first time in the
club's history in 2008/09. Before that promotion, Hull was the standard
answer to the question of the largest English city never to have hosted
top-division football, a claim that holds on local-authority population but
by a narrow margin over Plymouth, and one that depends on Hull's unusually
tightly drawn city boundaries. They have been up and down since — five
seasons in the Premier League across three spells, relegated most recently in
2017 — and were promoted again through the play-offs in 2025/26.

## Ownership & Finance

Hull have been locked out of one ground they did not own and unable to buy
another, and the two facts are the same fact seen from opposite sides.

The Needler family's ownership ended in July 1997 with a sale to David Lloyd.
In November 1998 Lloyd sold the club and kept Boothferry Park, and in a
dispute over rent the following year the club was locked out of its own
stadium — at the same time as facing a High Court winding-up petition over
around £500,000 of unpaid VAT, with a further Inland Revenue threat in
February 2001. Adam Pearson became chairman in March 2001 and took the club
out of that. He sold in June 2007, for a reported £13m, to a consortium of
Paul Duffen, Russell Bartlett and Martin Walker — and the sale included the
stadium's management company as well as the club. Assem Allam completed his
purchase on 16 December 2010 for a nominal £1, with the club reported to be
carrying more than £30m of debt and close to administration. He put the club
on the market in the same period as the FA refused the rename, having stated
repeatedly that he would sell if it was refused, and the sale eventually
completed on 19 January 2022, when the EFL approved a takeover by Acun
Ilıcalı's Acun Medya for a reported £30m.

The financial position under the current ownership is the sharpest in this
database. Hull is the only club appearing twice among the six highest
wage-to-turnover ratios recorded here: 140% in 2023/24, on turnover of £21.2m
against a £29.6m wage bill, and 142% in 2024/25, on £25.8m against £36.7m.
The pattern begins earlier — in 2022/23 revenue was £18m and wages rose 86%
in a single year to £24m, 131% of turnover, with an operating loss of £21m.
Hull's last Premier League season was 2016/17, so parachute payments were
long gone by the time those ratios were recorded; the money is owner funding
directed at a promotion push, and the 24% wage rise and more than doubled
player amortisation in 2024/25 are what that looks like in the accounts. It
is not new behaviour at this club either: the Allam ownership absorbed around
£55m of losses before reaching the Premier League in 2008.

Spending ahead of income has had concluded regulatory consequences. Hull were
among eight clubs under EFL transfer embargoes in July 2021, and had one
lifted in February 2022 when a loan was repaid in full. On 3 July 2025 the
EFL imposed a transfer embargo and a three-window fee restriction following
late payment of transfer and loan fees, the most prominent being around £1m
owed to Aston Villa. The club appealed; in August 2025 the appeal to overturn
the restriction failed, the panel did not accept the club's justification on
payment dates, and the restriction was reduced from three windows to two with
the third suspended for twelve months, triggerable on a further default. A
later embargo, over an overdue loan payment to Manchester City, was lifted
once the debt was settled. The owner has publicly attributed the position to
a cash-flow problem caused by money owed to the club not arriving.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Boothferry Park was built by a consortium led by the local builder Harold
Needler and opened on 31 August 1946, a goalless draw with Lincoln City in
front of 25,586, with its own railway platform serving it. It declined from
the 1970s, acquiring the affectionate name "Fer Ark" after letters fell off
the sign and were not replaced, and held its last competitive match on 14
December 2002.

Its replacement was built with public money. Hull City Council developed the
stadium on The Circle, the former Hull Cricket Club ground, at a cost of
around £44m, of which the council provided more than £42m — funded
substantially by selling part of its shareholding in Kingston Communications,
the municipally owned telephone company, with the balance from Single
Regeneration Budget grants and the Football Stadium Improvement Fund. It
opened on 18 December 2002 against Sunderland. The council kept the freehold
and let the operation to a management company on a fifty-year arrangement
beginning 16 December 2002, with break points at 2012 and 2037, on a
peppercorn rent plus service charges.

The structural oddity is what that produced. In August 2011 the *Yorkshire
Post*, working from a freedom of information request, reported that the
council had received £850.15 in lease payments for the year to July 2010 —
about £16 a week — on a stadium it had paid £46m for, while itself paying
£72,500 a year to rent office space inside it. The figure needs its context:
it is one year under a peppercorn-plus-service-charge lease rather than a
headline rent, and the counterparty management company was at that point
owned by the club's own owner. But the shape of the arrangement is real. The
club's owner controlled the operating income; the council owned the asset.

Attempts to reunite them failed. In 2011 Allam held talks with the council
about buying the freehold as part of a reported £100m sports village — pool,
ice arena, tennis, squash, gymnastics, a substantially expanded ground — and
the talks ended without agreement that September. The council declined to
sell, stating that it wanted public assets maintained for all the city's
teams and citing Hull Fair, held on the adjacent car park, a civic
constraint on a stadium transaction that few other clubs would encounter. It
also stated it had never received a formal offer. In December 2012 Allam
raised the possibility of building elsewhere. Naming rights have changed
three times regardless — KC Stadium, KCOM Stadium from 2016, and the MKM
Stadium from 25 June 2021 under a deal contracted with the management
company rather than with the council.

The ground is shared with Hull FC, the rugby league club, which makes a
council-owned stadium a genuinely civic asset rather than a football one; in
April 2021 the council publicly intervened in a contractual dispute between
the management company and the rugby club, stating that both clubs were
essential to the sporting culture of the city. The crossover runs deeper than
the tenancy: Adam Pearson, Hull City's chairman through the 2000s, took
charge of Hull FC in 2011.

Private ownership locked the football club out of its ground in 1999. Public
ownership then gave it a stadium it could not be locked out of and could not
buy. Both are versions of the same problem, and the club has spent a quarter
of a century on one side or the other of it.
