## Origins

Oxford United were founded in 1893 as **Headington FC**, at a meeting in the
Britannia Inn called by a local doctor so that Headington's cricketers had
something to do through the winter. A second village side, Headington Quarry,
appeared in 1907, and the two **merged in 1911 as Headington United** — which
means the "United" in the name, and the nickname that comes from it, belong
to a village half a century before the club took the city's name.

That renaming is the most consequential thing the club has ever done, and it
was done for an entirely practical reason: to get elected to the Football
League. The FA rejected the change in 1959; the club appealed on the grounds
that "Headington" was too parochial for a side competing nationally, and won.
The name changed on 25 June 1960.

The election results make the case better than any argument could. Standing
as Headington United, the club received **no votes in 1953, two in 1958, and
ten in 1960**. Renamed, it got **nineteen in 1961**. When Accrington Stanley
resigned in March 1962 and twenty-six clubs applied for the vacancy, Oxford
United took **thirty-nine**, against five for the nearest challenger. A
village club bought the prestige of a university city's name, and roughly
doubled its support in the boardroom with every ballot.

## Trajectory

From that election it took twenty-three years to reach the First Division,
and the club won the League Cup in 1986 — its only major honour. The UEFA Cup
place that came with it **was withdrawn**, because English clubs were serving
the ban imposed after Heysel. The single greatest achievement in the club's
history was made worthless abroad by a collective punishment it had no part
in.

Three years earlier, the club had come closer to abolition than to Europe.

In April 1983 **Robert Maxwell, who had bought Oxford out of near-bankruptcy
the year before, announced an agreement to merge the club with Reading** as
"Thames Valley Royals", to play at a proposed new stadium somewhere between
the two cities. **Oxford's own board voted unanimously in favour of it.** Two
thousand supporters staged a sit-in at the Manor Ground and Reading fans
marched.

What actually stopped it was neither the FA nor the Football League, which
never ruled on the merits. It was **company law**: a Reading director and a
Reading shareholder challenged the legitimacy of the share transfer that gave
the pro-merger faction control, and the High Court froze trading in the
disputed shares until mid-June. The Football League required squads to be
confirmed by 21 May. The injunction ran the clock out, and the merger died on
timing.

Maxwell's other lasting mark on English football is regulatory. Holding
Oxford and, from 1987, Derby County, he tried to buy Watford. **The Football
League responded by barring a member club's major shareholder from owning
more than two per cent of another** — the game's first modern
dual-ownership rule. His existing holdings were grandfathered; the Watford
purchase was blocked.

After his death in 1991 the club passed to the receivers and was sold on
twice. The long fall ended on the final day of May 2006, when a home defeat
by Leyton Orient dropped Oxford out of the Football League — **the first
winner of a major English trophy to fall into non-league football**. They came
back through the Conference play-off final in 2010, in front of 42,669 people
at Wembley.

## Ownership & Finance

The cause of the collapse was a stadium rather than a squad. After the Taylor
Report the Manor Ground could not practically be made all-seater, so the club
committed to a new build at Minchery Farm. Construction began in 1996 and was
**suspended in December 1997 for lack of money**, where it stayed for over two
years. By late 1998 staff were going unpaid and supporters had formed a
pressure group to keep the club alive.

**Firoz Kassam bought the controlling interest for £1 in April 1999**,
inheriting an estimated £15m of debt, and restructured it: a company voluntary
arrangement cut around £9m of debt to roughly £900,000, with unsecured
creditors receiving ten pence in the pound, and the secured creditors were
paid off **by selling the Manor Ground to another company he owned for £6m**.
The arrangement completed in 2002.

He then sold the club in 2006 and **kept the stadium**. That is the fact the
rest of the club's modern history turns on. Oxford have leased their own
ground from their former owner ever since, and between February 2018 and
April 2019 **his company issued five winding-up petitions against them** over
late stadium dues, including a claim for £204,000 in rent and service charges.
A club spent two decades paying its former owner to use a ground he had built
partly with the proceeds of selling their previous one.

Erick Thohir and Anindya Bakrie took 51% in 2022; Thohir became sole owner in
November 2025. The supporters' trust, OxVox, had the Kassam Stadium listed as
an **Asset of Community Value**, and the owners appealed and lost — which
gives the trust notice of any sale and a window in which to bid.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The Manor Ground, in Headington, was home from 1925 to 2001 and was famous
for being eccentric rather than good: a seven-foot slope from corner to
corner, summer use for cricket, bowls and tennis, and a set of ad-hoc
expansions after 1949 that included a dressing room with 180 seats on top of
it. Its record crowd was 22,750, for an FA Cup tie in 1964. A hospital stands
on the site now.

The Kassam Stadium, opened in 2001, is best known for having three sides. The
design provided for a fourth stand at the west end, aiming at a capacity
around 16,000; **the fourth side was cut during construction as a cost saving
during the club's financial crisis**, and the plans were later scrapped. The
west end is a car park, separated from the pitch by a few metres of run-off
and a wall, and signposted — with more optimism than the last quarter-century
supports — as **"The Future West Stand"**.

The geography of the move is its own small story. The club left a leafy
Headington ground named after a manor house for a retail-park site on the edge
of the Blackbird Leys estate. Oxford United has always been the city's other
institution, and the move made that literal.

A new stadium at Kidlington received full planning permission in February
2026, after a Section 106 agreement worth at least £5.1m. A judicial review
brought by a local group was refused permission in July 2026, the judge
holding the grounds not reasonably arguable; a renewal notice was outstanding
at the time of writing. The club is targeting 2028/29.
