## Origins

**The name came first, and it belonged to a place that has since stopped
existing as a place.**

Port Vale Wharf was established on the Trent & Mersey Canal by 1832 and is
labelled on an Ordnance Survey map of that year; there was a Port Vale Street,
Port Vale Corn Mills and a Port Vale House, all in the Longport area now
absorbed into Middleport. Whatever the club's founders did in the 1870s, they
did not invent the name — they took it from the little valley of canal ports
they lived among, which is why the frequently repeated line that Port Vale is
the only English league club not named after a place is exactly backwards. It
is named after a place. The place is gone.

The founding date is genuinely unresolved and the club has committed
commercially to one side of it. **The official date is 1876**, from a meeting at
Port Vale House, and the club marked its 150th anniversary across 2025/26 and
put **1876 on the crest** in a 2013 redesign. **The club's own historian, Jeff
Kent, finds no evidence of that meeting** and dates the club to 1879, as an
offshoot of Porthill Victoria; his standard reference work is titled *The Port
Vale Record 1879–1993*. Every source agrees the contemporary evidence is
exceptionally scarce. Nobody claims a document settles it.

Vale moved to Burslem in 1884 and became **Burslem Port Vale**, and dropped the
prefix in 1907 — not as branding but as a consequence of insolvency. The
chairman declared the club insolvent on **14 June 1907**, it resigned from the
Football League and was liquidated, the name carried on in local football, and
the ground it eventually settled at was miles from Burslem.

**The Valiants** is not, as the badge might suggest, of a piece with 1876. It
was adopted during the **1919/20 season**, after a deliberate nickname campaign
by a club officer named Huntbach, on the reasoning that the club had already
lived up to it.

## Trajectory

**Port Vale entered the Football League because another club was expelled for
illegal payments, and were themselves expelled for illegal payments
forty-nine years later.**

Leeds City were thrown out on 13 October 1919, eight games into the season, for
illegal wartime payments — the only English club ever expelled mid-season, their
refusal to produce their books treated as an admission. Vale, next in the earlier
election vote, were elected to take over their fixtures.

In 1968 the League found Vale in breach of **six financial regulations**: wages
paid to amateurs, illegal signing-on bonuses, associate schoolboys played in
contravention of the rules, and illegal payments to three named first-teamers.
The club was fined £4,000 and **excluded from the Football League** at the end
of 1967/68. On appeal the expulsion was reduced to a requirement to seek
re-election, and on **8 June 1968 Vale were readmitted by 39 votes to 9**. The
two-stage process is the story: they were expelled, and then they were voted
back in by the same body that had expelled them.

The context makes it worse rather than better. **Stanley Matthews** had been
general manager since 1965, unpaid beyond expenses, on an explicit strategy of
developing and selling schoolboys to fix the finances. He stood down in May
1968, owed some £9,000 in salary and expenses, and stayed on with the youth
team.

The modern collapse is a more ordinary story with a public-money twist. Bill
Bell's chairmanship from 1987 paid for a modernised Vale Park after the Taylor
Report — including a roof bought second-hand from Chester City — and the club
went up as Second Division runners-up in 1993/94 on 88 points, a single point
behind Reading. The money dried up after 1999, and the club went into
administration in December 2002, out of it under the supporters' vehicle
**Valiant 2001** in March 2003, and into administration again in **March 2012**
after an HMRC winding-up petition.

That last deduction is worth being precise about, because the received version
overstates it. Vale finished 2011/12 twelfth on 59 points with ten deducted.
**On the 69 they earned they would have finished ninth — three short of the
play-off places.** The administration cost them a season's dignity, not a
promotion.

**Port Vale have never played in the top division of English football.**

## Ownership & Finance

Three things have owned this club in twenty years: its supporters, a man who
kept the ground, and a couple from Burslem who gave it back.

**Valiant 2001** won control from the administrators in March 2003, and Bill
Bratt chaired the club for eight years. It ended badly — relegation in 2007, a
rejected takeover bid, sustained protests, an acrimonious EGM, Bratt's
resignation in 2011, and administration the year after. Supporter ownership at
Vale did not end in a sale; it ended in insolvency and a ten-point deduction,
which is a corrective worth having on a site that generally treats fan
ownership as the better outcome.

Running underneath it is public money. Vale borrowed **£2.25m from Stoke-on-Trent
City Council in 2006** to clear debts, at around £19,000 a month; by 2012 the
council was a major creditor and **wrote off £1.5m of it**.

The purchase out of administration in November 2012, for £1.25m, was made by a
group led by Paul Wildes with **Norman Smurthwaite**, who became sole owner in
May 2013. **The club and Vale Park were placed in separate companies**, with the
ground held by Smurthwaite personally, and a clause providing that if the club
were ever sold without the ground, a **twenty-five-year lease at well below
market rent** would be triggered. That clause is the whole of the next six
years: it made the club nearly unsellable on its own, and the ground valuable
whatever happened to the football. There were three serious bidders in 2016 and
none completed; the owner said publicly he would put the club into
administration if no buyer emerged; supporters marched.

**Carol and Kevin Shanahan**, Burslem-based founders of a local software firm
and lifelong supporters, bought **the club and the ground together on 7 May
2019**, reportedly for more than £5m, at the third attempt — part cash, the rest
in instalments secured on Vale Park itself. Their stated first act was for **the
football club to purchase Vale Park in its entirety**, so that the ground's
income belonged to the club rather than to a separate owner. It took two steps,
and it undid the 2012 separation.

The current cost of that is visible in the accounts: losses of £3.4m, £3.8m and
**£6.1m** across 2022/23 to 2024/25 — the largest in League Two in the last of
those years — with **net liabilities of £17.2m**, up from £11.1m, underwritten by
owner loans. The club attributes it to lower solidarity payments in a lower
division and early cup exits against a deliberate decision to invest in the
squad.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Vale Park was designed to hold seventy thousand people and opened without a
roof.**

The club left the Old Recreation Ground in Hanley after a lease dispute with
the city council in 1944, bought a thirteen-and-a-half-acre site near Burslem
station, and planned what the local press called **"the Wembley of the North"** —
a stadium of 70,000 to 80,000. What defeated it was not ambition but permission
and materials: post-war building supplies for non-essential work were close to
unobtainable, the club ran short of money, and the sources record a lack of
co-operation from both the corporation and the Ministry of Works. About £50,000
was spent. **Vale Park opened on 24 August 1950 against Newport County, entirely
uncovered.** Its record attendance, 49,768 against Aston Villa in 1960, was set
in a ground built for far more.

The Lorne Street stand is the same story at one-tenth scale and fifty years
later. Opened in 1999 and intended to hold about five thousand, it was left with
seats in only part of the lower deck because the money ran out, the upper deck
finished as hospitality boxes. **The unfinished section was finally seated in
2020 — twenty-one years on**, under the current owners.

Which is the shape of the whole club: a name from a wharf that is no longer
there, a founding date its own historian disputes, and a ground that has spent
seventy-five years being most of what it was meant to be.
