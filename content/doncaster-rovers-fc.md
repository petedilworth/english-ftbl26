## Origins

Doncaster Rovers was founded in September 1879 by **Albert Jenkins, an
eighteen-year-old fitter at the Great Northern Railway works**, who put
together a side from his workmates as the game spread out from Sheffield. The
first match, a 4–4 draw against the Yorkshire Institution for the Deaf and
Dumb, was played that month, and the name was settled on the walk home.

Why "Rovers" is not recorded. The usual gloss — that the club had no fixed
ground and was therefore roving — appears in no primary or club-published
source. What the club itself asserts is the occasion of the naming, not the
reasoning, and this page will do the same.

## Trajectory

**In June 1995 the man who controlled Doncaster Rovers paid a former soldier
£10,000 to burn down the main stand of its ground.**

**Ken Richardson** arrived at the club in March 1993, when **Dinard Trading
Company**, an Isle of Man company whose ownership was opaque, took a
controlling stake and appointed him a consultant. This is the detail that makes
the story a governance story rather than a crime story: **Richardson was never
a registered director of Doncaster Rovers.** The press called him chairman and
the club called him a benefactor, and neither was his legal position. He had
also, in June 1984, been convicted at York Crown Court of conspiring to
substitute one horse for another to defraud bookmakers — the Flockton Grey
case — fined £20,000 with £25,000 costs, given a nine-month suspended sentence,
and warned off by the Jockey Club for twenty-five years, reported at the time
as unprecedented.

None of that disqualified him from running a football club, because **nothing
did**. The Football League's fit-and-proper-person test did not exist until
2004.

The fire was set in the Belle Vue main stand on the evening of **29 June 1995**,
causing about £100,000 of damage. It failed, and it was solved because **the
arsonist left his mobile phone at the scene**. Richardson was arrested some nine
months later, immediately after a night match, and on **5 March 1999**, after a
two-week trial at Sheffield Crown Court, was convicted of conspiracy to commit
arson and sentenced to **four years**. The judge said he had exercised great
influence at the club and had used money to set serious crime in train. The man
paid to start the fire, a former soldier, got twelve months; two others received
nine months suspended. The prosecution's case on motive was that Richardson
wanted to force the club out of Belle Vue and into a new stadium. Later
summaries describe it as insurance fraud; that is a characterisation, not a
court finding.

**And he was still running the club through all of it.** The four years between
the fire and the sentence covered the worst season any Football League club has
had.

In **1997/98 Doncaster Rovers won four of forty-six matches, lost thirty-four,
took 20 points and conceded 113 goals.** In this site's records that is **the
most goals any club has conceded in a fourth-tier season**, and they finished
**fifteen points behind the club above them** — Brighton on 35, with Doncaster
on 20. Both figures are the kind that need checking twice, and both hold.

The causes were not on the pitch. The manager was sacked in August; a youth
coach took two games and resigned citing Richardson's interference; his
successor lasted seven games; and **Richardson's own associate then appointed
himself manager**. Most of the players were part-time and training was made
voluntary. Supporters formed an independent association and staged a mock
funeral, with a coffin, at the last home game.

The recovery took five seasons in the Conference — sixteenth, twelfth, ninth,
fourth, third — and then a first. **2002/03 was the first season the Conference
had play-offs**, a second promotion place into the Football League having just
been created, and Doncaster won them, beating Dagenham & Redbridge 3–2 after
extra time on a golden goal at Stoke on 10 May 2003. **They are the first club
ever promoted into the Football League through the Conference play-offs.** They
won the Third Division title the following season, back to back.

They have never had a point deducted, in any season, and have never been
through an insolvency process — which for a club with this history is worth
saying out loud.

## Ownership & Finance

Richardson's vehicle sold the club in the summer of 1998, and where it went is
its own story: the buyer was **Westferry**, and the purchase later became
evidence in an Irish public inquiry, after a director's claim to be its sole
owner gave way to the fact that the money came from the businessman Denis
O'Brien, and a letter connecting a government minister to the Doncaster deal
became central to tribunal proceedings. A fourth-tier English football club
turning up in the Moriarty Tribunal is not a thing most clubs can claim.

**John Ryan** became chairman under Westferry and rebuilt the club over a
decade, before resigning in November 2013 after falling out with fellow major
shareholder **Terry Bramall** over its direction — writing off his loans to the
club on the way out. A 2014 takeover by Ryan and the Doncaster-born singer
Louis Tomlinson collapsed when the associated crowdfunding target was not met.
Bramall is the majority owner now, and the club sits inside the **Club
Doncaster** group, which also absorbed the local rugby league club in 2013 and
the Belles in 2019. The club reported a £3m loss for 2024, and won the League
Two title in 2024/25.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**The club never owned Belle Vue and does not own the ground that replaced it**,
which is the through-line of a hundred years of Doncaster's infrastructure.

Belle Vue opened on 26 August 1922 on land the Corporation had offered the
previous year, taken on a twenty-one-year lease at **£50 a year**. Capacity ran
from 7,000 to about 40,000 by 1938 and back down to 11,500 by the end. The last
match was on 23 December 2006.

Then the irony that no novelist would risk. **On 7 February 2007, after the club
had left, a gas explosion destroyed most of the main stand** — residents
described a bang like a bomb going off. The stand Ken Richardson had paid to
burn down, and failed to, was finally destroyed by accident, twelve years later,
with nobody's money at stake. Housing stands on the site now, with streets named
for club figures.

The replacement was built and paid for by **Doncaster Metropolitan Borough
Council** — about £20m within a £32m sports complex — and **there was no
sale-and-leaseback, because the club had nothing to sell.** It was a rent-paying
tenant from 2007, and took a **99-year operating lease only in June 2012**, at
an inflation-linked £100,000 a year, by which time the council had absorbed
roughly £4m of losses on the stadium, guaranteed a £1m overdraft, and made two
emergency payments inside six months. The public built the ground, carried its
losses for six years, and then handed the running of it to the football club.

The name has changed three times in under twenty years — Keepmoat, then
Eco-Power from December 2021, and from July 2026 **Club Doncaster Sports
Village**, the club deciding not to sell the name again.

One further piece of the site's history belongs here, because it is an off-pitch
decision of exactly the kind this site records. **Doncaster Rovers Belles** were
founded in 1969 by women selling lottery tickets on the Belle Vue terraces, won
the league in 1992 and 1994 and six Women's FA Cups, and shared the new stadium
from 2007. When the FA restructured the Women's Super League into two divisions
for 2014, **the Belles were the only incumbent club not awarded a top-division
licence** — a decision announced after they had played **one game** of the 2013
season, on facility and commercial grounds rather than results, with Manchester
City, who had never played in the top flight, taking the place. The comparison
that matters is the last completed season before the decision: in 2012 the
Belles finished **seventh of eight, above Liverpool, who were kept up**. Their
appeal was dismissed in a statement issued at lunchtime on a busy sporting
Saturday. They did finish bottom in 2013 — after they had already been told they
were going down.
