## Origins

The club that AFC Wimbledon exists in response to was founded in 1889 as
Wimbledon Old Central, and spent most of its first nine decades outside the
Football League. Elected in 1977, it reached the First Division within nine
years, won the FA Cup in 1988, and was a founder member of the Premier League
in 1992 — one of the most compressed rises in English football history.

The break with its home came first. The Taylor Report required top-flight
grounds to be all-seater, and the club concluded that converting Plough Lane
was not economically viable. It left in 1991 for what was presented as a
temporary groundshare at Crystal Palace's Selhurst Park. The groundshare
lasted twelve years. A club without a ground of its own turned out to be a
club that could be moved.

## Trajectory

In August 2001 the board announced its intention to relocate to Milton
Keynes, some sixty miles away, into a stadium proposed as part of a retail
development. The Football League refused permission. The appeal went to an FA
arbitration, and in May 2002 a three-man independent commission ruled two to
one that the move could proceed, against the stated positions of both the
League and the FA.

The commission also considered what supporters might do in response, and
concluded that fans starting a new club afresh "would not be in the wider
interests of football." AFC Wimbledon was founded within days, on 30 May 2002.
The phrase became its founding slogan.

Open trials were held on Wimbledon Common that June, attended by 230 hopefuls.
The club entered the Combined Counties League — nine tiers below where
Wimbledon FC had been a decade earlier — and climbed: Combined Counties
champions unbeaten in 2003–04, Isthmian Division One in 2004–05, Conference
South in 2008–09, and promotion to the Football League on 21 May 2011, beating
Luton Town on penalties. Nine years, five promotions, back into the League the
original club had been elected to in 1977.

The original entity, meanwhile, entered administration in June 2003, began
playing in Milton Keynes that September still under the Wimbledon name, and
was renamed Milton Keynes Dons in June 2004. In 2007, under a settlement with
supporters' organisations, MK Dons renounced any claim to Wimbledon FC's
history before 2004 and transferred the trophies and heritage items to the
London Borough of Merton.

## Ownership & Finance

AFC Wimbledon has been supporter-owned since inception. The Dons Trust, a
democratic members' society created by a fans' vote in February 2002, holds
the controlling stake in the club's operating company. For two decades its
constitution guaranteed the Trust at least 75% plus one vote — a deliberately
high floor, designed by people who had watched what happened when a club's
control passed out of its supporters' hands.

That guarantee has recently been revisited rather than abandoned. Both boards
argued that competing in the EFL at a new stadium under the existing structure
was not sustainable, and a members' vote concluded in April 2026 lowered the
constitutional floor to 50.01% and lifted the cap on individual minority
holdings. No share sale had completed at the time of writing, and the Trust's
actual holding remained above 75%. A separate protection applies to the things
that matter most: the Plough Lane freehold, any relocation, and the club's name
and status are "super-restricted actions" requiring a supermajority of members.

The club has never entered administration. Its only points deduction came in
2014, three points for fielding a player whose rolling monthly contract had
lapsed — an administrative slip rather than a financial failure.

## Infrastructure & Environment

AFC Wimbledon played at Kingsmeadow in Kingston upon Thames from 2002 to 2020,
sharing with and then buying the ground from Kingstonian. Selling it to Chelsea,
who took it on for their women's team, underwrote the return to Merton.

The new ground stands on the site of the former Wimbledon Greyhound Stadium, a
few hundred yards from the ground Wimbledon FC left in 1991. It opened on 3
November 2020 — behind closed doors, under COVID-19 restrictions, so the first
match at the stadium supporters had spent eighteen years working towards was
played in front of nobody. Capacity is 9,215, with planning permission for
expansion to as much as 20,000.

The old Plough Lane site, sold in 1998 and demolished in 2002, is now a housing
development whose blocks are named after Wimbledon players. Both things are
true at once: the original ground is flats, and there is a football club named
Wimbledon playing a few hundred yards away, owned by the people who go to it.
