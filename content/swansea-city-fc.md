## Origins

Swansea Town was formed in the summer of 1912, in a town that played rugby.
Earlier attempts at a football club there had not lasted, and the first
professional fixture the new club played was a Southern League match against
Cardiff City, drawn 1-1 in front of about 12,000 people. The derby and the
club are the same age.

Why a Welsh club plays in the English league system is a question with a
plain answer that gets lost behind the constitutional-sounding version. There
was no Welsh national league to join. When Swansea, Cardiff, Newport County
and Wrexham were founded, senior football in Wales was organised regionally
at best, and English competition — the Southern League for the south Wales
clubs, the Birmingham & District League for Wrexham — was the only viable
level to play at. Football League membership followed for Cardiff in 1920 and
Swansea in 1921. The League of Wales was not created until 1992, eighty years
after Swansea's foundation, and the clubs already established in England
stayed where they were.

The name changed with the city rather than the club: Swansea was granted city
status in 1969 and Swansea Town became Swansea City. The badge followed a
similar civic logic. The club's early emblem was simply the Swansea coat of
arms, formally adopted as the crest in the early 1960s and used on programmes
and stationery rather than shirts; a swan appeared on the shirt around the
time of the renaming, was redesigned in 1972, and was briefly replaced
altogether by a Welsh dragon in 1973 before returning. A version showing the
swan standing on the castle from the city arms was worn through the 1990s,
and a new crest arrived for 2021-22.

## Trajectory

The distinctive thing in Swansea's trajectory is not a relegation. It is that
winning a trophy in England is now the club's only route into Europe, and it
took a governing-body ruling to establish that.

Until 1995, Cardiff, Swansea, Wrexham, Newport and Merthyr Tydfil could enter
the Welsh Cup and qualify for the Cup Winners' Cup through it, and Swansea
used the route repeatedly: European campaigns in 1961-62, 1966-67, 1981-82,
1982-83, 1983-84, 1989-90 and 1991-92. The FAW then barred the English-based
clubs from the Welsh Cup, and from that point only clubs inside the Welsh
league system could represent Wales in Europe. In March 2012 UEFA's Executive
Committee rejected an FAW request to reopen a Welsh Cup route for the exiled
clubs and confirmed that Swansea and Cardiff could qualify only through the
English system — league position in the Premier League, or by winning the FA
Cup or the League Cup.

Which is exactly what happened a year later. Swansea won the League Cup on 24
February 2013, the club's first major trophy, and took the English Europa
League place that came with it: past Malmö 4-0 on aggregate and Petrolul
Ploiești 6-3, second in a group behind Valencia including a 3-0 win in
Valencia, and out to Napoli in the last thirty-two. It was the club's first
European football since 1991, and it arrived through an English competition
rather than a Welsh one. The question was reopened in January 2025, when
"Prosiect Cymru" proposed putting Cardiff, Swansea, Newport and Wrexham into
a reformatted Welsh League Cup with a Conference League place for the winner,
projected to raise around £3m a year for Welsh football. The English FA board
rejected it, citing concerns over competition integrity, fixture congestion,
player welfare and the standing of existing competitions.

Beneath that, the ordinary trajectory is one of the steepest in this
database in both directions. Swansea were in the fourth tier for eight of the
nine seasons between 1996/97 and 2004/05, and came within a point of dropping
out of the Football League altogether in 2002/03 — twenty-first with 49
points, one clear of the relegation places on the final day. Seven years later
they were in the Premier League, where they stayed for seven seasons from
2011/12 to 2017/18. They have been in the Championship every season since.

## Ownership & Finance

The 2001-02 crisis is the reason the supporters' trust exists, and the trust
is the reason the club does.

In the summer of 2001 the London-based owner Ninth Floor plc, which had lost
around £4.5m through the club, sold Swansea to Mike Lewis for £1, with some
£800,000 of loans still owed. The Swansea City Supporters' Trust was launched
on 27 August 2001 at the Patti Pavilion, more than 600 people attending, with
Supporters Direct there to advise on the structures by which supporters could
run a club if it came to that. In October Lewis passed the shares on, again
for £1, to Tony Petty, and the £801,000 owed to Ninth Floor went with them.
An attempt to dismiss seven players and two coaches was blocked by the PFA.
On 24 January 2002 a local consortium led by the former Swansea player and
director Mel Nurse removed Petty for £20,000, reportedly about a day before
the club would have ceased trading, and the consortium agreement was signed
on 6 February. The club then entered a company voluntary arrangement with
debts of £1.7m.

The Trust's terms in that agreement are the part worth reading closely. Every
other member of the consortium was a corporate body; the Trust was given
extra time to raise its £50,000, that investment carried a seat on the board,
and it was permitted to invest a further £50,000 for a second. Its
shareholding settled at 21.1% and the board seat was continuous, so that when
Swansea reached the Premier League the club had something no other top-flight
club had: about a fifth of the equity held by its supporters, with a director
in the room.

The 2016 sale is what tested it. A US consortium led by Jason Levien and
Steve Kaplan bought a controlling 68% in a deal valuing the club at around
£100m, ratified by the Premier League on 1 July 2016. The Trust did not sell,
and kept its 21.1%. It stated publicly that it had not been part of the
preliminary negotiations, and Levien has said talks began in 2015 while the
Trust says it was alerted in March 2016 — both positions are on the record
and neither has been adjudicated. What is documented is what the owners
themselves published: a written apology in the matchday programme in October
2016 for not informing the Trust's board representative of the decision to
appoint a new manager, saying that not informing him sooner "was an error on
our part and one for which we take full responsibility." Trust members
balloted on selling part of the shareholding in 2017 (927 voted, 684 in
favour), and in 2019 voted to pursue legal action over the nature of the 2016
negotiations. The dispute was settled in February 2022 without adjudication,
and the settlement is the durable fact: the Trust holds a permanently
protected, non-dilutable 5% and has indefinite representation on the club
board. Successive share issues have since cut its actual holding to somewhere
around 7.5%, and will keep cutting it — but not below the floor, and not out
of the boardroom.

Those share issues are how the losses get funded. Swansea were relegated from
the Premier League in 2018 and parachute payments ran for three seasons to
2020-21; when they stopped, revenue fell 29% in a single year, to £19.7m in
2021-22. The eleven months to 30 June 2024 — a short period, because the
year-end was moved to align with the EFL's reporting calendar — show turnover
of £21.5m against a wage bill of £27.3m. That is 127% of everything the club
earned going out in wages, and the ratio is honest arithmetic, because both
figures cover the same eleven months; it should not be compared like-for-like
with the twelve-month ratios elsewhere in this database. The pre-tax loss was
£15.2m, closed only in part by £10.5m of profit on player trading. The year
to 30 June 2025 was worse: turnover £22.3m and a pre-tax loss of £21.6m, a
club record.

The ownership group put £21m into the club by share issue during 2024-25, on
top of more than £20m injected when it bought Kaplan and Levien's 74.95% in
November 2024. The buyers were Andy Coleman, Brett Cravatt, Jason Cohen and
Nigel Morris, and minority investors have since been added with more
publicity than capital — Luka Modrić in April 2025, Snoop Dogg that July,
Martha Stewart in December. Each injection keeps the club solvent and dilutes
its supporters a little further, which is the trade the 2022 settlement was
written to bound.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The Vetch Field was rented from the Swansea Gaslight Company and named for
the fodder crop grown on the site, and Swansea played there for ninety-three
years. Its record attendance was 32,796 against Arsenal in February 1968; by
the end, after the rear of the North Bank was closed on safety grounds in
1990, it held around 11,500. The last match was on 11 May 2005, the stands
came down in February 2011, and the council has marked the site with a blue
plaque.

The move was a municipal project, not a club one, and the constraint was
capital rather than sentiment. Neither Swansea City nor the Ospreys could
fund a new ground, and none of the three grounds in use met modern standards.
The council promoted a shared stadium on the site of the council-owned Morfa
athletics ground, funded principally through public borrowing at a cost of
around £27m, with the wider scheme underwritten by a 355,000 sq ft retail
park on adjoining land. It opened on 10 July 2005 at around 20,500, the
largest purpose-built venue in the city, with the council keeping the
freehold and the ground let to a specially incorporated management company
on a fifty-year term — a structure deliberately designed so that no single
tenant controlled the venue.

That design was undone in stages. Talks stalled in 2015, and expansion plans
that had run through 2012 and 2013 for another 11,000 or 12,000 seats, taking
capacity to the low thirty-thousands, were shelved in December of that year.
Then in November 2017 the club agreed a new long-term lease with the council
and the Ospreys, completed in February 2018, under which Swansea City became
lead tenant with the Ospreys as sub-tenant, took sole control of the
management company, and — for the first time since the ground opened — paid
rent to the council, index-linked thereafter. The commercial consequence
arrived three years later: the Liberty Stadium became the Swansea.com Stadium
on 9 August 2021 under a ten-year, seven-figure naming deal the club could
only sign because it now controlled the venue.

The freehold is the last piece, and it moved in July 2026, when Swansea
Council's cabinet voted unanimously to sell the stadium to the club, subject
to detailed heads of terms. The price has not been disclosed and the council
has said it will not be; the leader stated it exceeded fair open-market
value, and the structure includes contingent payments on promotion and
retained ticket and hospitality benefits for the council. Until that
completes, the ground Swansea play in is still owned by the city, which is
how it was built in the first place.
