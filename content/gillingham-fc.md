## Origins

Gillingham was founded as **New Brompton FC** at a meeting in the Napier Arms
on 18 May 1893, and it was founded as a business: a share company capitalised
at **£1,500** by local businessmen, prompted by the success of a junior side
called Chatham Excelsior and wanting a club that could compete nationally
rather than locally. It bought a plot of land for **£600** the same year, and
that plot is Priestfield. The club has occupied one site since the day it
existed.

The name changed to Gillingham for 1912/13.

**It was not a dockyard team**, and the distinction is worth making because the
assumption is natural. Chatham Dockyard employed most of the local workforce
and much of it lay inside Gillingham's boundaries, and Brompton sits between
the old village of Gillingham and the docks. But the dockyard is where the
crowd worked, not who founded the club. No source records dockyard
sponsorship, dockyard founders or a works-team lineage.

The dockyard's ending is nonetheless part of the club's context. **It closed on
30 March 1984 after four hundred and fourteen years**, with more than seven
thousand redundancies and local unemployment reaching around nineteen per cent
— and Gillingham finished fourth, fifth and fifth in the Third Division in the
three seasons that followed. The club peaked as the town's economy collapsed.
That is an irony, not a cause, and this page leaves it at that.

## Trajectory

**Gillingham were voted out of the Football League in 1938 and voted back into
it in 1950, and neither had much to do with football.**

They had entered the League in 1920 by absorption rather than merit — the
Football League created the Third Division by taking in the entire Southern
League first division, and Gillingham had finished **bottom** of it and would
otherwise have been relegated. They then finished bottom of their first League
season too, and had to seek re-election **five times in seventeen years**. The
first four ballots they won. On **30 May 1938 they lost**, to Ipswich Town —
professional only since 1936, Southern League champions, and the first
non-League club to top an admission ballot since 1920.

Twelve years in the Southern League followed, with titles in 1946/47 and
1948/49, and then the League expanded. **In June 1950 both regional Third
Divisions went from twenty-two clubs to twenty-four**, taking the League from
88 to 92, and Gillingham and **Colchester United** — also in this batch — filled
the two new southern places. No incumbent lost anything. The same mechanism
that removed Gillingham restored them: a show of hands among rival clubs,
twelve years apart, with the club's own results doing no more than setting up
the question.

The rest of the trajectory is a long approach to the second tier and a short
stay in it. Fourth, fifth and fifth in the Third Division in 1985, 1986 and
1987; a play-off final lost over three matches in 1987; another play-off final
lost, to Manchester City, in 1999; and then **promotion in 2000**, beating
Wigan 3–2 after extra time at Wembley. **Five seasons in the second tier
followed, 2000/01 to 2004/05, and eleventh in 2002/03 is the club's highest
finish in its history.** They have never played in the top division.

**Gillingham have never had a point deducted.** The 1995 insolvency was
administrative receivership and predates the automatic penalty by nine years —
which is the interesting fact rather than an omission. The clearest off-pitch
cause of a recent relegation is a different sanction: **a transfer embargo
imposed in November 2021** under an EFL monitored loan agreement, after the club
took a £182,000 loan from the pandemic rescue package and then filed its
accounts late, restricting it to free agents and half-season loans. They went
down from League One at the end of that season.

**A claim this page will qualify rather than repeat.** Gillingham are routinely
called Kent's only Football League club. It was true from 1950 to 1989 and
again from 1992, but **Maidstone United were in the League from 1989 to 1992** —
playing their home games at Dartford, so that Kent had two League clubs and
only one League ground in the county town — and between 1938 and 1950 Kent had
no League club at all. Since 2024 **Bromley** have been in the League, which is
historically Kent and administratively London since 1965, so the claim now
turns on a definition rather than a fact. Charlton, often assumed into it, has
never been in administrative Kent: the parish left for metropolitan London in
1855, sixteen years before the club existed.

## Ownership & Finance

**Priestfield has been inside the football club, outside it, back inside it,
and outside it again.**

**Paul Scally** bought the club for **£1** in the summer of 1995, out of
receivership and shortly before liquidation, taking on debts reported between
£1.5m and £2m, and was chairman for twenty-seven years. The ground's journey
under and after him runs like this:

- **1893 to 2007**: owned by the club, bought as farmland at its founding.
- **2007–08**: **sold out of the club for about £9.8m** to Priestfield
  Developments Ltd, a company Scally solely owned, as part of a restructuring
  of the club's debts, and leased back at a nominal rent.
- **2011**: **bought back by the club for £1,050,000** — three years after
  leaving for nearly ten million. Scally called it something of a business
  masterstroke; supporters were reported as puzzled.
- **2022**: pledged as security to the incoming owner, a charge registered
  entitling him to the freehold in exchange for his investment.
- **2022–23**: **sold to GFC Holdings Ltd, the club's holding company, for
  £17.46m** — so it sits outside the operating company again.
- **Now**: Gillingham Football Club Ltd **pays rent to GFC Holdings Ltd**,
  £108,333 in the 2024 accounts.

Three valuations at three dates for three purposes, and this page will not do
arithmetic across them. What can be said is that the supporters' questions
about the 2007–2011 round trip are documented, and that an answer to them is
not.

**Brad and Shannon Galinson bought 70% for about £7m in December 2022**, with
the club bottom of League Two, Scally retaining 30%. He was **voted off the
board by shareholders in October 2024** after twenty-nine years, and in 2025 the
owners' vehicle **sued him in the High Court**, alleging matters concealed or
misrepresented during the sale, breach of assurances and diversion of
resources. **Scally denies it.** Those are pleaded allegations, untested, and
they are recorded here as such.

The finances since are what a committed owner in League Two looks like: turnover
of £8.2m against a **£4.3m loss** in 2023/24, and £7.5m against a **£5.7m loss**
in 2024/25, the latter including a **£7m loan from the chairman**. An earlier
figure needs its asterisk — the £600,000 profit reported for the takeover year
only exists after a tax credit of about £2.7m.

One earlier arrangement deserves recording because it is a piece of English
football's furniture. **Between 1997 and 1999 Brighton & Hove Albion played
their home games at Priestfield**, seventy miles from Brighton, after the
Goldstone Ground was sold to developers. Gillingham were the landlord for a
club that had lost its ground — which reads differently now that Gillingham's
own ground has been outside the club for most of the past two decades.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Priestfield holds 11,582 and has been in continuous use since 1893, which makes
it one of the longer single-site tenancies in English football — the club
bought the land before it played a match and has never moved.

What has changed is who holds it, and the present position is that the
**operating company rents it from the holding company**. The current owner has
committed publicly to Priestfield's long-term future rather than relocation,
and has been redeveloping around it: the former leisure centre next door
demolished for a fan zone of around nine hundred capacity, with planning
submitted to the council.

A club that has owned the same patch of ground for a hundred and thirty-two
years, and spent much of the last twenty renting it from itself, is a reasonable
emblem for how English football has arranged its property.
