## Origins

Rotherham United was formed in 1925 by the merger of the town's two clubs, and
which of them brought what to the marriage is the part usually got wrong.

**Rotherham County** brought the Football League place. Founded in 1877 as
Thornhill FC, renamed County in 1905 and installed at Millmoor in 1907, it had
been elected to the Second Division when the League expanded after the First
World War. **Rotherham Town**, founded in 1899, had applied at the same time
and been rejected, and spent its whole existence in the Midland League.

Two qualifications matter. The first is that County's League place was not
secure: relegated to Division Three (North) in 1923, they finished **bottom of
it in 1924/25** — twenty-second of twenty-two — and went into the merger
needing re-election. The second is that there was an *earlier* Rotherham Town,
founded 1878, which really was a Football League club, elected in 1893 and out
by 1896. It is a different club from the one that merged in 1925, and
conflating them produces the common line that Town brought League experience
to the deal. It brought a Midland League record and a ground at Clifton Lane.

What the record does not settle is what the merger did legally — whether
County's League membership simply continued under a new name, or whether the
new club was elected to a place County had vacated. Given that County had
finished bottom and were applying for re-election, those are materially
different stories, and this page leaves it open rather than picking.

## Trajectory

**Rotherham United have had forty points deducted, more than any other club in
this site's records** — and thirty-seven of them fell in three consecutive
seasons, which no other club comes close to. The next largest three-season
total in the whole database is nineteen.

The sequence, and what each one actually cost:

- **2006/07, ten points**, carried in from an administration the previous
  close season. Rotherham finished twenty-third on 38 and went down. **On the
  48 they earned they would have finished twenty-first and stayed up.** This
  is the one that did the damage.
- **2007/08, ten points**, for a second administration in two years. They
  finished ninth on 64. Restore it and they finish eighth on 74 — still four
  short of the play-off places. It cost them nothing but the appearance of a
  season.
- **2008/09, seventeen points**, imposed at the start of the season because the
  club had come out of administration **without a company voluntary
  arrangement**. Bournemouth took seventeen in the same division that same
  season for exactly the same reason. Rotherham finished fourteenth on 58 —
  and **on the 75 they earned they would have finished fifth and been in the
  play-offs**. This is the one that cost them something sporting.

Read together, that is a club playing at roughly promotion pace for two
seasons while carrying twenty-seven points of penalties, and finishing ninth
and fourteenth for it.

A fourth deduction, unrelated, came in **2014/15**: three points for fielding a
loanee against Brighton on 6 April 2015 whose youth loan had expired the day
before, plus a £30,000 fine. They stayed up anyway and chose not to appeal
once they were safe.

The club was relegated to the fourth tier at the end of 2025/26 — twenty-third
of twenty-four on 41 points — for the first time since 2013.

## Ownership & Finance

**Rotherham United has spent nearly all of the last twenty years as a tenant,
and the interesting thing is that escaping one landlord meant acquiring
another.**

The **Booth family**, of the Rotherham scrap-metal firm C F Booth, owned the
club for about seventeen years. In December 2004, with debts around £3m in the
wake of the ITV Digital collapse, they sold it **for a nominal £1 to a
supporters' consortium — and kept the property**. Millmoor itself, which sits
beside the family's Clarence Metal Works, the Tivoli nightclub in front of it,
and the Hooton Lodge training ground all stayed with the Booths. The club then
paid **£200,000 a year in rent** and carried the maintenance.

That is the transaction that produced everything else. A club with no assets,
a running deficit reported at around £140,000 a month, and a rent bill went
into administration in 2006, and again in 2008.

**Tony Stewart** arrived through the council. After the second administration
he was one of six businesspeople invited to a town hall meeting about the
club's future, and took over as chairman for 2008/09. He funded the **New York
Stadium at a cost of about £20m** from private money — and here is the part
that completes the circle. **The club does not own it.** The ground belongs to
**R U Estates Limited, a Stewart family company**, and Rotherham pay around £1m
a year to play in it. The structure that drove them out of Millmoor survived
the move intact; only the landlord changed, and this one is friendly.

In March 2026 Stewart transferred the majority shareholding in the family
holding company to his son, who now holds ultimate control, with Tony Stewart
remaining chairman.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**In May 2008 Rotherham United left the town of Rotherham and did not play a
home game in it for four years.**

Talks with the freeholder over the terms of a new arrangement at Millmoor
broke down — the club's account is that the demands were unmeetable, and it is
worth noting that no statement of the Booth position is on the public record.
The last match there was a 1–0 win over Barnet on **3 May 2008**, ending
101 years of occupation. Millmoor was not condemned and is not gone; it stood
until demolition plans were reported in 2026.

What followed is the clearest exile case in English football. Rotherham played
**four seasons at the Don Valley Stadium in Sheffield** — five miles away, in a
different city and a different metropolitan borough. Don Valley was a
**25,000-seat athletics stadium** built for the 1991 World Student Games, with
a running track between the crowd and the pitch and a single tier of seating
in a bowl. **The Football League made it conditional: return to the town within
four seasons or be expelled.**

The numbers say the rest. Rotherham's record football attendance at Don Valley
was **5,404**, for a League Cup tie in August 2008, in a ground holding 25,000.
By the final exile season the home league average was **3,498**.

They came back in 2012 to the **New York Stadium**, built on the site of the
**Guest and Chrimes** brass foundry, in the part of Rotherham that has been
called New York on maps for well over a century. The foundry is real and
listed: Peter and Edward Chrimes established it in 1843, John Guest joined in
1847, they moved to the site in 1857, and they made high-pressure taps, sluice
valves and fire hydrants. The story that New York City's hydrants were made in
Rotherham circulates everywhere and every source hedges it with "said to be" —
and at least one reading of the listing points the other way, to hydrants of a
*New York pattern* made in Yorkshire. The area's name and the foundry's trade
are documented. Which named which is not, and this page will not decide it.
