## Origins

Manchester United began in 1878 as Newton Heath LYR, formed by workers at
the Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway's Newton Heath depot, playing in the
railway company's own green and gold. The club severed its formal ties to
the railway around 1890, dropping "LYR" from its name, and by 1901-02 was
around £2,670 in debt and close to extinction, having already run a
fundraising bazaar to stay afloat. In March 1902, brewery owner John Henry
Davies led a group of local businessmen who cleared the debt and took
over; the new name, Manchester United, is credited to Italian-Manchester
businessman Louis Rocca after "Manchester Celtic" and "Manchester Central"
were both rejected. The popular account of how Davies came to invest —
involving club captain Harry Stafford's stray St. Bernard, Major, and a
chance meeting Davies's daughter is said to have had with the dog — is
widely repeated, including by the club itself, but its details vary
between tellings and no contemporary record of it has surfaced; treat it
as enduring club folklore layered onto a well-documented rescue rather
than part of the historical record itself.

"The Red Devils" is not the club's original identity. Through the 1950s
the side was known as "the Busby Babes," a name that became too painful to
use after the Munich air disaster of 1958. Manchester's rugby league club,
Salford, had been nicknamed "Les Diables Rouges" by French journalists
during a 1934 tour and carried the English translation ever since; Matt
Busby adopted "Red Devils" for his own side in the disaster's aftermath,
reportedly finding it more fitting than reviving a name tied to the players
who had died.

## Trajectory

On 6 February 1958, British European Airways Flight 609 crashed on its
third attempt to take off from Munich-Riem Airport, having stopped there
to refuel on the return leg from a European Cup match in Belgrade. Slush on
the runway prevented the aircraft from gaining enough speed to lift off — a
finding a 1969 British investigation established after German investigators
had initially, and for over a decade, blamed pilot error, a conclusion that
formally exonerated Captain James Thain, who had spent the intervening
years disputing it. Twenty-three people died: eight players, including
Duncan Edwards, who survived the crash but died of his injuries a fortnight
later; three club staff; eight journalists; and members of the flight crew.
Manager Matt Busby survived with severe injuries serious enough that he was
given the last rites twice in hospital.

What followed was a decade of institutional rebuilding rather than a quick
recovery. United fielded a makeshift side within days of the crash and
continued competing while Busby recovered and rebuilt the club around the
players who remained and those who followed. On 29 May 1968 — very nearly
ten years after Munich — Manchester United beat Benfica 4-1 after extra
time at Wembley to become the first English club to win the European Cup,
with crash survivors Bobby Charlton and Bill Foulkes both playing; Busby
was knighted that year. He later described the win as personally
"cleansing," given his own sense of responsibility for having pushed the
club into European competition against the Football League's wishes in the
first place.

## Ownership & Finance

The Glazer family completed a £790m takeover in 2005 financed as a
leveraged buyout: rather than primarily using their own capital, they
borrowed against the club itself, loading roughly £525m of debt onto it,
including high-interest payment-in-kind loans. United posted a net loss
the following year for the first time since 1931. Across the two decades
since, independent financial reporting puts net interest payments alone at
over £1bn, with more than £150m further paid out to the family in
dividends on top of refinancing and advisory fees — a structure that
produced, from around 2010, an organised fan protest movement built around
wearing green and gold, Newton Heath's original colours, adopted
specifically as a symbolic rejection of the Glazer-era red "brand" in favour
of the club's pre-corporate identity.

Sir Jim Ratcliffe's INEOS completed a purchase of a 25% stake, reported at
around £1.25bn, in February 2024, with further investment lifting his
effective holding toward 28%; control was explicitly divided, with INEOS
taking over football operations while the Glazer family retained majority
ownership and overall control of the club. The period since has been
marked by heavy cost-cutting: several rounds of redundancies through 2024
and 2025 totalling several hundred roles, cuts to staff catering, and
fan-facing changes including retying the senior citizens' ticket discount to
the state pension age and a third consecutive year of season-ticket price
rises, prompting organised supporter protests. As of the most recent
reporting, the Glazers remain majority owners and no further change of
control has been confirmed; suggestions that Ratcliffe might eventually
take full ownership remain analyst speculation rather than an agreed deal.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Old Trafford opened in February 1910 and was briefly the largest stadium
in England before the original Wembley was built. German bombing aimed at
the nearby Trafford Park industrial estate destroyed the ground's main
stand on the night of 11 March 1941, and Manchester United played home
matches at Manchester City's Maine Road for eight years while it was
rebuilt, reopening in 1949. The "Theatre of Dreams" nickname is credited to
Bobby Charlton, a Munich survivor, quoted by journalist John Riley in his
1987 book *Soccer*.

The ground's age has become a live issue: extensive reporting on a leaking
roof and general disrepair, including a visibly flooded away end during a
May 2024 match, has run alongside serious redevelopment plans since the
INEOS investment began. In March 2025 the club announced proposals for a
new 100,000-seat stadium designed by Foster + Partners, on a site near the
current ground as part of a wider Trafford Wharfside regeneration scheme,
at a reported cost of around £2bn. The project reached a planning-framework
milestone in July 2026 with a wider masterplan going before Trafford
Council, still short of full stadium planning permission; reported target
completion dates have shifted across successive rounds of coverage and
should be treated as provisional given the project's early stage.
