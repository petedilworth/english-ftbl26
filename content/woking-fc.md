## Origins

Woking Football Club spent most of its life saying it was founded in 1889 and
has since decided it was founded in **1887**, on the evidence of newspaper
reports and its own early yearbooks. The first match the record shows is a win
over Chertsey on **5 November 1887**. Most reference works still carry the old
date; the club changed its own.

The early history is a very long stay in one league. Woking joined the West
Surrey League in 1895/96 and won it by a point on the final day; reached the
**first round of the FA Cup in January 1908**, against Bolton Wanderers, after
five qualifying rounds; joined the **Isthmian League in 1911 and stayed in its
top division for seventy-two years**. The high point of the amateur era was the
**1957/58 FA Amateur Cup**, won 3–0 against Ilford at Wembley in front of
**71,000** — a crowd this club will not see again.

The nickname is a colour. **The Cards, or the Cardinals**, from the cardinal red
of the red-and-white halved shirts. A local tradition attaches it to Cardinal
Wolsey receiving news of his appointment while staying at Woking Palace in 1515;
that is a story the town tells, not a documented origin, and the shirts are
enough.

## Trajectory

**Woking's entire recorded history is one division.** Thirty-one seasons in this
site's data, all of them in the fifth tier, from 1992/93 onwards, plus four
seasons below it that the data cannot see. They are not alone in that —
Altrincham have more fifth-tier seasons than Woking does — but they are one of
a small group of clubs for whom the National League is not a stage but the whole
career.

And their two best seasons were spent behind champions who were not allowed to
go up.

The rule is the point. Through the mid-1990s the Football League admitted the
Conference champion — **one club, no play-offs until 2003** — and required the
champion's ground to meet a **capacity of 6,000 including at least 1,000 seats,
certified by 31 December during the season in question**, months before anyone
knew who would win it. Three seasons running, the champion failed it and
**nobody was promoted at all**:

| Season | Champions | Blocked | Woking |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1993/94 | Kidderminster Harriers | Aggborough failed the capacity test | 3rd |
| 1994/95 | Macclesfield Town (80 pts) | Moss Rose failed by 31 Dec 1994 | **2nd, 75 pts** |
| 1995/96 | Stevenage Borough (91 pts) | Broadhall Way failed | **2nd, 83 pts** |

This site's data corroborates it from the other end, and it is the cleanest
possible confirmation: **no club carries a relegation status in the fourth tier
in 1994, 1995 or 1996.** Northampton, Exeter City and Torquay United each
finished bottom of the Football League in those years and each stayed up,
because the trapdoor above them was bolted shut. Three seasons in which nothing
came up and nothing went down.

**The runner-up was never in the frame.** There was one promotion place and it
belonged to the champion; when the champion failed, the place simply went
unused. That was not universal in English football — the Southern League
promoted its runner-up in 1996/97 when its champions' ground failed — but the
Football League did not do it. Woking finished second twice and were never
denied anything, because there was never anything to deny them.

Two details are worth keeping. **Macclesfield asked the League for permission to
approach Chester about a temporary groundshare before the deadline and were
refused**, on the ground that Chester was too far outside their own conurbation:
the rule was about a qualifying ground in the right place, not simply a
qualifying ground. And **Stevenage sued.** The case was heard in the Chancery
Division and judgment given on **23 July 1996**; the challenge failed on delay,
the judge holding that Stevenage and every other Conference club had known the
criteria since May 1995 and had left it too late.

**Macclesfield won the Conference again in 1996/97 and went up**, Moss Rose
having been improved in the meantime; Kidderminster won it again in 1999/2000
and went up. Since 1997 every champion has been promoted, and the modern rule
reverses the old one: a promoted club has until **31 March of its first season
in the new division** to reach the grade. The work now follows promotion instead
of gating it.

Whether **Kingfield** would have passed is the question this page cannot answer
and should not pretend to. No League ruling, club statement or contemporary
report on Woking's ground in either year could be found. What the construction
record shows is that Kingfield had **no significant seated accommodation until
the Leslie Gosden Stand — around 2,000 seats — was built in 1995**, which makes
it hard to see how Woking could have met the seat requirement at the December
1994 cut-off that failed Macclesfield. That is an inference from dates, not a
finding. It is also the honest shape of the story: a club whose two best-ever
seasons were spent finishing behind champions who were blocked by a rule that
would probably have blocked Woking too.

The rest is quieter. Three **FA Trophies in four seasons — 1994, 1995 and
1997**. Relegated out of the fifth tier in 2009 after seventeen consecutive
seasons in it, up again as Conference South champions in 2012; relegated again
in 2017/18, and back through the National League South play-off final in May
2019. This site records **no promotions at all** for Woking, which is a coverage
artefact and not a fact: both of those promotions were won at the sixth tier,
below the bottom of the data. **Woking have never had a point deducted.**

## Ownership & Finance

Woking have twice been close to going under and have never actually gone.

**In February 2002**, with the club facing administration, a local businessman
and lifelong supporter became chairman and rescued it — and he also came to own
the ground, through a separate company. **In October 2020** an American-led
investment group arrived; one of its principals took majority control in 2021
and put in **over £3.4m** of his own money.

**In July 2024 he said publicly that the club was within weeks of
administration**, and that a **£600,000 loan from his parents** was what allowed
the season to start. That is an unusually direct statement of what benefactor
funding actually is at this level. In **December 2024 the shareholders voted
through a sale**, and **Cardinal Football Group LLC**, led by an American
businessman who is also part of a Major League Soccer ownership group, acquired
a majority — reported as **85 per cent of the voting rights** — with the previous
owner keeping a minority stake. The supporters' trust was briefed through the
process under confidentiality and published both statements.

No insolvency event is documented at any point: no administration, no CVA, no
winding-up order. Both 2002 and 2024 were near-misses, and they should be
described as what they were.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Kingfield is owned by Woking Borough Council**, which acquired the freehold
around 2018; the club operates it. The Leslie Gosden Stand that changed the
ground in the mid-1990s was itself substantially public money — a **£250,000
Football Trust grant** and a council **interest-free loan of around £1.7m** that
the club was still carrying years afterwards. It was the first of four planned
phases, and the later phases took the ground to about 6,000 by the end of the
decade, which is roughly where it still sits.

The redevelopment that was meant to replace it is a planning story rather than a
building one. In **April 2018 the council approved a tripartite arrangement**
with the club and a developer, and formal agreements were signed in **February
2019**: a **9,026-capacity stadium** on the Kingfield Road site, funded by
**1,048 homes** on the land around it. **The council refused planning permission
in June 2020.** The developer appealed, a two-week inquiry followed, the
inspector indicated that something closer to **93 homes** would be reasonable
rather than 1,048, and in **December 2021 the appeal failed.** Nothing was built.

The council's own conduct in getting there was formally criticised. An
independent investigation found that it had **bought land without a valuation**,
agreed a **£250m loan facility** to a developer whose value officers knew to be
nominal, kept **no minutes of more than fifty meetings** with that developer, and
had no risk framework for a project of that size; due diligence was described as
wholly inadequate.

**Woking Borough Council issued a section 114 notice on 7 June 2023**, against
roughly **£2.4bn of debt**, with government commissioners already in place. It
is tempting, and wrong, to join that to the football club. **No stadium work was
ever carried out.** The scheme was refused in 2020 and dead on appeal eighteen
months before the notice, and the council's debt is overwhelmingly a
town-centre regeneration one. The accurate and narrower statement is this: the
council bought the football ground's freehold, backed a developer badly, was
criticised for it, and then went bust for other reasons — after which it had no
capacity to revive the scheme even if it had wanted to.

So Kingfield stays as it is, and what the club can do to it is now measured in
shipping containers: planning permission in January 2025 for a seven-container
food court, and LED floodlights for 2026. A ground that once could not be
improved fast enough to matter now cannot be improved at all, and for a reason
that has nothing to do with football.
