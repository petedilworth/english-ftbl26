## Origins

Southend United was founded in a pub, in a village, to compete with the
amateurs.

The meeting was at the **Blue Boar Hotel** on Victoria Avenue in **Prittlewell**
— the old parish out of which the seaside resort grew, not the seafront — and
it was called in 1906 by the landlord, **Oliver Trigg**, who gathered
footballers and local businessmen to set up a **professional** club in a town
whose strongest side, Southend Athletic, was amateur. The name proposed at that
first meeting was **Prittlewell United**. It lasted about a month before being
dropped for something that covered the whole borough.

The colours were blue from the start, reportedly chosen to distinguish the new
club from the amateurs it was set up to outrank, and read locally as a
reference to the sea. Southend have worn blue in every season but one.

The nickname is borrowed from a neighbour. **"The Shrimpers"** comes from the
shrimping fleet at **Leigh-on-Sea**, which was a separate town until it was
absorbed into the borough — so the club is named for an industry that only
became its own once local-government boundaries moved. The shrimp still
occupies a quarter of the badge, alongside the sea, the Essex seaxes and a
football.

## Trajectory

Southend United have never played in the top flight. Seven seasons in the
second tier is the whole of it, six of them consecutively in the 1990s, and
the best finish in this site's record is **twelfth, in 1991/92**.

What the club is likely to be remembered for is the speed of the fall at the
end. **Southend are the only club in this data relegated in both 2019/20 and
2020/21** — checked against every club in it — and the only one that went from
the third tier in 2019/20 to the fifth by 2021/22.

Neither relegation was caused by a rule. The 2019/20 season was curtailed and
settled on points per game, but Southend had 19 points from 35 games and were
far enough adrift that the arithmetic made no difference. What is off-pitch is
the state the club was in while it happened: **the first three months of the
2020/21 campaign were spent under a transfer embargo over unpaid tax**, and on
leaving the Football League after 101 years the board disclosed debts of
**£17.4m**.

Then the deduction. In **August 2023 the National League docked Southend ten
points** for failing to clear an outstanding HMRC debt by a deadline it had set
in writing the previous month, taking the club to **minus four before a ball
was kicked**. It did not relegate them — they finished ninth. What it cost is
exactly calculable from this site's own table: **restore the ten points and
Southend finish on 75, level with sixth-placed Gateshead and ahead of them on
goal difference — inside the play-off places.** The sanction did not threaten
the club's survival. It removed its route out.

One earlier deduction sits in the record and should be described precisely. In
**June 2020 an EFL disciplinary commission imposed three points and a £7,500
fine** for paying players late in December 2019 and February 2020 and for
fielding an ineligible player against Lincoln City. **The three points were
suspended**, conditional on a further breach, and there is no report of the
suspension ever being activated. Southend do not belong on a list of clubs that
have served that deduction, and this site's data records it as unapplied.

For all of it, **Southend have never been in administration** — no CVA, no
winding-up order, no insolvency points penalty. That is the genuinely unusual
part. Nearly every comparable collapse on this site runs through an insolvency
procedure. Southend's ran through more than thirty winding-up petitions and out
the other side.

## Ownership & Finance

Everything on this page turns on a single month.

**In November 1998 Southend United and Roots Hall were bought for £4m** by a
joint venture between a property company and a listed property group. **In the
same month, the ground was transferred out of the football club into a separate
company, Roots Hall Limited.** From that point the club was a tenant of the
stadium its own supporters had built and given it, and — this is the part that
took twenty-five years to become obvious — **the ground was no longer part of
the football club that would eventually be sold.**

What followed is the longest running financial attrition on this site. A
specialist insolvency practice counted **more than thirty winding-up petitions
against the club from 2010 onwards**, its August 2023 write-up headed
"petition #31". The documented ones alone make the pattern: administration
avoided in November 2009 by paying £690,000 of HMRC arrears; two petitions in
2010, one from HMRC and one from a commercial lender; a transfer embargo in
February 2020 with £668,000 owed; £493,991 still outstanding that October;
a fresh petition in April 2023; another in February 2024, this one from a firm
of solicitors over unpaid bills. Not all of them were the taxman's.

The regulatory conflict is the under-reported half. Southend spent roughly
**fifteen months under a National League transfer embargo, from September 2022
to late December 2023**. In 2024 the League required the club to post a **£1m
bond in escrow**, citing a failure to evidence sufficient funds for the coming
season. **Southend took it to FA arbitration and won**: an FA appeal panel
ruled on 31 July 2024 that the League was not entitled to impose financial
conditions where there had been no change in circumstance, having earlier
granted interim relief restraining it from sanctioning the club. The written
reasons are published. The club was also named in Parliament during the
Football Governance Bill debates as an example of the cliff-edge a financial
resilience regime is meant to prevent, and the supporters' trust published its
own account of the ownership in November 2023, timed to a governance session at
a party conference.

The sale, when it came, was held up by the housing and not the football. Terms
were stated in July 2023 — **£1 for the shareholding and £4.5m for the ground**,
payable over three years. A consortium agreement followed on **3 October 2023**,
contracts were exchanged that December, and the takeover **completed on 19 July
2024**, ending roughly twenty-five years of the previous ownership. Turnover for
the year to July 2024 was **£2.52m against a pre-tax loss of £2.65m**; the
following year it was **£5.1m**.

**The club still does not own Roots Hall.** In March 2025 the city council
entered conditional contracts with a housing company for **805 homes at Fossetts
Farm** — a deal that triggers phased payments of up to **£12m to refurbish Roots
Hall** and, if planning permission follows, unlocks the transfer of the ground
to the new owners. The position is contingent, not settled.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Roots Hall is the benchmark this site already uses. Scunthorpe's Glanford Park
of 1988 is the first purpose-built new ground for a Football League club **since
Roots Hall in 1955** — a thirty-three-year gap. It is worth knowing what was on
the other end of it.

The site was the club's original ground from 1906 until the First World War,
when it was requisitioned for storage. It then became a **sand quarry and a
rubbish tip**, and the club spent nearly forty years elsewhere: at **the
Kursaal** from 1919, and at a **greyhound track on Grainger Road** from 1934.
The wasteland was bought back in **1952**.

What happened next is the fact that gives the 1998 transfer its weight. **Roots
Hall was built by a small group of supporters, at a cost of £73,997, funded by a
modest FA grant and money raised by the Supporters' Club — and then donated to
the football club.** It opened on **20 August 1955** against Norwich City in
front of 17,700, declared open by the secretary of the FA, and it was
unfinished: the stands were unconcreted, and the pitch drained so badly it had
to be relaid completely the following summer. Capacity eventually reached about
35,000, and the record is **31,090 against Liverpool in the FA Cup in January
1979**. The nominal figure now is 12,392, with parts of the ground closed and
being reopened section by section as work proceeds.

The replacement was approved and never built. **Fossetts Farm** was first
mooted in 1998 and a **22,000-seat stadium with retail** received planning
permission in 2007/08 — financed entirely on an adjacent retail development,
including a supermarket taking the Roots Hall site, which never happened. The
scheme was restructured onto housing in 2020, approved again in revised form in
2021 and 2022, cut to a **16,226-seat** design, and abandoned in 2023 when the
incoming consortium said the stadium was not viable. **Thirteen planning
applications over fifteen years produced no stadium.** The site is still
largely green belt, and the club's training ground sits on it.

So the two halves of the page meet. The ground the supporters built and gave
away is the one the club is still playing in and still does not own, and the
ground that was going to replace it exists only as approvals. Whether Roots
Hall comes back to the club now depends on houses being built somewhere else.
