## Origins

Exeter City traces itself to 1901 and the formation of St Sidwell's United, a
date the club formally adopted in 2016 after research into contemporary
newspaper evidence. In 1904, following a match in which St Sidwell's beat
local rivals Exeter United 3–1, the two clubs merged; the combined side took
the name Exeter City and continued at Exeter United's ground, St James Park.
Professional status followed in 1908, and the club joined the Football League
in 1920 when the Southern League's top division was absorbed as Division
Three. The "Grecians" nickname predates the club itself: residents of the
St Sidwell's parish, just outside the city walls, were being called
"Grecians" as far back as 1669, for reasons that are themselves disputed —
one popular but unverified legend ties it to a 1726 re-enactment of the
Siege of Troy staged nearby. St Sidwell's United carried the name into the
1904 merger, and it has stuck to Exeter City ever since.

## Trajectory

Exeter's modern history pivots on a single year. The club finished 23rd in
Division Three in 2002–03 and dropped out of the Football League by one
point, after a season of mounting financial trouble. Five years in the
Conference followed, ending with a play-off final win over Cambridge United
at Wembley in May 2008.

What makes Exeter worth studying is not the fall — plenty of clubs have had
one — but what the fall produced. The club that came back up was owned by its
supporters, and has stayed that way for more than two decades.

## Ownership & Finance

In May 2003, shortly after relegation, the club's chairman and vice-chairman
were arrested over allegations of financial irregularities; both later
pleaded guilty, in April 2007, to criminal offences relating to their time in
charge. Supporters' Trust figures were appointed to the board, and by
September 2003 the Trust had acquired a majority shareholding, inheriting
reported debts of around £4.5m.

Notably, the club did not enter administration. It agreed a company voluntary
arrangement in October 2003, approved by 88% of creditors — which meant no
points deduction, and creditors paid rather than written off. The debt was
then cleared largely by an accident of the cup draw: an FA Cup tie with
Manchester United in January 2005, a goalless draw at Old Trafford followed
by a replay at St James Park, generated close to £1m.

The Trust remains the majority shareholder more than twenty years on, and
continues to put money in directly rather than merely holding shares —
raising its annual contribution to £240,000 for 2026–27 after loans to cover
an overspend. Fan ownership here is not a symbolic arrangement; it is the
club's actual funding model, with all the constraints that implies. Exeter
cannot outspend a benefactor-backed rival, and does not try to.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The club does not own St James Park. The site was sold during the financial
difficulties of the mid-1990s, passed to a housebuilder, and was then bought
by Exeter City Council and leased back to the club, which later signed a
25-year lease. It is a reminder that fan ownership and asset ownership are
different things: the supporters own the club, the council owns the ground.

The ground has been redeveloped in stages — the Big Bank rebuilt in 2000, the
Cowshed terrace converted to seating in 2001 — to a capacity of 8,696, modest
even by fourth-tier standards and a real constraint on matchday income. The
club and Trust have agreed to a feasibility study into a possible future move,
tied to the council's Local Plan, which puts the question of where Exeter City
plays back where it has sat since the 1990s: partly in the club's hands, and
partly in the council's.
