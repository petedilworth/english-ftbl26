## Origins

Charlton Athletic was formed on 9 June 1905 by a group of teenagers, roughly
fifteen to seventeen years old, in East Street — now Eastmoor Street — in
south-east London. That makes the club unusual for its era. Most English
clubs of the period were spun out of a factory, a railway depot or a church
mission; Charlton was a street team that simply refused to fold. There is a
live historical dispute worth acknowledging: several published histories tie
the club's genesis to the East Street Mission, the Blundell Mission, or a
side called Charlton Reds, while the club's own museum states it was founded
as Charlton Athletic from the outset with no such connection. The nickname
"the Addicks" is traditionally traced to Arthur Bryan, an East Street
fishmonger who supplied haddock and chips to players after matches —
"haddock" softening into "addick" in south London dialect — though the tale
rests on club tradition rather than contemporary record, and competes with
theories deriving the name from "Addicts" or from "Athletic" itself.

The club moved to The Valley in 1919 — a ground dug out of a disused chalk
pit and built substantially by the supporters themselves. It is worth
holding that detail in mind, because seventy years later the supporters
would have to do something similar to get it back.

## Trajectory

The chalk pit made The Valley cheap to expand, and it grew to hold around
75,000, among the largest club grounds in England. What it did not make the
club was solvent. On 8 March 1984 Charlton reached a High Court winding-up
hearing and survived only when a rescue package from the Sunley Group was
approved with roughly twenty-five minutes remaining before the Football
League's deadline.

The deeper damage came the following year. In September 1985, with the club
in financial trouble and the ground facing safety requirements it could not
afford to meet, Charlton left The Valley altogether — and stayed away for
more than seven years. Everything that has happened since, including a
modern identity as a yo-yo club between the second and third tiers, sits
downstream of those seven years and the campaign that ended them.

## Ownership & Finance

Charlton has passed through a rapid sequence of owners in the modern era,
but the consequential fact is not who owns the club — it is who owns the
ground. When SE7 Partners, the UK arm of the US-based Global Football
Partners, completed its purchase from Thomas Sandgaard on 21 July 2023, the
deal **excluded The Valley and the Sparrows Lane training ground**. Both
remained with the club's former owner, Roland Duchâtelet.

This is the single most misreported fact about Charlton: the club plays at
The Valley as a tenant, not an owner. In late 2025 it announced it had
extended the leases on both sites to 2040, which eases its regulatory
exposure and removes the risk of suddenly needing a new home, but does not
resolve the underlying separation between club and ground. The current
ownership is a consortium of seven principal shareholders, with Gavin Carter
becoming chairman in December 2024.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The exile ran from September 1985 to December 1992. Charlton first shared
Selhurst Park with Crystal Palace — a groundshare between south London
rivals that supporters found genuinely difficult — and then, when Wimbledon
also moved into Selhurst Park, relocated again to West Ham's Upton Park for
1991–92 and part of 1992–93.

Greenwich Council's Labour administration had refused planning permission to
rebuild The Valley. So the supporters formed a political party. The Valley
Party contested the Greenwich borough elections of 3 May 1990, standing 60
candidates and pointedly declining to oppose the two councillors who had
backed the stadium plans. Backed by an award-winning poster campaign devised
by a supporter working in advertising, it took 14,838 votes — just under 11%
of the borough total — and won no seats at all under first-past-the-post.

Winning seats was never the point. The council's planning committee reversed
its position, and Charlton returned to The Valley on 5 December 1992, beating
Portsmouth 1–0. The ground was then rebuilt in stages — a single-tier east
stand in 1994, the two-tier west side in 1998, the north end and corner
infills in 2002 — reaching today's capacity of 27,111. It remains one of
very few instances anywhere in English football of supporters using the
ballot box, rather than the turnstile, to decide where their club plays.
