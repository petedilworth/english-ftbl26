## Origins

Crawley Town was founded in **1896**, disbanded in 1935, re-established in 1938,
and renamed **Crawley Town in 1958**. The dates matter because of what happened
in between.

**Crawley was designated a new town on 9 January 1947**, one of the first six
under the New Towns Act — and the designation was **challenged in court** and not
confirmed until that December, which delayed the whole development. The
population of the area that became Crawley went from **10,481 in 1951 to 53,768
in 1961**, more than half the total increase of the entire county.

So this is not a new-town club in the way Milton Keynes Dons are. **The club
predates the designation by fifty-one years.** It is an 1890s village side that a
new town was built around, and which took the word "Town" into its name only in
1958, once there was a town to be named for. This site's Stevenage page notes
that Stevenage, Crawley and Milton Keynes are the Football League's three
new-town clubs; Crawley is the one where the club came first.

## Trajectory

**Crawley Town carried a points deduction in four consecutive seasons — 2005/06
to 2008/09 — which is the longest unbroken run of sanctioned seasons of any club
in this site's records.** Rotherham and Reading managed three.

The four, in order: **three points** for exceeding the agreed playing budget
under the Conference's wage cap; **ten** for entering administration in June
2006; **six** for a further breach of the same financial regulations, with a
transfer embargo attached; and **four reduced to one on appeal** for fielding an
unregistered player. Twenty points applied, twenty-three imposed. They survived
relegation every time.

There were **two administrations, in 1999 and 2006**, not one — and the first has
an off-pitch cause worth stating, because it is attributed in part to the
running costs of the stadium the club had just moved into. The 2006 one came
with the club roughly £1.4m in debt and, by one account, about an hour from
being wound up before the administrators accepted a deal paying creditors around
half.

Then, within two years of the last of those sanctions, the club spent more
money than anyone at that level ever had.

**In the summer of 2010 Crawley spent something like £600,000**, including a
reported **£225,000 on a striker from York City — a non-league record fee** — and
six-figure sums on others, under a banner the club itself used: *Project
Promotion*. The title odds went from 33-1 to 5-1 in eight weeks. **Where the
money came from was never disclosed.** It was described as coming in part from
anonymous Far Eastern investors, with persistent reporting pointing to Thailand
and long-standing suspicion of people acting as shadow directors without
sitting the Owners' and Directors' Test, and it "was certainly never
satisfactorily explained by anyone at the club."

What it bought was **the Conference title in 2010/11 on 105 points**, fifteen
clear of AFC Wimbledon — the fifth-highest fifth-tier total in this site's
records — and, along the way, **a run to the FA Cup fifth round and a 1–0 defeat
at Old Trafford in front of 74,778**. Promotion again the following season took
them to League One in successive years. The manager who did it, **Steve Evans**,
had arrived in 2007 from Boston United — a club that took a ten-point
administration deduction in the same season Crawley took theirs — and had
himself received a suspended sentence for tax fraud months before.

From twenty points of penalties to a hundred and five points and Old Trafford,
in five years, on money nobody would identify.

Since: relegated from League One in 2015, **promoted through the play-offs in
2024** with an 8–1 aggregate semi-final against MK Dons and a 2–0 final against
Crewe, relegated again in 2025, and twenty-second in League Two in 2025/26 —
surviving by one place, two seasons after Wembley.

## Ownership & Finance

**In April 2022 Crawley Town was bought by a group whose plan was to sell ten
thousand NFTs and let the buyers vote on how the club was run.**

**WAGMI United** — the name stands for "We're All Gonna Make It" — was founded by
two Americans from the NFT world, with investors reported to include the
president of an NBA franchise. Neither founder had worked in football. They
bought the controlling shares from a Turkish businessman who had himself
acquired the club in 2016 for a reported **£1**.

What they actually did is more specific than the mockery it attracted, and
worth setting out precisely.

**The NFT sale happened**: 10,000 tokens at £355 in July 2022, raising a
reported £3.5m. **They conferred no ownership stake** — they were access tokens
with consultative voting attached, and no published framework governed how or
when a vote occurred. Decisions were taken ad hoc on a Discord server.

**Two votes actually took place.** In June 2022 around eight thousand token
holders and a thousand season-ticket holders voted on whether to offer a
trialist a contract; he was signed. In July they voted on which position to
recruit in, and 52.4% chose a midfielder. **Team selection was never on the
ballot**, and the promise that holders could vote on whether the owners stayed
was conditional on the club *not* being promoted by the end of their second
season — a condition that never triggered, because it was.

The first season was chaos: **five managers in 2022/23** and the club's worst
finish since entering the League. At one point the entire first-team squad was
transfer-listed, later explained to supporters as a trial of new software. In
January 2024 the supporters' alliance balloted its own members and **60% wanted
the owners to put the club up for sale**; the group reported receiving no reply.

And then it worked. **Promotion in May 2024.** Not, in the end, on NFT money —
Companies House filings show WAGMI made **interest-free loans of over £5.5m to
the club**, which is the same thing every other benefactor owner in this project
has done, arrived at by a more elaborate route.

**They sold in July 2025, not 2024.** The chief executive resigned saying new
investors had "a different vision of the future" and criticising the summer's
recruitment as having departed from the data-driven approach; eleven days later
a majority buyout completed. The club is now owned by **KB Sports and Leisure**,
held by the three Khalili brothers, one of whom had already been a minority
shareholder under WAGMI.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Crawley Town has never owned a ground, and the council sold the last one.**

The club played at **Town Mead** for forty-eight seasons. **In 1997 the council
sold Town Mead to developers and moved the club into the newly built,
council-owned Broadfield Stadium** — and the running costs of that ground are
part of what put the club into administration two years later. The move that
gave Crawley a modern stadium is also the move that cost it its first
insolvency.

Broadfield holds 5,567 and belongs to **Crawley Borough Council**, with the club
a tenant on a lease. No record of a freehold purchase exists. The current owner
has spoken publicly of movement on both the stadium and the training ground,
which is to say the tenancy is still an open question thirty years on.

The name has been sold three times and given back once — Checkatrade from 2013,
The People's Pension from 2018, and back to plain Broadfield Stadium for
2022/23. The ground is also home to Brighton & Hove Albion's women's team.

A club founded in a village in 1896, playing in a stadium its council built,
having been briefly owned by a cryptocurrency collective, is not a typical
English football story. But the shape of it is entirely typical: somebody else
owns the ground, and the money that made the good years arrive was never
properly explained.
