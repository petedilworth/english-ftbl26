## Origins

Portsmouth Football Club was constituted on 5 April 1898 at the High
Street office of the solicitor John Edward Pink, by a syndicate of six
local businessmen with £8,000 of capital. The money and the chairmanship
came from John Brickwood, who owned Brickwoods Brewery; Pink was his
solicitor. The ground came a year later — Fratton Park opened in August
1899, and the first football played there was a friendly against
Southampton that September, which makes the common line that Portsmouth
have played there since 1898 wrong by a year.

They did not begin in blue. The first kit was salmon pink with maroon
trim, reportedly the colours of the city's trams, and the club spent its
first season nicknamed the Shrimps. White arrived around 1909 and blue
around 1912. The star and crescent on the badge is lifted straight from
the arms of the City of Portsmouth, traditionally attributed to a grant by
Richard I in 1194.

The nickname is a genuine mystery, and the honest thing is to say so.
"Pompey" belongs to the city and the naval base rather than the football
club, and it was in use before 1898 — Portsmouth inherited it. Beyond
that, nothing is settled. The competing explanations include a slurred
contraction of Portsmouth Point, the abbreviation "Pom. P." in ships' logs,
the captured French warship *Pompee* that ended as a harbour hulk,
volunteer firemen drilling on Southsea Common, and sailors who climbed
Pompey's Pillar in Alexandria in 1781 and came home as "the Pompey boys."
The only peer-reviewed treatment, by the linguist Richard Coates in 2009,
works through them and concludes that not one comes near being convincing;
he specifically demolishes the derivation from Portuguese *bom bahia* by
way of Bombay. A club nickname whose origin has defeated a professional
onomastician is a better story than a tidy answer would be.

## Trajectory

Portsmouth won the FA Cup in 2008 and were in the fourth tier by 2013.
The mechanism was not a footballing collapse but a funding withdrawal
meeting a cost base that did not move.

The wage bill had reached £36.9m by 2006/07, a 49 per cent rise in a
single year, and in the Cup-winning season salaries ran at around 78 per
cent of turnover. Transfer spending across two seasons was roughly £68m
out against £29m in. When the owner's backing was withdrawn and the
principal lender called in a £30m loan in December 2008, none of that
adjusted. Tax arrears followed immediately: HMRC presented a winding-up
petition in October 2009, dismissed on payment, and a second in December
for £11.5m. The administrator's diagnosis was that Portsmouth had a
Premier League cost base and Championship income.

What followed is best counted precisely, because it is usually counted
wrong. There were **two** administrations — February 2010 and February
2012 — and **three** deductions totalling 29 points, because the second
administration produced a penalty for entering it and a further penalty
for leaving it. The 2013 sanction was an exit condition, not a third
insolvency. Only the first of the three actually changed anything: by
2012/13 Portsmouth would have gone down without it.

They also lost a European place to the regulations. Portsmouth reached the
2010 FA Cup final, which ordinarily carried a Europa League place, but the
Football Association and Premier League declined to put forward a late
application for a UEFA club licence, which required no overdue payables to
tax authorities or other clubs as at December 2009. The place went to the
seventh-placed league club instead.

## Ownership & Finance

The reason an ordinary insolvency became a three-year near-death
experience is that Fratton Park had been separated from the football
club. The ground sat under a fixed charge held by Portpin, the company of
the previous owner Balram Chainrai, while the club's business was in
administration. Neither was worth much without the other, and no buyer
could take one without the other.

That is what the Pompey Supporters' Trust had to solve. The settlement,
reached at the High Court on 10 April 2013 with the club facing
liquidation and the loss of its Football League membership, paid Portpin
£3m for the charge over the ground, £450,000 for other assets, £125,000
at the end of each of the next two seasons, and a further £2m contingent
on Portsmouth reaching the Premier League within ten years — a window
that has since closed unexercised.

The purchase was funded three ways: a community share issue at £1,000 a
share raising somewhere between £2m and £2.5m from more than two thousand
supporters, a parallel scheme of sixteen "Presidents" taking larger
stakes, and a £1.45m bridging loan from Portsmouth City Council approved
unanimously at a special council meeting. The Trust ended up with 48.5 per
cent and three of nine board seats — the largest single holding rather
than a majority, which is worth stating precisely, since Portsmouth's
billing as the largest fan-owned club in English football is a
characterisation that has been widely repeated rather than a measured
fact.

Michael Eisner's Tornante Company bought the club in August 2017 for
£5.67m plus a commitment of around £10m in equity. The vote needed 75 per
cent of the club's equity and got 81.4; among the community shareholders
turnout was 93.6 per cent and 80.3 per cent were in favour. Shareholders
were offered their full original £1,000 back, more than 2,300 applications
were processed by hand, and a number of supporters donated the money
straight back. The sale created a Heritage and Advisory Board with three
Trust seats holding a veto over the club's name, its colours, and any
relocation more than fifteen miles from the city centre.

The scale Portsmouth now operate at is the measure of the fall. Turnover
of £13.6m rising to £24.6m, against seven seasons in the top flight and an
FA Cup.

Two of English football's defining insolvency judgments came out of this
club. The High Court dismissed HMRC's challenge to the 2010 creditors'
arrangement in *HMRC v Portsmouth City Football Club Ltd*, and the case
that followed directly from it, *HMRC v The Football League*, upheld the
football creditors rule — the rule under which Portsmouth's players and
fellow clubs were paid in full while the tax authority took twenty pence
in the pound. The judge added that the League should not read the result
as an endorsement of the practice.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Fratton Park is hemmed in by terraced streets and carries two Archibald
Leitch stands, the South from 1925 with its mock-Tudor Frogmore Road
frontage and the North from 1935 — architecturally distinguished and
structurally limiting in equal measure. The Taylor Report's all-seater
requirement, met in 1996, cut capacity from a historic peak of around
50,000 to roughly 20,000.

The escape plan was ambitious and never happened. Herzog & de Meuron were
commissioned and produced designs for a 36,000-seat waterfront stadium,
first at The Hard in the city centre in 2007 and then at Horsea Island in
Portsmouth Harbour in 2008, within a wider development reported at around
£600m; the move from the first site followed Royal Navy concerns about
basing the new aircraft carriers nearby. The financial collapse overtook
it.

Tornante's approach has been the opposite — rebuilding the ground the club
already has, in phases that keep it open, beginning in June 2021 and
running through the North Stand, the South Stand and the Milton End, with
cumulative infrastructure spending reported at around £25m. The ground
came with the club in the 2017 sale, so for the first time in the modern
era Portsmouth's stadium and Portsmouth's football club are held together.
That, rather than any league position, is the structural change that
matters.
