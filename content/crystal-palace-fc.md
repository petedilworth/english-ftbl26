## Origins

Crystal Palace is the only English league club named after a building, and
the building came first. Joseph Paxton's iron-and-glass hall from the Great
Exhibition of 1851 was moved to Sydenham Hill, where the Crystal Palace
Company ran it and some two hundred acres of grounds. In 1894 the
lower-terrace fountains were filled in to make a sports arena, and it
staged twenty FA Cup finals between 1895 and 1914. The professional
football club was formed in 1905 by a company set up around the Palace's
general manager, to play at the ground the business already had.

When the club was founded is genuinely disputed, and the dispute is worth
laying out rather than resolving. There was an earlier amateur Crystal
Palace club, formed in 1861, which was a founder member of the Football
Association in 1863, entered the first FA Cup in 1871-72, reached the
semi-final, and stopped playing around 1875. In 2020, following five years
of research by the author Peter Manning, the club announced that it is the
world's oldest professional football club, founded 1861; in 2022 it
replaced 1905 with 1861 on the crest. The continuity argument is corporate
— the same Crystal Palace Company that founded the amateur side took a
controlling interest in the 1905 professional one.

The Football Association and the National Football Museum reviewed the
claim together and did not accept it, concluding there were two separate
clubs of the same name. An FA spokesman put it in 2022 as a broad
consensus among historians that no clear, substantial and continuous link
had been shown. The most detailed public rebuttal sits on a Palace
supporters' site, which is the tell that this is not a case of rivals
sniping: the argument runs through the club's own following. Roughly thirty
years separate the amateur club's disappearance from the professional
club's formation, and the documentary bridge is thin.

The nickname is the other half of a story told on Brighton's page here.
Palace were the Crystals within months of forming in 1905 and the Glaziers
soon after, both from the glass of the Palace. Malcolm Allison arrived as
manager in March 1973, and over that close season rebranded the club: the
Glaziers became the Eagles, after Benfica's *as águias*, and claret and
blue became red and blue stripes, reportedly after Barcelona — though the
club's own account hedges the second attribution and so should anyone
repeating it. A new eagle badge followed and lasted until 1987. Palace had
been relegated that season; the rebrand was an attempt to borrow the aura
of Europe's biggest clubs, and its most durable consequence was to hand
Brighton's supporters something to mock.

## Trajectory

Palace's league record is a yo-yo pattern between the top two divisions —
sixteen seasons in the first tier, four promotions and three relegations,
and never lower than the second. What nearly ended the club had nothing to
do with any of that.

The near-death came in 2010, and its cause was structural. Crystal Palace
FC (2000) Ltd went into administration on 26 January, placed there by a
lender holding a charge over it, and took a ten-point deduction that
dropped the team into a relegation fight it survived on the final day.
But the club was only half the problem: Selhurst Park had been in
separate ownership since 1998, and by 2009 the property vehicle holding
it had itself collapsed, leaving the freehold in the hands of a bank's
administrators. Any rescue had to buy a football club from one
administration and a stadium from another, and neither asset was worth
much without the other. On 31 May 2010 the club announced that without a
deal within twenty-four hours it would be liquidated. Supporters protested
outside the bank's London headquarters as well as at the ground.

## Ownership & Finance

The separation of club and ground is the spine of Palace's institutional
history, and it lasted twelve years.

Ron Noades sold Crystal Palace to Mark Goldberg in 1998 for a reported
£22m but excluded Selhurst Park from the sale, retaining the freehold
through his company Altonwood and granting the club a ten-year lease. The
club entered administration within a year when Goldberg's funding failed,
and Simon Jordan bought it out of administration in 2000 for around £10m —
still as a tenant, still paying rent to the former chairman. Altonwood
sold the freehold in 2006 for £12m, but not to the club: it went to a
joint venture between a bank and a property group, which granted Palace a
twenty-five-year lease at £1.2m a year in 2008 and then went into
administration itself in 2009.

It ended in June 2010, when four Palace supporters — Steve Parish, Stephen
Browett, Martin Long and Jeremy Hosking, a quarter each, reportedly agreed
on a handshake — bought the club out of administration and the Selhurst
Park freehold from the bank for £3.5m. CPFC Selhurst Park Limited was
incorporated that August, and the club has owned its ground since. Josh
Harris and David Blitzer invested in 2015; John Textor took a stake in
2021 and sold it in 2025 to Woody Johnson, the Premier League approving
the transfer of 42.92 per cent. Parish has been chairman throughout and
retains a little over ten per cent.

That reunification is what makes the current stadium project possible.
Croydon Council approved a Main Stand redevelopment in 2018, the
permission lapsed during the pandemic, a revised scheme was approved in
August 2024, and a £125m loan led by an investment bank was arranged in
December 2025 to fund it — borrowing secured against a stadium the club
actually owns. Works began in January 2026.

Palace also produced one of the cleaner recent illustrations of what
multi-club ownership costs. The club won the FA Cup in May 2025, its first
major trophy, and qualified for the Europa League. UEFA's Club Financial
Control Body found that Textor's simultaneous interests in Palace and
Olympique Lyonnais breached its multi-club ownership rules as they stood
at the assessment date of 1 March 2025 — a blind trust arrangement came
after it — and demoted Palace to the Conference League, Lyon keeping the
Europa League place on the strength of a higher domestic finish. The Court
of Arbitration for Sport dismissed Palace's appeal in August 2025. A
trophy won on the pitch, and a European place lost to a filing deadline.

Financially the club runs on player trading. Revenue reached £189.2m in
2023/24 with a £33.0m pre-tax loss, then £196.6m in 2024/25 with a pre-tax
profit of £8.3m — one of only twelve clubs out of seventy-six in this
database's accounts to make a profit that year, and achieved largely
through around £66m of profit on player sales offsetting an operating
loss.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Selhurst Park was built on a former brickfield bought from a railway
company in 1922, designed by Archibald Leitch, and formally opened on 30
August 1924. Its capacity today is around 25,500, low for a Premier League
club, and the constraint is the usual south London one: a ground wedged
into residential streets with a railway line alongside.

The Main Stand being replaced dates from that 1924 opening. The approved
scheme takes capacity past 34,000 and adds a club shop, museum and café,
and it has been a decade in the making — approved in 2018, lapsed, revised,
approved again in 2024, financed in 2025, started in 2026. The delay is
attributable to the pandemic, construction cost inflation and changed
London planning requirements rather than to anything specific to the club.
It is a slow project, but it is one the club can undertake at all only
because of what four supporters bought from a bank in 2010.
