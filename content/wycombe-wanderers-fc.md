## Origins

Wycombe Wanderers was formed in 1887 at a meeting in the **Steam Engine** pub
on Station Road by young men who worked in High Wycombe's furniture trade — the
town having been the centre of English chair-making since the sixteenth
century. It matters that this was a pub-meeting club of men who happened to
work in the trade, and **not a works team**: no factory founded it, in the way
that Thames Ironworks founded West Ham.

The name was settled at that meeting. "North Town Wanderers" was proposed;
another founder countered with **Wycombe Wanderers**, on the grounds that some
players lived in other parts of the town, and that carried. "Wanderers" was
most likely borrowed from Wanderers FC, winners of the first FA Cup, who had
played in High Wycombe in December 1877.

The shirts are the club's most distinctive feature and the one most often
described wrongly. The **colours** date from 1888 — Oxford blue and Cambridge
blue, taken from the universities' rowing colours by a club of local working
men with no connection to either. The **quarters** do not. The first jerseys
were halved; stripes came in early in the twentieth century; quarters from
around 1930; and then the quarters were abandoned in the late 1960s for plain
shirts, with one season in stripes after floodlights went in. That was
unpopular enough to be reversed, but the design kept moving until 1990.
Wycombe have worn quarters continuously only since then. The crest is the
chained swan of the Buckinghamshire arms, first evidenced on the shirt at the
1931 Amateur Cup final.

Whether the club has been "the Chairboys" continuously is genuinely unsettled.
One account has the name in use from early on; another has it lapsing for a
long period and being revived in the 1980s by a fanzine that found it in an old
history book. The second appears in several fan sources and no primary one, and
this page will not assert continuity either way.

The club stayed amateur for a very long time and stopped being amateur because
of an administrative decision, not an ambition: **the FA announced it would no
longer recognise amateur football, abolishing the distinction, and Wycombe
turned semi-professional in 1974**. Before that came the Isthmian League —
sixty-four seasons of it, eight titles — and the **FA Amateur Cup in 1931**,
won 1–0 against Hayes in front of 32,000 at Highbury.

## Trajectory

Wycombe reached the Football League in 1993 under Martin O'Neill, as Conference
champions and FA Trophy winners in the same season, and went up again through
the play-offs a year later. The near miss the season before is the one worth
recording: **in 1991/92 the Conference had a single promotion place, and Wycombe
and Colchester United finished level on 94 points**, Colchester going up on goal
difference. (This site's own table for that season disagrees with the published
record and is flagged as not final; the documented outcome is Colchester's.)

The contrast that gives that period its shape is what happened to the champions
either side. **Kidderminster Harriers won the Conference in 1994 and were
refused promotion** for failing the Football League's ground requirements, as
Macclesfield were in 1995 and Stevenage in 1996. Adams Park, opened in 1990,
cleared the bar. Wycombe arrived through the door in the three-year window when
it was open for them and shut for everyone else.

**The 2020 promotion was made by a rule, and the counterfactual runs against
the club, not for it.**

The season was suspended on 13 March 2020 with clubs having played 34, 35 or 36
matches, and in June the League One clubs voted to curtail it and settle the
table on **unweighted points per game**, keeping promotion, relegation and the
normal four-team play-offs. Wycombe had 59 points from 34 games. On raw points
that put them **eighth — outside the play-off places altogether**. On points per
game it put them **third**, at 1.735 against Peterborough's 1.686 from a game
more, and into the play-offs. They then won them, beating Fleetwood over two
legs and **Oxford United 2–1 at Wembley on 13 July**, and reached the second
tier for the first time in the club's history.

So the rule did not promote Wycombe — they won the play-offs on the pitch — but
without it they would not have been in them. Four clubs voted to play on, and
one of them, Tranmere, was relegated on the same arithmetic and considered legal
action, having argued against unweighted points per game before the vote was
taken.

The single Championship season ended in relegation on 43 points, **one point
behind Derby County** — and that gap became an off-pitch case of its own.
Derby's amortisation policy was subsequently ruled impermissible, Wycombe's
owner accused the club of systematic cheating, and Wycombe pursued a
compensation claim alongside a parallel one from Middlesbrough, with the EFL
stating both were football debts and pressing for mediation. Wycombe have never
had a point deducted; their grievance is that in the season that mattered,
neither did anyone else.

## Ownership & Finance

**Wycombe Wanderers was owned by its supporters for eight years, and the thing
they kept when they sold it was the ground.**

The route in was a promise. **Steve Hayes** took a quarter of the club for
£250,000 in 2004 and full ownership in 2009, writing off £3m of debt owed to him
in exchange for shares — and in the same year gave the supporters' trust an
undertaking that **if he sold, the trust would have first refusal**. He also
owned London Wasps from 2009, and promoted a 20,000-seat stadium for them at
Wycombe Air Park; when the scheme was shelved after a change of council
leadership he looked for buyers for both clubs. The trust's undertaking was
exercised, and **on 29 June 2012 Wycombe Wanderers Trust acquired the football
club.**

The structure it built is the reason any of this survived. The club became a
wholly owned subsidiary of the trust — but **Adams Park, the training ground and
the club's memorabilia went into a separate trust subsidiary, Frank Adams Legacy
Ltd**. Operating club in one vehicle, property in another.

When the club was sold in **February 2020** to **Feliciana EFL Ltd**, Rob
Couhig's company — 75% of the club for £2.2m that settled the debts, approved by
95% of the legacy members who voted — the property did not move. All charges
against Adams Park were lifted and the club took a fifteen-year lease from the
trust at £150,000 a year. In **2023** the trust was diluted to 10% of the votes
in exchange for the owner taking on stadium maintenance, working capital, all
operating losses and named capital projects, with the trust released from future
cash calls — and **the lease extended to fifty years, the debt-free freehold
staying with the trust**. In **May 2024** Couhig sold not the club's shares but
**the company that owns them**, to a vehicle controlled by **Mikheil Lomtadze**;
the trust records that none of the agreed terms changed. A director bought 10%
in June 2026.

So the current position, stated precisely: **Wycombe Wanderers is not a
fan-owned club and has not been since February 2020.** The supporters' trust
holds 10% of the votes and two seats on the club board. What the supporters do
still own, outright and debt-free, is **the stadium and the training ground**,
leased to the club for fifty years. The ground is more fan-owned than the club
is, and that was designed rather than lucky.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Adams Park is named for a man who bought a football ground and gave it away.
**Frank Adams**, a former player and captain, purchased the freehold of
**Loakes Park** from Lord Carrington and presented the deeds to the club in
1947.

The club left Loakes Park because of a hospital. The site had been earmarked
since the 1960s for the expansion of the neighbouring Wycombe Hospital, and the
club had been looking to move ever since. **The sale to the health authority
almost entirely funded the new ground** at Hillbottom Road, about two miles
west of the town centre, which cost around £3.5m and opened for 1990/91 holding
6,000. It was named for Adams.

From 2002 the ground was shared with **London Wasps**, on a condition imposed by
the district council that the two clubs produce an agreed **"green transport
plan"** to cut car journeys to matches — a planning condition as the price of a
groundshare, which is an unusually specific piece of municipal leverage. Wycombe
laid a hybrid pitch to take both codes. Wasps left for Coventry in 2014, and it
is worth noting that for three of those years, 2009 to 2012, the arrangement was
not landlord and tenant at all: **Steve Hayes owned both clubs**.

One recent near-miss belongs on the record because it connects two pages on this
site. In March 2024 Wycombe entered exclusive talks to buy **Bearwood Park**,
Reading's training ground, from a club in financial crisis that had said it
needed to sell the site. Reading supporters protested. Within weeks Wycombe put
the purchase on hold, due diligence having found planning restrictions that
permitted only Reading to use it. A club that owes its own survival to keeping
property out of an operating company was nearly the buyer of another club's, and
was stopped by the planning file.
