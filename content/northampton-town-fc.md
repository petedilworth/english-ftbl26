## Origins

Northampton Town was founded in March 1897 by **schoolteachers** — members of
the Northampton & District Elementary Schools' Association, together with a
local solicitor, at the Princess Royal Inn on Wellingborough Road. It is worth
saying that plainly, because the nickname invites the opposite assumption.

**The Cobblers were never a works team.** Northampton had made shoes since the
thirteenth century and by the 1830s the trade employed something like a third
of the town's men, so the nickname is the town's, in the same way that
Reading's Biscuitmen and Stoke's Potters are their towns'. No shoe factory
founded this club or employed its players as an arrangement.

The name itself was settled by arbitration. The founders intended to be
"Northampton Football Club"; **the town's rugby club objected**, the dispute
went to the Football Association, and the FA ruled that the new club must be
Northampton **Town**. A club named by a governing body in a dispute with
another sport is a fair opening for a page whose later chapters are mostly
about institutions.

Two seasons in the Northants League, two in the Midland League, the Southern
League from 1901, and election to the Football League in 1920.

## Trajectory

**Northampton Town were in the Fourth Division in 1960/61 and the First
Division in 1965/66, and back in the Fourth Division by 1969/70.**

The climb was three promotions, not four — there were only three divisions to
climb — and it went: third in the Fourth Division in 1960/61; Third Division
champions in 1962/63 with 62 points and 109 goals; runners-up in the Second
Division in 1964/65, behind Newcastle. Then one season in the First Division,
finishing twenty-first of twenty-two on 33 points, two behind Fulham. Then
down again in 1966/67, a season's stay in the Third, and down again in 1968/69.

The claim usually attached to this is that no other club has done it. **That
does not survive checking against our own data, and the club it does not
survive is Swansea City** — fourth tier in 1977/78, sixth in the First Division
in 1981/82, fourth tier again in 1986/87. Both round trips take **nine
seasons**, and they are the two fastest in the sixty-eight this site records.
The next quickest is Blackpool at sixteen. So the accurate claim is that
Northampton share the record rather than hold it, which is still a remarkable
thing to share.

The documented distinction that *is* Northampton's alone is narrower and
bleaker: they were the first club since Bradford Park Avenue in 1921 and 1922
to be **relegated in successive seasons from the first tier to the third**.

The cause was infrastructure, and it is visible in the next section. A club
reached the First Division with a three-sided ground, five players in the squad
who had played at that level, and no money to sign any more.

There is no points deduction anywhere in the club's record, including for the
1992 administration — the Football League's automatic insolvency penalty was
not introduced until 2004. The club won the Fourth Division in 1986/87 and
2015/16, both times on 99 points.

## Ownership & Finance

**Northampton Borough Council lent this football club more than £10m of public
money, almost none of it was spent on what it was for, and more than a decade
later nobody has been convicted of anything.**

The 2013 agreement gave the club a long leasehold at Sixfields and a loan of
**up to £12m**, drawn from the Public Works Loan Board, to rebuild the East
Stand and develop the land around it — a hotel, a gym, three hundred homes,
capacity up by about two thousand. The council's chief executive subsequently
authorised **£13.5m** in total; a later independent report found the increase
above the approved £12m was never reported to cabinet and that some officer
decisions went beyond delegated authority and were therefore unlawful. **£10.25m
remains outstanding**, of which £7.25m went to a development company now in
liquidation.

Building started in the summer of 2014 and **stopped on 9 October 2014**, with
contractors claiming £2.9m unpaid. Loan repayments stopped at the same moment.
Nothing in the scheme was finished. The East Stand stood as a shell.

The police investigation — **Operation Tuckhill**, not the Operation Tarlac that
circulates and belongs to an unrelated NHS fraud case — interviewed more than
550 witnesses and suspects and passed evidential packages on thirty individuals
to the CPS. **Charges were brought in January 2025** against five men, including
the club's former chairman: conspiracy to commit fraud by false representation,
with further counts of fraud by abuse of position and furnishing false
documents. **All five pleaded not guilty. The trial is listed for August 2028**,
estimated at twenty weeks. Nothing has been proved against anyone, and this
page says nothing more about it than that.

What the council has recovered is about **£460,000** — chiefly from the sale of
a house in Eastbourne after a court ordered its occupant to repay £418,709 —
against £2.3m spent on tracing the money and nearly £1m of police costs. The
authority that made the loan no longer exists; it was abolished in 2021 and its
successor inherited both the debt and the chase.

The other half of the ownership story is older and better. **Northampton Town
Supporters' Trust was the first in England**, formed at a public meeting at the
Exeter Rooms on **2 January 1992** attended by more than six hundred people,
called over a series of misleading statements by the then chairman and
establishing debts of £1.6m. **The club entered administration three months
later, on 2 April.** The order matters: because the supporters had organised
*first*, they were in position when the collapse came, and took **two seats on
the board** — a place guaranteed by agreement for twenty-three years, plus a
holding of around eight per cent.

That guarantee expired into the **2013 agreement, which did not carry it
forward**. Fan representation written into a council arrangement in 1992 was
written out of a council arrangement in 2013, immediately before the money went
missing. It is difficult to construct a cleaner argument for supporter
representation than the sequence this club provides by accident.

In October 2015 the club missed the repayment deadline, HMRC petitioned to wind
it up over £166,000, staff and players went unpaid, **the PFA paid the players'
wages**, and the council lodged an administration petition. **Kelvin Thomas
bought the club for £1** on 26 November 2015, cleared the tax bill and the
wages, and put in £1m. The council loan was not part of it and remains
outstanding.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Northampton Town played First Division football at a three-sided ground.**

The **County Ground** was Northamptonshire County Cricket Club's, bought as a
ploughed field in 1885 for £2,000 and developed for cricket, cycling, tennis
and athletics. The football club arrived in 1897 as **rent-paying tenants**,
initially using the cricket pavilion, and stayed ninety-seven years. There were
stands on three sides only; the fourth was open to the cricket outfield. That
was the ground that hosted the 1965/66 season, and the reason the club could
not consolidate is not complicated: it did not own its home, and could not
develop what it did not own.

The last match there was on **12 October 1994**, a 1–0 defeat by Mansfield.

**Sixfields was built by the borough council, not by the club** — the contractor
was appointed in December 1993 to build "Northampton Borough Council's new
Sixfields Community Stadium" — and the freehold is still public, held now by
West Northamptonshire Council. The club began the 1994/95 season back at the
County Ground because the stadium was late, and moved in on 15 October 1994.

The East Stand is the part everyone knows, and the timeline is the point.
**Work stopped in October 2014 and did not resume until April 2024** — nearly a
decade of a football club playing in front of an unfinished structure paid for
with money that had gone somewhere else. A rival bidder challenged the land
deal that finally unlocked it; the council won the judicial review; the sale
completed in April 2024; the rebuilt stand was finished in February 2025 and
formally opened during 2025/26. Capacity is now a little over 8,200, and for
the first time the ground has hospitality facing the pitch.
