## Origins

Colchester United was founded in 1937, and for most of that year there were
two football clubs in Colchester.

**Colchester Town**, founded in October 1873, was the town's club — amateur,
sixty-four years old, playing at Layer Road. Along the coast, **Ipswich Town
turned professional in 1936** and were elected to the Football League in 1938,
and the prospect of that concentrated minds in Colchester. A professional
club, **Colchester United, was formed in 1937**, took over Layer Road, took
the blue and white stripes and took the crest. Town, £300 in debt and stripped
of players by United's new reserve side, folded that December.

Why Town died is genuinely contested, and the two accounts attribute opposite
motives to the same people. One has Town's own officials **opposing**
professionalism, so that supporters founded a rival professional club against
them. The other has United formed **by Town's own owners**, who intended to
run an amateur Town alongside a professional United and were prevented by the
county association's rules. This page will not choose between them. What is
not in doubt is the legal position: **Colchester United is a separate creation,
not a continuation.** The 1873 date belongs to a club that no longer exists.

The badge has an off-pitch cause too, and a very English one. From 1937 the
club used the borough's coat of arms, inherited from Town. **In 1972 a dispute
with Colchester Borough Council over the club's use of the borough arms forced
a redesign**, and the replacement was a Roman eagle — Colchester having been
Camulodunum, and Britain's oldest recorded town. There is an eagle on the
shirt because of an argument with the council about intellectual property.

Layer Road's own origins are military. It opened in September 1907, its
inaugural match was won 10–2 by a battalion of the King's Royal Rifle Corps,
and it was handed to the Army as a drill ground during the First World War.
The garrison connection is to the ground rather than to the club.

## Trajectory

Colchester reached the Football League by a rule change rather than a
promotion. **In 1950 the League expanded from 88 clubs to 92**, and Colchester
went into the Third Division South along with a re-elected Gillingham, who
also appear in this batch.

**On 13 February 1971 they beat Leeds United 3–2 in the FA Cup fifth round at
Layer Road, as a fourth-tier club.** Leeds were top of the First Division that
day and finished the season a point behind Arsenal — the accurate description
is the best side in England at the time, not the reigning champions, which
they were not. Colchester led 3–0 through a pair from Ray Crawford and one
from Dave Simmons before Leeds pulled two back. The side was old enough to be
called Dad's Army, and the result is still listed among the FA Cup's largest
shocks.

**Nineteen years later they finished bottom of the entire ninety-two-club
Football League.** In 1989/90 Colchester were twenty-fourth of twenty-four in
the Fourth Division on 43 points and went into the Conference — their first
season outside the League in forty years — and what happened next is in the
next section, because it was not a football matter.

Two seasons in the Conference followed. The second, **1991/92, produced a
double**: the club won the division and the **FA Trophy**, beating Witton
Albion 3–1 at Wembley on 10 May 1992, on its first appearance there in
fifty-five years of existence. The title itself is the season already
documented on this site's **Wycombe Wanderers** page, from Wycombe's side —
one promotion place, two clubs level on 94 points, Colchester up on goal
difference. What Colchester's side adds is smaller and better: **the December
meeting between those two clubs drew 919 people**. The average home gate that
season was 3,381; the two sides who would end it inseparable played in front
of a crowd you could fit in a school hall.

*A note on this site's own data.* The tier-five tables for 1990/91 and 1991/92
are imported from a source that disagrees with the published record, and both
are flagged as not final. Ours has Colchester winning 1990/91, when Barnet
did, and Wycombe winning 1991/92, when Colchester did. The published record is
what this page follows.

Colchester's highest finish came in **2006/07 — tenth in the Championship**,
the only full season the club has spent in the second tier. They were relegated
the following year, in the season they moved grounds, and have been in the
fourth tier since 2017.

**They have never had a point deducted and have never been through an
insolvency process.** For a club that finished bottom of the Football League
and was rescued by its council, that is worth recording.

## Ownership & Finance

**Colchester United was saved in March 1991 by the borough council buying its
ground.**

With the club in the Conference and in debt, the council bought **Layer Road
for around £1m** and leased it back for a maximum of three years. That is the
transaction the 1992 promotion rests on: there was a club to promote because a
local authority had bought its stadium. And it set the pattern for everything
since — **Colchester United went back into the Football League as a tenant of
the ground it had just sold, and has not owned a ground since.**

**Robbie Cowling** bought a controlling stake in 2006 and later took the club
to 100% by compulsory purchase of the residual holdings — around 35,000 shares
spread across some 110 small shareholders, after fans had voluntarily sold him
half a million for £300,000. The town's MP, himself one of those squeezed out,
called it making the club the private plaything of a millionaire; Cowling's
answer was that the residual holdings were worth less than the cost of
administering them.

His money comes from **JobServe**, the online recruitment company he
co-founded in 1994, and the scale of the funding is on the record: a **£3.6m
loss for the year to June 2025**, cumulative losses of about **£45.9m**, and
**£40.2m owed to his own group** — with, as the club puts it, no external debt
at all. That distinction matters and this site's finance table does not draw
it: the figure is money owed to the owner, not borrowing from a bank.

What is unusual is his stated ceiling. Cowling has said that even if Colchester
were promoted he would fund League One on **a relegation budget**, and set out
his reasoning in terms of three kinds of owner — one who loses what he is
comfortable losing, one who loses more than he is comfortable with, and one
who loses more than he can afford — with the observation that too many fall
into the last two. He has been trying to hand the club on for years and cannot:
**three takeovers collapsed in the twelve months to June 2026**, the last of
them fronted by John Terry.

Alongside that sits a genuine commitment. **Colchester run a Category Two
academy while playing in League Two**, which is expensive and rare at that
level, and it works as a business: Sammie Szmodics, Kane Vincent-Young,
Macauley Bonne, Kwame Poku and Junior Tchamadeu all came through it and were
sold on. Player trading is not an accident here; it is the plan.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Colchester United does not own its stadium, and the council that owns it is
the same council that saved the club in 1991.**

The **Colchester Community Stadium** opened in August 2008 at Cuckoo Farm on
council land, at a total cost of **£15.4m**, of which the council found **£10m**
— part of it borrowed — with further money from the regional development
agency, central government and the Football Stadia Improvement Fund. **The
council also donated the land, and put the eventual proceeds of the Layer Road
sale into the project.** It owns the freehold and lets the ground through a
wholly owned subsidiary, which sub-lets to the club. Any sale of Colchester
United conveys the lease, not the ground.

Layer Road itself, closed in 2008 and demolished in 2012, was sold by **the
council** — not the club — to a housebuilder in 2011 for about £1.5m, against
a hoped-for £4m before the property market turned. Fifty-eight houses stand
there.

The occupancy terms are the most elegant thing on this page. **Since 2018 the
club's rent has been a percentage of gate receipts rather than a fixed sum,
and JobServe — the owner's own company — took the naming rights for a fee
equal to that rent.** The club therefore occupies the stadium effectively
rent-free, and the arrangement scales: more supporters through the turnstiles
means more rent to the council and more sponsorship from the owner. It is a
neat piece of structuring, and it is also a fair summary of the club's whole
position. Colchester United has been kept going by its council and its owner
in roughly equal measure for thirty-five years.
