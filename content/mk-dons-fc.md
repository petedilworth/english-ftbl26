## Origins

Most English clubs can be dated from a meeting in a pub, a church hall or a
factory yard. MK Dons has to be dated from a legal ruling, and the club's own
answer is a precise one: its history begins on 7 August 2004.

The sequence that produced it starts in August 2001, when Wimbledon FC's
board announced an intention to move to Milton Keynes. Wimbledon had sold
Plough Lane in 1991 and been tenants at Crystal Palace's Selhurst Park ever
since. The Football League board rejected the application unanimously on 16
August 2001, its chief executive saying the exceptional circumstances that
might justify such a move did not exist. Wimbledon took the contractual route
to arbitration, and in January 2002 a three-person FA panel found the
League's decision had not been properly taken and its procedures were unfair
— a ruling that decided nothing about the merits and simply sent the question
back. The League referred it on to an FA commission of enquiry in April.

That commission — Raj Parker, the FA's legal adviser, in the chair, with
Steve Stride of Aston Villa and Alan Turvey of the Isthmian League — sat over
four days in May 2002 and reported on the 28th, approving the relocation by
two votes to one. The dissenter is widely reported to have been Turvey, and
no reasoning for the minority view is on the public record. The majority
found exceptional circumstances: eleven years without a ground of the club's
own, shareholders unwilling to keep funding it, evidence from Deloitte &
Touche of losses estimated at £3-4m a year attributable to not playing in its
own stadium, half the running costs of Selhurst Park with no right to brand
or market the place, attendances down about nine per cent to an average of
6,832 — and a finding that without a move the club would be liquidated. Both
the FA and the Football League had opposed the move, and both said afterwards
that the decision was not an open door to franchising.

The passage that survives is a different one. Considering what supporters
might do instead, the majority wrote that resurrecting the club from its
ashes as, say, "Wimbledon Town" would "not be in the wider interests of
football." AFC Wimbledon was founded two days later and made the phrase its
slogan.

What happened next split the club in two along a line that is legal rather
than sentimental. The Wimbledon Football Club Ltd entered administration in
June 2003; Grant Thornton were appointed; the first match in Milton Keynes
was played at the National Hockey Stadium on 27 September 2003, a 2-2 draw
with Burnley in front of 5,639, still under the Wimbledon name. A sale
agreement with Milton Keynes Dons Limited followed on 4 March 2004, the
administrators trading on to the end of the season with a £1.5m loan, and the
company voluntary arrangement that emerged paid football creditors in full
and preferential creditors, the Inland Revenue among them, about thirty pence
in the pound. The Revenue's challenge to that arrangement was dismissed by
the Court of Appeal in *Inland Revenue Commissioners v Wimbledon Football
Club Ltd* on 28 May 2004 — two years to the day after the commission
reported — which is now a leading English authority on the football creditors
rule. The League approved the change of name to Milton Keynes Dons in June
2004, and the new name was first played under on 7 August.

The line was then drawn deliberately. In October 2006 the club, its own
supporters' association, the Wimbledon Independent Supporters' Association
and the Football Supporters' Federation reached an accord: Wimbledon FC's
replica trophies and memorabilia, its trademarks and its domain names would
go to the London Borough of Merton, MK Dons would renounce any claim to
Wimbledon's honours and playing record, and any reference to Milton Keynes
Dons would relate only to events after 7 August 2004. The handover took place
on 2 August 2007, the club's chairman presenting the collection — a miniature
1988 FA Cup among it — to the Mayor of Merton, and it went on display in
Morden Library the following year. The League share transferred in 2004; the
history did not.

The name kept one word from before, and it is the word both clubs want. "The
Dons" was Wimbledon's, a plain contraction of the place, and it is now the
nickname of two clubs sixty miles apart, one of which exists because the
other moved.

This database draws the same line in a slightly different place, and it is
worth naming as a judgement rather than a fact. Wimbledon FC holds rows from
1993/94 to 2002/03. The MK Dons record begins with 2003/04 — a season played
half in south London and half in Milton Keynes, whose club name in the
standings still reads "Wimbledon" — because that is the season of the move.
The club's own reckoning starts a year later, at the renaming. Neither
boundary is wrong; they are answers to different questions, one about where
the football was played and one about what the club agreed to call its own.

## Trajectory

MK Dons have never played in the top flight, and one season in the second,
under their own name, is the whole of their record above League One: 2015/16,
twenty-third and relegated. That makes the trajectory unusually legible. The club arrived in
the Football League with a second-tier place inherited from a relegation it
had just suffered — Wimbledon finished bottom of the First Division in
2003/04 with 29 points — and then went down again in 2006 to the fourth tier,
where the actual building started.

The rise from there was quick: fourth in 2006/07, champions of League Two in
2007/08 with the Football League Trophy alongside it, and a decade in League
One that produced three top-six finishes before the promotion of 2014/15. The
falls have been quicker. Relegated from the Championship at the first attempt
in 2016, out of League One in 2018, promoted in 2019, third in 2021/22 and
beaten in the play-offs, relegated again in 2023, and back up as runners-up
in League Two in 2025/26.

The off-pitch cause underneath all of it is the one the commission was asked
to weigh. A club that moves does not take its support with it. Wimbledon's
average attendance in the season before the ruling was 6,832 and falling; MK
Dons started from something closer to nothing, in a town with no Football
League history and a ground built for numbers the football has rarely
justified. Nothing about the record since suggests the relocation solved a
footballing problem. What it solved was a property problem.

## Ownership & Finance

The stadium came before the club, and that is the fact the whole arrangement
turns on. Pete Winkelman, a music promoter, led the Milton Keynes Stadium
Consortium from 2000 — before any approach to Wimbledon — with a scheme for a
stadium at Denbigh, in Bletchley, underwritten not by football but by the
commercial development around it. Asda, IKEA and an IMAX cinema paid for the
ground; football was the anchor tenant the planning case needed. Build cost
is reported at around £50m.

That is an unusual order of operations, and it explains the shape of the
business that resulted. Winkelman's Inter MK Group lent the administrators
£1.5m to keep Wimbledon playing to the end of 2003/04 and bought the club out
of its company voluntary arrangement; the same interests owned the stadium,
the hotel built into the West Stand, the arena next door and the local radio
station. The football club was one asset in a property group rather than the
group's reason for existing.

It was sold that way too. On 9 August 2024 Winkelman sold MK Dons together
with the wider Stadium MK Group — the club, the Sports and Education Trust,
the stadium, the hotel, the Marshall Arena, MKFM and the car parking — to a
Kuwait-based consortium led by Fahad Al Ghanim, whose family also owns Kuwait
SC. The EFL ratified the deal and the parties stated the club and the stadium
group were left debt-free. Twenty years of one ownership ended in a single
transaction because the club had never been separable from the property in
the first place.

Almost every club in this database has at some point had its ground and its
football separated, usually to the football's cost. MK Dons is the inverse
case: a club that has never been anything other than part of its stadium's
business, which is a structural protection and a structural dependence at the
same time.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The first three and a half years were spent at the National Hockey Stadium, a
temporary and largely unsuitable home, before Stadium MK opened for a
restricted-entry match against a young Chelsea side on 18 July 2007 and was
formally opened by Queen Elizabeth II that November. Designed by Populous,
it opened at around 22,000 with the pitch dug well below ground level so that
later expansion meant adding to the top rather than rebuilding, and a
122-room hotel occupying the West Stand. The expansion duly came for
2014/15, taking capacity to around 30,000 with the hotel extended to 300
bedrooms wrapped around the bowl.

The result is a ground that spends most of its life larger than the football
in it. Its record attendance — 30,048 — was set by Fiji against Uruguay at
the 2015 Rugby World Cup, not by a home fixture; it hosted three World Cup
matches that year and group games and a semi-final at the 2022 Women's Euros.
A League One and League Two club owns a stadium built to host international
tournaments, which is the physical form of the same fact the ownership
history shows: the venue was the project, and the club came to fill it.

Milton Keynes itself is the underlying cause. Designated a new town in 1967
and built out across the following decades, it reached a population of a
quarter of a million with no Football League club of its own, in a country
where almost every town of that size acquired one before 1930. The commission
that approved the move in 2002 was, whatever else it was doing, resolving
that mismatch by transfer rather than by growth — and the League and the FA
both said immediately afterwards that the decision was not an open door to
franchising. No comparable relocation has been approved since, though no rule
change is on the record that would prevent one — what stands in the way is a
precedent nobody involved wanted to set twice.
