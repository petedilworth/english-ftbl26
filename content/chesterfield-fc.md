## Origins

Chesterfield puts 1866 on its crest and the evidence supports 1867. The club's
own historian dates the first Chesterfield FC to **19 October 1867**, at a
meeting of Chesterfield Cricket Club held at the County Hotel on Saltergate,
and that is the only date for which documentary evidence exists.

The larger problem is continuity, and the same historian is the one who raises
it. On his account there have been **four clubs of the name**, and the present
one is the fourth:

- the first, founded 1867, lasting to 1881;
- the second, formed 1884, professional as **Chesterfield Town**, elected to
  the Football League in 1899, and **into voluntary liquidation in 1915**;
- the third, formed immediately after, which **disbanded in 1917 after a
  scandal over payments to players** in which around forty players and
  officials were suspended;
- and the fourth, **Chesterfield Municipal FC, founded on 24 April 1919 by
  Chesterfield Council** — a town council founding its own football club —
  which won the Midland League in 1920 and **became Chesterfield FC in
  December 1920 under pressure from the football authorities to cut its ties
  with the council**.

That last item is a better origin story than the date dispute, and it is
documented: the present legal entity exists because a local authority created
a football club and was then made to let go of it.

**The Spireites** are named for the crooked spire of the parish church of St
Mary and All Saints, which twists about forty-five degrees and leans nine and
a half feet from true. Two technical explanations are current and neither is
folklore: unseasoned timber under some thirty-two tons of lead cladding, and —
now generally preferred — differential thermal expansion, the sun heating the
lead on the south face so that it expands more than the lead on the north, and
rotating the frame a little at a time over centuries. The devil is not
involved.

## Trajectory

**Chesterfield are the only club in this site's records ever promoted while
carrying a points deduction**, and that sentence needs both halves.

In 2000/01 the Football League found the club in breach of its regulations
and a disciplinary panel — the League's, not the FA's — deducted **nine
points** and fined it £20,000. Chesterfield **earned 89 points and finished
the season third on 80, and went up**. Across sixty-nine seasons and every
division in the data, no other club has been promoted with points removed.

The second half is that the deduction still cost them something. Restore the
nine and Chesterfield finish **second on 89**, above Cardiff City's 82.
Brighton took the title on 92 and would have taken it either way. The nine
points did not cost the championship, as is often written; they cost the
runners-up place, and Chesterfield were promoted regardless.

What the deduction was for is the subject of the next section.

The club has never played above the third tier — thirty-five seasons there is
its ceiling — and its **four fourth-tier titles**, in 1970, 1985, 2011 and
2014, are the joint most of any club in the data, level with Doncaster Rovers.

Between those, in **1997, Chesterfield reached the FA Cup semi-final as a
third-tier club** and drew 3–3 with Middlesbrough at Old Trafford before losing
the replay. The disallowed goal is remembered as a robbery and the record is
more particular than that. Leading 2–1 on 68 minutes, Jonathan Howard's shot
hit the underside of the bar and came down apparently over the line. The
referee, David Elleray, has **accepted that the ball crossed the line** — but
his stated reason for not giving it is that he had already blown for a free
kick to Middlesbrough for a foul in the build-up, so on his account it was
never a line decision at all. Chesterfield's players dispute that; one of them
says the linesman had given the goal. Elleray later apologised to Howard in
person. It would have made it 3–1, not 4–1, and **no third-tier club has ever
reached an FA Cup final**, so Chesterfield would have been the first.

Relegation out of the Football League came in successive drops — twenty-fourth
of twenty-four in League One in 2016/17 and again in League Two in 2017/18 —
and the return took **six seasons**, as **National League champions in 2023/24
on 98 points**. The season before that they lost the National League play-off
final on penalties to Notts County, who also appear in this batch.

## Ownership & Finance

**Darren Brown bought Chesterfield for £1.2m in 2000, took more than £800,000
out of it, and went to prison.**

Brown ran a print-broking business and a sports group holding ice hockey and
basketball clubs, and raised £400,000 from business contacts to buy a
controlling stake in Chesterfield, which he regarded as the flagship. What he
then did has three documented strands.

**He disconnected three turnstiles from the computer that recorded
attendances.** All three took cash on the day, so there was no record of how
many people came through or of what they paid. Declared crowds of around four
thousand were run against real crowds of seven or eight.

**He produced a false contract to a transfer tribunal.** A bogus document
halving a player's real wages and more than halving his signing-on fee fed the
tribunal's valuation, setting the fee at £150,000.

**And he paid players illegally in cash** — bonuses of up to £600 in envelopes
after matches, said to have come straight from the money taken through the
disconnected turnstiles.

Meanwhile more than £800,000 of the club's money went to prop up his other
failing businesses, **the ground was mortgaged** to guarantee a loan, and he
offered to sell the Saltergate pitch to developers. Administrators found the
club roughly £2m in debt about a year after he arrived, having been solvent
before. Brown relinquished control in March 2001 as the evidence mounted,
**pleaded guilty to two counts of fraudulent trading and was jailed for four
years** at Nottingham Crown Court, the judge calling him entirely reckless as
a businessman while noting he had seemed ambitious, plausible and persuasive
to those who backed him.

**The club was saved by its supporters, twice over.** The Chesterfield
Football Supporters' Society was formed at a meeting attended by a thousand
fans, a collection raised £6,000, and the Society bought the club days later —
then discovered the mortgaged ground and the £2m and **put the club into
administration itself**. Day-to-day running went to two local businessmen, an
accountant and a supporter who had won the lottery, **who personally
guaranteed the major debts**, and the Society emerged with a court order
taking the club out of administration in fan ownership.

Which is where the site's usual caution applies, because two different bodies
get conflated. The **Supporters' Society** owned the club from 2001 and lost
control to a consortium of former directors in 2003. The **Community Trust**
that bought the club from Dave Allen in **August 2020** is a separate
organisation — a registered charity affiliated to the club, seventeen years
later, believed to be the first community-trust charity to own a professional
English club, and financed by **£500,000 loans from each of the borough and
county councils, secured by a joint charge on the stadium**.

**Chesterfield is not fan-owned now.** Phil and Ashley Kirk, brothers from the
town, invested £1m in 2022, more in 2023, and took **majority ownership in
March 2024**; total family investment has since approached £10m. Phil Kirk
died in August 2025, aged 59, and his widow is now effectively the owner with
his brother as chairman. The Community Trust retains a minority stake.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Saltergate** was home from 1871 until July 2010 — about a hundred and
thirty-nine years, one of the oldest grounds in England when it closed. Its
record crowd was 30,561 against Tottenham in 1938; its final capacity was
just over eight thousand, cut by post-Taylor seating. The last match was
against Bournemouth on 8 May 2010, and the site was sold for housing in 2012.

The replacement stands on the twenty-two-acre site of the former **Dema Glass
works** at Whittington Moor, ten acres of which the club acquired from the
developer redeveloping the rest. Planning permission came in July 2008 after
the government declined to call the scheme in, the council wanting a stadium
to anchor its regeneration of the corridor north of the town centre. It cost
**£13m** and opened for 2010/11.

Who paid is a mixture, and the council is in it twice. **Dave Allen's £4m for
eighty per cent of the club in May 2009 was directed principally at the
stadium**; the borough council lent the club £1m in 2009, since repaid; the
regional development agency contributed; the club put in £500,000. The **club
owns the ground** — but since 2020 it carries a joint legal charge in favour
of both councils securing their loans, so the councils hold security over it
rather than title. It has been the b2net, the Proact, the Technique and now
the SMH Group Stadium in fifteen years.
