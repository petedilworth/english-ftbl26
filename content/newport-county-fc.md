## Origins

**Newport County was wound up in the High Court on 27 February 1989, and the
football club that plays in Newport today was founded four months later by
people who had watched it happen.**

The original club was formed in 1912, was a founder member of the Football
League's Third Division in 1920, and lost its League place in 1988. What
finished it was a winding-up order. Within the same season it was **expelled
from the Football Conference for failing to fulfil its fixtures and its record
expunged** — so nothing it did in 1988/89 counts, or exists. An appeal to the FA
was rejected on the ground that only the liquidators had standing to bring one.

**Newport AFC** was formed in June 1989 at a public meeting in the Lysaght
Institute and elected to the Hellenic League, six divisions below where the old
club had been. It took the name **Newport County AFC** in 1999, after
supporters were balloted on three options.

There is **no legal continuity**, and the proof of that is unusually neat,
because two bodies said so simultaneously and in opposite directions. **Newport
City Council refused the new club use of Somerton Park on the ground that it
was a continuation of the old one and liable for its arrears. The Football
Association of Wales ruled that it had no connection to Newport County at
all.** Whichever answer suited the body giving it.

The club's own merchandise carries both dates — **1912 and 1989, with the word
"Exiles" between them** — which is the honest answer and also the whole story.

**This site's own data cannot render any of it.** Newport's standings run from
1959 to 1988 and then stop, and start again in 2011. **The twenty-two missing
seasons are what a liquidation costs**, and they are the longest such gap in
this database. The rows before and after the hole belong to different companies,
and the table cannot say so.

## Trajectory

The original club spent sixty seasons in the Football League and left them
comprehensively. **In 1987/88 Newport County finished twenty-fourth of
twenty-four on 25 points with a goal difference of minus seventy — nineteen
points behind the club above them.** It was their second successive relegation,
and it made them only the second club automatically relegated from the Football
League, after Lincoln City the year before.

Before that there had been a genuine high point, and it came through the same
Welsh route this site records on Cardiff's and Shrewsbury's pages. Newport won
the **Welsh Cup in 1980** — beating Shrewsbury, who as an English club could not
have taken the European place anyway — and went into the **Cup Winners' Cup**,
where they beat Crusaders and a Norwegian side before losing a quarter-final to
Carl Zeiss Jena. A Fourth Division club two seasons earlier, a European
quarter-final.

The phoenix club took twenty-four years to get back. Hellenic League champions
at the first attempt, up through the Southern League, into the Conference South
on the 2004 restructure, champions of it in 2010, and then **the Conference
Premier play-off final at Wembley on 5 May 2013, beating Wrexham 2–0** — the
first Wembley final between two Welsh clubs.

Since 2013 they have played **fourteen consecutive seasons in the fourth tier**
without going up, losing play-off finals in 2019 and 2021, and the season most
worth recording is one they nearly lost. **In 2016/17 Newport were eleven points
adrift in March and stayed up on the final day**, finishing twenty-second on 48
points with Hartlepool relegated on 46. Cheltenham, also in this batch, were
twenty-first on 50 in the same table.

**Newport County have never had a point deducted** — neither club.

## Ownership & Finance

**The reformed club was not allowed to play in Newport, and it took a High
Court ruling to change that.**

The first exile followed the winding-up directly: refused Somerton Park by the
council over the old club's arrears and refused sanction by the FAW, Newport AFC
spent **1989/90 playing home games at Moreton-in-Marsh in Gloucestershire**, in
England, some eighty-five miles away. Two seasons back at Somerton Park
followed.

Then the FAW created the **League of Wales for 1992/93** and required Welsh
clubs in the English pyramid to join it. Newport refused, and **the FAW
withdrew permission for them to play at home at all** — so they went to
**Gloucester for 1992/93 and 1993/94**. Two separate exiles, with two different
causes, and two full seasons at home in between.

It ended in court. In ***Newport Association Football Club Ltd and others v
Football Association of Wales Ltd***, decided by **Mr Justice Blackburne on 11
April 1995**, the FAW's ban was held to be an **unreasonable restraint of
trade**, and the court rejected the argument that it was needed to protect the
League of Wales's European representation. The claimants were **Newport,
Caernarfon Town and Colwyn Bay** — three non-league clubs, three of the eight
the FAW had leaned on. Cardiff, Swansea and Wrexham were never in that fight and
were never punished, which this site's Cardiff page now records. Newport had
already returned home in 1994 under interim relief.

The nickname the club has carried ever since comes from those seasons.

The **Supporters Trust took control on 1 October 2015**, when the previous
majority owner transferred his shareholding, alongside a community share issue
that raised around £236,000 against a £195,000 target, and held the club for
eight years. In **September 2023 the members voted to give it up** — 455 of 464
in favour — and in **January 2024 Huw Jenkins**, formerly of Swansea City,
bought **52%** for an initial £500,000.

**Newport County is therefore not a fan-owned club.** The Trust holds **27%**
and two board seats, with the rest spread among other investors. Jenkins has
since said publicly that he would sell to a buyer meeting the EFL's and the new
regulator's conditions, and as of mid-2026 a purchase of his stake was awaiting
regulatory approval. Turnover runs to around £4.5m, of which about £1m a year
goes on the ground.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Newport County have not owned a ground since 1989 and do not own one now.**

**Somerton Park** was the original club's home from 1912. The phoenix club was
barred from it in 1989, used it from 1990 to 1992, was barred from Wales
entirely from 1992 to 1994 — and it was **demolished in October 1993**, while
the club was in Gloucester. A housing estate called Somerton Park stands on the
site.

**Newport Stadium at Spytty Park** took them in for 1994/95 and held them for
eighteen years. They left in 2012 because the pitch was judged not fit for
purpose against Conference and Football League grading requirements, and it
remains the training base.

**Rodney Parade** is a rugby ground, and the club is a tenant in it. It was
owned by **Newport RFC**, then by the **Welsh Rugby Union from 2017**, and since
2023 by the **private consortium that took the Dragons back into private
ownership**, the deal explicitly covering the nine-acre site. Newport signed a
ten-year lease in 2013, a short-term deal in 2023 and a new ten-year lease in
July 2024 — the last of them, the chairman said, to satisfy the EFL's rules on
security of tenure.

Sharing a pitch with two rugby teams did what sharing a pitch with two rugby
teams does. New drainage went in during the summers of 2013 and 2014 and the
problems continued: matches **abandoned at half-time against Barnet in
September 2016 and Morecambe that December** for waterlogging, an FA Cup tie
against Blackburn postponed, and a contributing cause that reads like a
parable — the pitch had been laser-levelled for the first time since it was laid
in **1875**, and settled unevenly. A hybrid surface went in after the WRU
takeover.

Capacity for football is 7,850. The ground's all-time record crowd, 31,000,
belongs to Newport RFC against South Africa in 1952 — which is a fair summary of
whose ground it is.
