## Origins

The club was founded in 1899 by a man who could not play football.

**Bartley Wilson** was a lithographic artist who could not walk unaided
without sticks, and in the autumn of 1899 he called a meeting at his own home
near Cardiff Castle to give the members of **Riverside Cricket Club**
something to do through the winter. He was elected secretary of the club he
had convened, which is the role he kept. The exact dates of those founding
meetings are not known — the club says so plainly — so it treats **7 October
1899**, the first recorded fixture, a friendly against Barry West End, as its
birthday. A football club whose founding date is its first match rather than
its first meeting is being more honest than most.

It was called **Riverside AFC**, and the name it has now had to be applied
for. Cardiff was granted **city status in 1905**, and the club's request to
take the city's name was refused before it was granted on **5 September
1908**. Twelve years later, in May 1920, it was elected to an expanded
Football League Second Division.

The Bluebirds came from the theatre. Maurice Maeterlinck's play *The Blue
Bird* ran for six nights at the **New Theatre, Cardiff, in late October
1911** — Maeterlinck won the Nobel Prize in Literature a week after the run
closed — and a supporter applied the title to a team that had recently changed
from chocolate and amber into royal blue. It became official later. That the
nickname of one of British football's more combative supporter cultures
derives from a Belgian symbolist play about two children searching for
happiness is not a fact the club advertises.

## Trajectory

Cardiff City are **the only club from outside England ever to have won the FA
Cup**, beating Arsenal 1–0 at Wembley on 23 April 1927 through Hughie
Ferguson, and a century later that remains true. It is worth being precise
about what being Welsh in the English league system has and has not cost
them.

When the FAW created the League of Wales for 1992/93, **Cardiff, Swansea and
Wrexham declined to join and were never punished for it** — which is the part
usually told backwards. The FAW's coercion, banning clubs from playing home
matches in Wales, was aimed at the smaller "Irate Eight", and it worked on
them: Newport County, among others, was exiled to Gloucester for two seasons
until three of the eight took the FAW to the High Court and won. Mr Justice
Blackburne ruled in April 1995 that the ban was an unreasonable restraint of
trade. Cardiff were never in that fight; they were too big to be leaned on.

What Cardiff actually lost was quieter and permanent: UEFA held that the Welsh
Cup could only feed a European place if clubs playing in England were excluded
from it, and from **1995/96 Cardiff were barred from the Welsh Cup**. Before
that, the Welsh Cup had been their route into Europe. A Welsh club in the
English pyramid is not penalised for being there. It simply has one fewer door
than everyone else, and the door it lost was the European one.

Below that, the pattern is one of the most volatile in the country: four
top-flight seasons in the sixty-nine this site records, forty-four in the
second tier, and ten in the fourth. Relegation in **2024/25**, bottom of the
Championship on 44 points, put the club in the third tier for the first time
since 2002/03; they came straight back up in 2025/26 as runners-up on 91
points, behind Lincoln City. The BBC's account of the relegation attributed it
to governance rather than a bad season — three managers in that year, eight in
the previous four, and a wage bill larger than two-thirds of the division.

One negative is worth stating because of the company it keeps: across all
sixty-nine seasons in this site's data, **Cardiff City have never had a point
deducted**. For a club that spent 2009 and 2010 fighting winding-up petitions,
that is not nothing.

## Ownership & Finance

In May 2012 Cardiff City announced that its home shirt would change from blue
to **red**, that the bluebird would be removed from the middle of the badge
and replaced with a Welsh dragon, and that the words **"Fire & Passion"**
would be added underneath.

The stated reason was market access. The club's own announcement described
reactivating rebranding proposals "with a view to exploiting and maximising
its brand and commercial revenues in international markets"; the investors'
view was that red sold better in Asia and carried more Welsh resonance —
the dragon, the flag. **Vincent Tan's** own account is less corporate and more
useful: *"I put in, I think £20 million and said that's enough and said I'm
willing to put in maybe £100 million if I'm able to change the colour from
blue to red and I said red's my lucky colour."*

Two details are usually left out, and both matter.

**It had been tried before.** The rebrand was floated in 2010 and dropped
after what the then chairman called vociferous opposition, then brought back
in 2012 attached to an investment pledge. The second attempt was not a
misjudgement of the mood; the mood was already known.

**And it was never fully reversed.** Supporters organised — Bluebirds Unite,
a heritage walk in 2013, a march in March 2014 that for the first time united
all five major fan bodies — and on **9 January 2015**, the morning after a
decisive meeting with supporters, Tan agreed to restore blue. But that was
the *kit*. The crest was not revealed until March 2015 and did not restore
the old badge: the bluebird returned to the centre and "Fire & Passion" was
dropped, but **the red dragon was kept**, moved to the foot of the shield.
Cardiff played the whole of their 2013/14 Premier League season in red and the
whole of 2018/19 in blue. What the supporters won back was most of it.

The rest of the finances are a long argument about money owed. Sam Hammam's
control from 2000 left behind **£24m of loan notes issued in 2004 by
Langston Corporation**, litigated from 2007 and settled by Tan in 2013 on
terms that were never published — which is precisely why supporters' groups
formally demanded disclosure at the time. In between, in 2009 and 2010, the
club fought a **£2.7m HMRC winding-up petition**, defaulted on the payment
schedule it agreed, and by one director's account came within minutes of
administration.

Then Emiliano Sala. The Argentine forward signed from Nantes in January 2019
for €17m and died when the aircraft carrying him to Cardiff crashed in the
Channel. Cardiff refused the first instalment on the ground that he had never
been registered as their player. **FIFA ruled in September 2019 that the €6m
was owed**; the **Court of Arbitration for Sport dismissed Cardiff's appeal in
August 2022**, finding the transfer complete and its conditions fulfilled
before Sala's death; and in January 2023, under an EFL transfer embargo,
Cardiff paid around £7m — the instalment plus accrued interest. A further
ruling has ordered roughly €11m for the remaining instalments. The matter is
not closed.

The structural position is simpler than any of it. At 31 May 2025 the club
owed Tan close to **£90m** against total liabilities of £161m; a **£42m share
issue in May 2026, subscribed solely by Tan**, converted that much of the debt
to equity and left about £55m outstanding. The supporters' trust chairman, a
chartered accountant, describes the club as balance-sheet insolvent. Cardiff
City is solvent because one man has decided it is.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Ninian Park was named after a guarantor. When the club took the lease on a
former rubbish tip in 1910 it needed someone to stand behind the **£90 annual
rent**, and **Lord Ninian Crichton-Stuart**, second son of the Marquess of
Bute, did. He was elected MP for Cardiff two months after the ground opened
and was killed at Loos in 1915. The ground carried his name for ninety-nine
years.

By the 1990s the club owed money to its landlord, the council, and **closed
three of the four stands to save on policing costs** — a detail that says more
about the period than any league position. Ninian Park closed in 2009 with a
capacity of 21,508, was handed to a housebuilder that September, and was
demolished by the end of the year. A hundred and forty-two houses stand there
now, on streets that kept the name.

The replacement is a more complicated object than it looks. **Cardiff City
Stadium** opened in 2009 at a final cost of £48m, seating 26,828, and was
expanded to **33,280** in 2014 by adding a tier to the Ninian Stand. The
stadium company belongs to the club's holding company — but the land does not.
The site is Cardiff City Council's, granted on a **125-year lease in November
2006** out of a wider retail and leisure development at Leckwith, and the
accurate formulation is that the club owns the stadium but not the ground it
stands on. Around it sit a 470,000 square foot retail park, a replacement
athletics stadium, a hotel and a housing estate: the football ground is one
component of a regeneration scheme, which is how most new English grounds are
now financed and rarely how they are described.
