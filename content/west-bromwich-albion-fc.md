## Origins

West Bromwich Albion began in 1878 as **West Bromwich Strollers**, a team of
young men who worked at George Salter's Spring Works. Their first match, that
November, was a goalless draw with another works side from a soap factory.
For the first years the club was run by a seven-man playing committee and
funded by a subscription of sixpence a week.

In 1880 they became West Bromwich Albion, and in doing so **became the first
club anywhere to take the "Albion" suffix** — every Brighton, Burton and
Stirling Albion since is downstream of a Black Country spring works. They
turned professional in 1885, won the FA Cup in 1888, and were founder members
of the Football League later the same year.

The nickname is one of English football's genuinely unsolved questions, and
the honest answer is that nobody knows. Two things are agreed: it appeared at
The Hawthorns in the 1900s, a quarter of a century after the club was
founded, and **the club spent decades trying not to be called it** before
giving in. That second fact is the most revealing one — a name the
institution found embarrassing, which won anyway, and now appears on the
merchandise and the mascot.

Of the explanations, the one with a named source is the club historian Tony
Matthews's: the men who collected the gate money carried it in large leather
bags from the two entrances behind the goals round the perimeter track to the
cash office at half-time, to shouts of "here come the bagmen". It has a
mechanism and a ground with only two entrances to support it. The more
attractive version — that Aston Villa supporters mocked the baggy trousers
worn by Albion's foundry-working crowd, cut loose to keep molten iron off the
skin — has no contemporary attestation and the timing is wrong, since the
working dress long predates the word. The class story is the better story and
the weaker evidence.

The other nickname is the one on the badge. A **throstle** is a song thrush,
in the Black Country dialect — a word that has effectively died out of
general English and survives on a football shirt. The birds were common in
the hawthorn scrub on the site the club moved to in 1900, and the modern
crest puts a throstle on a hawthorn bough: the bird and the ground's name in
a single device.

## Trajectory

Albion have spent the majority of their existence in the first division and
have also, twice, been in the third. The clean version of the club's history
— never below the top two tiers — is wrong: they went down in 1990/91 after
losing an FA Cup tie at home to non-league Woking, finished seventh in the
Third Division in 1991/92, the lowest placing in their history, and came back
up through the play-offs in 1993.

It was in that third-tier period that the club acquired its modern chant.
"Boing Boing" dates from 1992/93 — two competing origin stories, one
involving a rave record heard in Amsterdam and one the exaggerated bounce of
the ball on Preston's plastic pitch — which makes the defining sound of a
West Bromwich crowd a product of the club's lowest ebb.

The modern pattern is the yo-yo, and its causes are financial rather than
footballing. Relegated from the Premier League in 2021 after a single season,
Albion received **two years of parachute payments rather than three** —
£44m, then £36m — and then nothing. **2023/24 was the first season since 2002
in which the club received no Premier League money of any kind**, and the
wage bill was still set at Premier League scale: **£42.9m against turnover of
£28.2m, about 152%, the highest ratio in the Championship**. The loss was
£33.9m.

The structural cause underneath that is churn in how the club decides
anything. After 2021 it abandoned the director-of-football model and went
back to a manager-led structure; under new ownership it went back the other
way, appointing a club president and sporting director, who was then
dismissed in February 2026. Two reversals of recruitment philosophy in five
years is not a run of bad luck.

## Ownership & Finance

Jeremy Peace was chairman from 2002 and built a holding approaching ninety
per cent before **selling to Guochuan Lai's Yunyi Guokai in September 2016**,
for a fee never disclosed and reported anywhere between £150m and £200m.

The Lai period produced one transaction that explains it better than any
narrative could. In **March 2021, at the height of the pandemic, £4.95m was
transferred from the football club to Wisdom Smart Corporation** — a company
related to the owner — to help it through. Repayment was deferred to the end
of 2022 and then missed. It was eventually **repaid in full with interest in
March 2024**, immediately after the club changed hands. A Championship club
lent its owner's associated company five million pounds during Covid, and got
it back only when he sold up.

Meanwhile the club borrowed. A **£20m loan from MSD in December 2022, secured
on The Hawthorns and the training ground at around 14% interest**, was
increased to £30m in November 2023 — roughly £4m a year in interest, on money
raised to fund day-to-day costs. Shilen Patel's Bilkul Football completed its
purchase in February 2024, reported at around £60m, and committed to clearing
the debt.

Minority shareholders have been an unusually real force here. The residue of
the pre-2016 share structure gave supporters a genuine constituency, and
**Shareholders for Albion** used it — pressing directors for answers, setting
deadlines and calling for a general meeting — alongside a supporters' campaign
that took its complaints as far as a parliamentary audience. Four separate
supporter organisations delivered a joint open letter in March 2023.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The Hawthorns opened on 3 September 1900, built in about four months, and was
**the first Football League ground built in the twentieth century**. It takes
its name from the hawthorn bushes on the estate it was built on, and it sits
in Sandwell hard against the Birmingham boundary — a B71 postcode, an away
end called the **Smethwick End** and a home end called the **Birmingham Road
End**. The stand names are a map of the club's position on the seam between
the Black Country and the city it is not part of.

At **551 feet above sea level it is the highest of the ninety-two English
league grounds**, which is a claim relative to who happens to be in the
league — a single promotion could falsify it.

The Taylor Report's all-seater requirement rebuilt both ends and cut capacity
to just over 25,000; the East Stand followed in 2001 at £7.5m. The current
capacity is around 26,850, and the ground and training complex are charged as
security against the MSD borrowing, which the club has said it would sell only
as an absolute last resort, after players.

The thing this club is most historically important for, though, is not a
building. In 1978/79 Albion fielded **Laurie Cunningham, Cyrille Regis and
Brendon Batson** in the same side — the first English club to do so regularly
with three Black players. The label they were given, "the Three Degrees", was
a manager's joke about a soul group that stuck as a historical term, and has
been argued with ever since for flattening three men into one act. What they
faced is documented: National Front literature handed out at grounds, being
spat at on arrival, mass monkey chanting; Batson's own description was of
having to run the gauntlet. Before his England debut in 1982, Regis received a
bullet in the post.

The institutional consequence is the part that belongs on a page about what
shaped English football rather than one club. **Batson became deputy chief
executive of the PFA from 1984 to 2002 and was central to the launch of Let's
Kick Racism Out of Football in 1993** — the campaign that became Kick It Out.
The line from those terraces to the game's first national anti-racism
machinery runs directly through one of the three players who stood on them.
The statue commemorating them was unveiled in 2019 in West Bromwich town
centre rather than at the ground, and took roughly six years to fund.
