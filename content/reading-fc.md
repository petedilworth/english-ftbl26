## Origins

Almost everything commonly said about how Reading FC began is either unproven
or wrong, and the club's own historians are the ones who established it.

The **founding date** is given nearly everywhere as **25 December 1871**, at
the Bridge Street Rooms. The better-sourced account, from the research Roger
Titford did for Reading Museum and the supporters' trust, is that the club was
formed in **the winter of 1871–72** at a first-floor room at **10 Gun Street**,
and that the precise date is not known. The reason it is not known is stark:
**no club record survives from before 7 September 1876**, the first entry in
the earliest surviving minute book, and nothing suggests an earlier one
existed. A Christmas Day founding meeting is the sort of detail that gets
tidier with retelling. The club marked its 150th anniversary across both 2021
and 2022, which fits the winter rather than the date.

The first match was on **21 February 1872**, a goalless draw with Reading
School.

**The two mergers in the club's standard history did not happen as described.**
Reading did not merge with Reading Hornets in 1877 — two Hornets players joined
Reading's committee that September, the Hornets faded, and they were never
wound up. Reading Hornets exist today and date themselves to 1873. And the 1889
"merger" with Earley was not a merger: it was a nearly-dead club scavenging.
Reading took Earley's secretary and about half its players, absorbed Kendrick
FC, and recruited the entire Theale team to represent it. The club was saved
from dissolution around 1889–90 by Horace Walker and rebuilt from there.

**Reading were never Huntley & Palmers' works team.** This is the most durable
error about the club, and the mechanism behind the nickname is more interesting
than the myth. Reading was founded in 1871 by young businessmen, shopkeepers
and students; the biscuit nickname arrives roughly twenty-five years later, in
the 1890s, and it was a **press coinage** — newspapers gave distant clubs an
identity by naming them for the town's trade. Match reports called them the
Biscuit Boys, the Biscuits, the Biscuiteers, "the team from **Biscuitopolis**,"
and the forms eventually collapsed into Biscuitmen. A separate **Huntley &
Palmers FC** did exist, and entered the FA Cup; the two are different clubs.
The Palmer family's documented connection to Reading is civic — money and land
to the town, including Palmer Park — not to the football club.

**"The Royals" was chosen by competition.** Huntley & Palmers stopped producing
in the town, the supporters' club ran a contest in the summer of 1976 to
replace a nickname that no longer meant anything, and the manager, Charlie
Hurley, picked it — for the Royal County of Berkshire, on the reasoning that
biscuits were no longer part of the place.

## Trajectory

Reading have played three seasons in the top division of English football, all
since 2006, and hold **the highest points total any club has recorded in the
second tier: 106 in 2005/06**, from 31 wins and two defeats in 46 games.

But the trajectory that defines the modern club is the other one. **Reading
were deducted points in three consecutive seasons — 2021/22, 2022/23 and
2023/24 — eighteen in all, six in each, and none of it for insolvency.** The
club never entered administration. Every sanction was regulatory: profitability
rules, unpaid wages, unpaid tax.

- **November 2021, six points**, for losses of £57.8m across 2017–2021 against a
  £39m limit, settled by agreed decision with six more suspended against
  compliance with a business plan.
- **April 2023, six points** — not a new breach but **the 2021 penalty being
  activated**, after the EFL found Reading had broken the agreed decision,
  including by relying in its submissions on profit from a proposed sale of an
  investment to an entity the club's owner controlled.
- **2023/24, six points in three separate sanctions**: one for paying players
  late three times; three when the owner missed the deadline to deposit 125% of
  the monthly wage bill; and two for persistent late payment to HMRC, with the
  owner personally fined £100,000.

**The middle one relegated them.** Reading finished 2022/23 twenty-second on 44
points. Restore the six and they finish **twentieth on 50**, level with
Rotherham and QPR and above Cardiff, and stay up. The other two deductions did
not change the outcome of their seasons; that one did.

A seventh sanction is worth recording because it was never applied. On 13
January 2024, around a thousand supporters walked onto the pitch in the
sixteenth minute of a home game against Port Vale, in protest at the owner, and
the match was abandoned. The EFL agreed a **three-point deduction, suspended**,
explicitly in line with a comparable case at Blackpool in 2015. It was never
activated, and the fixture was replayed in full. The supporters' campaign
itself urged fans not to do it again.

## Ownership & Finance

**The club sold its own stadium to its owner's parent company, which sold it on
to another company the same owner controlled, at an £11m mark-up.**

Reading was bought in 2017 by **Renhe Sports Management**, owned by the
siblings **Dai Yongge and Dai Xiu Li**, which took 75% of the shares. The
football club then sold the stadium to Renhe for **£26.5m** and leased it back
at **£1.5m a year on a lease running to June 2043**. The stadium was
subsequently sold on for **£37.5m to Prestige Fortune Asia Ltd**, a British
Virgin Islands company also owned by Dai and sitting outside the football
club's group — a gain of about £11m on what the club had been paid. Because
profitability was assessed on the consolidated group accounts, the disposals
changed the reported losses.

The training ground went the same way in the end. **Bearwood Park**, opened in
2019, is nearly 120 acres and reportedly cost over £50m. In March 2024 the club
said it needed to sell the site to "support short-term funding"; Dai said he
was open to selling it separately from the club; Wycombe Wanderers entered
talks and then put the purchase on hold. The supporters' campaign called the
club's statement inappropriate and unprofessional.

That campaign was called **Sell Before We Dai**. It ran for about two years on
volunteer labour and a small seed fund from the supporters' trust, used
advertising vans, marched, and campaigned for the Football Governance Bill.
When the club was finally sold it wound itself up and gave its remaining
£4,700, rounded up to £5,000, to the club's community trust.

The end came from an unexpected direction. In **March 2025 the EFL confirmed —
in open court — that Dai had been disqualified under its Owners' and Directors'
Test**, reportedly over his business interests in China rather than anything at
Reading, and required to divest. **On 14 May 2025 the sale to Redwood Holdings**,
owned by **Rob Couhig and Todd Trosclair** of New Orleans, completed, after a
first attempt had collapsed the previous September and Dai's stadium company had
sued Couhig's vehicle in the Commercial Court in January. The deal **included
the stadium and Bearwood Park**: for the first time since the sale-and-leaseback,
the club owns the ground it plays on and the ground it trains on.

The clearest measure of what the period cost is not in the men's accounts.
**Reading Women** were relegated from the Women's Super League in 2023, went
part-time, and then **withdrew from the Championship for 2024/25 and dropped to
the fifth tier** because the club could not guarantee their funding. A division
was left with eleven teams. A supporters' group called it the darkest day of
the ownership, and it is hard to argue.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Elm Park** was home from 1896 to 1998, and the last league match there — a
1–0 defeat by Norwich on 3 May 1998, the club already relegated — was followed
by an auction of the contents, including the chairman's armchair. It was
demolished in 2000 and built over with housing.

The move happened because of a price. Reading Borough Council sold the
club the **Smallmead site — a former rubbish tip on the southern edge of town —
for £1**, on condition that the club delivered the **A33 relief road** and
enabled hotel and retail development alongside it, with a rugby groundshare.
The stadium opened on 22 August 1998 as the Madejski Stadium, and has carried a
sponsor's name since July 2021.

It is worth putting the two transactions next to each other. The ground came to
the club for a pound and a road; twenty years later it left the club for
£26.5m and came back for a lease at £1.5m a year; and it returned to the club
only in 2025, with a new owner. A football ground can be a home, an asset, or a
lever, and this one has been all three inside a single generation.
