## Origins

Millwall Rovers was formed in 1885 by workers at J.T. Morton's canning and food-preserving factory on the Isle of Dogs — commonly remembered as a "jam factory," though jam was never the company's main product. The club's first secretary, 17-year-old Jasper Sexton, was the son of the landlord of the pub where the founders met and held their early changing rooms; a significant number of the founding workforce had come south from Aberdeen and Dundee with Morton's own Scottish operations, a lineage some historians connect to the club's original blue-and-white colours and to the lion drawn from the Scottish royal standard, though that connection is treated by at least one club historian as itself a myth worth scrutinising rather than settled fact.

The club left the Isle of Dogs for New Cross in 1910, and the move was forced rather than chosen: the Millwall Dock Company reclaimed the club's East Ferry Road ground as a timber yard roughly eleven years into a fourteen-year lease. The eviction coincided with a commercial reality — the Isle of Dogs was a place people worked rather than lived, a poor base for a paying crowd — and the club played its last match there on 10 October 1910 before opening at Cold Blow Lane, New Cross, twelve days later. The "Millwall" name, and the club's original East London identity, has stayed tied to a birthplace it left behind more than a century ago.

"The Lions" nickname traces to the 1899-1900 FA Cup run, when Millwall Athletic beat the reigning Football League leaders, Aston Villa, in a quarter-final replay and reached the semi-final — a run the press dubbed the "Lions of the South." The lion itself didn't appear on the club crest until the 1930s, decades after the name had already stuck. The club's wider national identity, "No One Likes Us, We Don't Care," followed a different and more gradual path: it emerged from the terraces in the late 1970s, set to the tune of Rod Stewart's 1975 hit "Sailing," as a direct response to a period of hostile press coverage that had cast Millwall as English football's foremost hooligan club. Academic treatment of the period, notably Garry Robson's study *No One Likes Us, We Don't Care: The Myth and Reality of Millwall Fandom*, frames it as self-mythologising terrace culture answering back to sustained media narrative rather than a chant tied to any single dated incident — organic reclamation rather than a Brighton-style one-off origin story.

## Trajectory

Millwall's reputation for crowd trouble is real and well documented, most concretely in the record of the Old Den itself: the ground was closed by the Football Association five times because of it, more than any other English club. The clearest single flashpoint came on 13 March 1985, an FA Cup sixth-round tie at Luton Town that ended in a pitch invasion and serious violence — 47 people injured, including 33 police officers, 31 arrests, a knife thrown at the Luton goalkeeper, and around £60,000 combined damage to the stadium and a return train. Luton responded by banning away fans for four seasons and introducing a membership-card scheme, and the incident fed a wider political reckoning that year, alongside the Bradford City fire and the Heysel disaster, that saw Margaret Thatcher's government convene a "War Cabinet" on football violence. It is accurate to call Millwall-Luton a significant contributor to that mid-1980s climate; it would overstate the case to call it a direct cause of the Taylor Report, which followed the Hillsborough disaster four years later and addressed a different failure — crowd safety, not fan violence — even though both threads fed into the same era of stadium reform.

Set against that reputation, Millwall's financial history is comparatively unremarkable: a brief period of administration in 1996-97, following mounting debt and a poor run of results, is the club's one real financial crisis, and it has never carried an EFL points deduction.

## Ownership & Finance

American businessman John Berylson, founder of Chestnut Hill Ventures, became Millwall's chairman in October 2007 and remained in the role for just over fifteen years, investing heavily in the club across that period. He died on 4 July 2023 in a car accident in Falmouth, Massachusetts. His son James Berylson took over as chairman within days, committing to continue his father's ownership, and the club has stayed in family hands since, held through Millwall Holdings; a road near the Den was renamed in John Berylson's memory the following year.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The Old Den, on Cold Blow Lane in New Cross, was Millwall's home for 83 years from 1910 to 1993 and earned its reputation as one of English football's most intimidating away grounds partly through that FA record of five FA-ordered closures. The New Den, which replaced it in 1993, was the first stadium built for an English club after the Taylor Report's all-seater requirement and the first new football ground built in London since 1937; it cost around £16m and holds 20,146.

The ground's tenure was seriously threatened decades later by a dispute with Lewisham Council. In 2012 the council gave developer Renewal outline planning consent for a regeneration scheme around the Den, and in September 2016 its cabinet voted to compulsorily purchase land the club used for parking and its Community Trust, for transfer to Renewal. The dispute escalated when Renewal's associated charity claimed a £2m funding pledge from Sport England toward the scheme that Sport England said had never existed, triggering a Charity Commission investigation and an independent council review; the council abandoned the compulsory purchase order in January 2017 after a sustained public campaign by the Association of Millwall Supporters. Wrangling over the underlying land continued for several more years before Lewisham agreed in May 2024 to convert the club's lease into a 999-year term — turning what began as a genuine threat to the ground's future into the closest thing to permanent tenure English football allows.
