## Origins

Stoke City's badge says 1863. The best available evidence says 1868, and the
gap between those two numbers is a small masterclass in how institutions
acquire their own history.

The traditional account has four former Charterhouse pupils, apprenticed to
the North Staffordshire Railway, forming Stoke Ramblers in 1863. Archival
work by Martyn Cooke, published in *Soccer & Society* in 2020, checked it
against the records. Charterhouse has no pupil of one of the names given. Of
the two who did attend, one would have been twelve in 1863 and the other
thirteen, and neither had left school. The earliest contemporary reference to
a football club in Stoke is in *The Field* in September 1868, reporting that
a new club had been formed "under the charge of H. J. Almond" — who arrived
in the town that year as a civil engineer with the railway. The first
recorded Stoke Ramblers match was in October 1868.

The interesting part is how the date moved. Local publications in 1870 said
1868. By the 1880s newspapers were saying 1865. *The Book of Football*
printed 1863 in 1905, and it has been repeated ever since. **The club got
older as it aged.** In 2026 supporters voted by 68% to 32% for a new crest,
and the new crest carries 1863.

This page states the evidence rather than picking a side, but the honest
summary is that Stoke's claim to be the second-oldest professional club in
England, or the oldest in the Football League, rests on a date that first
appears in print four decades after the fact.

The name followed the town's status: **Stoke Football Club became Stoke City
in 1925**, when Stoke-on-Trent was granted city status. "The Potters" is the
ceramics industry, and the badge makes it literal with a bottle kiln beside
the Staffordshire knot. That industry is now largely gone — the city lost
more than sixty per cent of its employment base in the late twentieth
century, and pottery firms were still closing in 2025. The nickname is a
memorial, in a city where the largest private employer is the online
bookmaker that owns the football club.

## Trajectory

A founder member of the Football League in 1888, Stoke finished bottom in
each of the first two seasons and **were voted out of it in 1890**. They won
the Football Alliance, were voted back in 1891, and have never quite escaped
the sense that their membership is conditional.

It happened again, worse, in 1908. Relegated the year before and with gates
collapsing, the club was **put into liquidation and resigned from the League**.
Twelve local businessmen raised the money to buy the club and its assets and
re-founded it as *Stoke Football Club (1908) Limited*. They then failed to
get back in — the re-election ballot went to Tottenham — and spent eleven
years in the Birmingham & District and Southern Leagues before returning in
1919. A founder member of the Football League spent 1908 to 1919 outside it,
as a different company.

The modern collapse was 1984/85, and its causes were on the balance sheet.
The club was reported to be around half a million pounds in debt; eight
players left during the season; the manager, carrying the roles of manager,
coach and scout at once, was ordered to rest for exhaustion and then sacked
in April. Paul Bracewell had been sold to Sunderland for £250,000 the summer
before and was resold to Everton for £425,000, winning the league and a
European trophy in the season Stoke recorded this:

> **Played 42, won 3, drawn 8, lost 31. Scored 24, conceded 91. 17 points,
> 22nd of 22.**

It was the lowest top-flight total of the three-points-for-a-win era, and it
stood for twenty-one years until Sunderland's 15 in 2005/06; Derby's 11 in
2007/08 is now the record. Stoke did not return to the first division for
**twenty-three seasons**.

## Ownership & Finance

Peter Coates, a local man who had made money in catering at football grounds
and then in betting shops, was majority shareholder from 1986 and chairman
from that September. In November 1999 he **sold seventy per cent to Stoke
Holding SA**, a Luxembourg-registered vehicle fronted by Icelandic
businessmen, for **£3.5m**. In May 2006 the same shares came back to him for
**£1.7m**. Seven years of foreign ownership, and the sellers took a nominal
loss of £1.8m on the way out.

What came next was bet365, founded in Stoke-on-Trent in 2000 by Coates's
daughter Denise and run from a portakabin before it became one of the largest
gambling companies in the world. The ground was renamed for it in 2016 under
an agreement between the club and its own parent — a related-party
transaction, and worth naming as one.

The structural change came in **August 2024, when bet365 demerged the club**.
John Coates became outright owner; **£90.5m of inter-company loans were
waived**, leaving the club debt-free; and ownership of the stadium and the
Clayton Wood training ground passed to the football club itself. The stated
reason was that separation was the most sustainable way for bet365 to
continue expanding internationally — a licensing decision, in other words,
that happened to hand a Championship club its own freehold.

The accounts need reading carefully because of it. Stoke's 2024/25 filing
shows a profit of £60.8m; that is the loan waiver. **Excluding it, the loss
widened from £25.7m to £29.7m.** The wage bill exceeded total revenue in
2023/24 — £34.4m against £32.3m, about 107% — and was cut to roughly 94% of
revenue the following year. This is a club whose income is Championship-sized
and whose costs have not been.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Stoke played at the Victoria Ground from **March 1878 to May 1997** — a
hundred and nineteen years, and at the end the oldest ground still in use in
the Football League. It was demolished a month after the final match.

Then nothing happened, for a very long time. The site was acquired by a
developer as part of the relocation; permission for housing came in 2007 and
was stalled by the recession and by complications with the ground itself.
**The site lay empty for more than twenty years.** Building finally began in
2019, and the estate that stands there now is called Victoria Park, with two
hundred homes and streets named after former players and managers. Two
decades of weeds where the oldest ground in the League had been, and then a
housing development that put the names back.

The Britannia Stadium cost around £14.7m and was paid for jointly — the club,
the city council, and a £4.5m Football Trust grant, with a £1m naming-rights
deal from a building society. Sir Stanley Matthews opened it in 1997 and his
ashes were interred beneath the centre circle in 2000. Ownership stayed mixed
until 2007, when the club bought out the council and the regeneration company
for around £6m, and moved again in the 2024 demerger. Public money and a
building society built it; the club bought out the council; a betting
company's restructuring finally handed over the deeds.

The other thing Stoke is known for is a song. "Delilah" has been sung at the
ground since some point in the 1970s or 1980s — two participants tell
different origin stories and neither is a record — and the club has kept it
through the period in which the Welsh Rugby Union removed it from official
playlists in 2015 and banned choirs from performing it in 2023, on the
grounds of what the lyrics describe.
