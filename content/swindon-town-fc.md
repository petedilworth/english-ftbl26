## Origins

Swindon was made by the Great Western Railway. The company's directors
authorised a works there in February 1841, it opened in January 1843, and a
village of about two thousand people became a railway town of fifty thousand —
GWR building not just the factory but the terraces around it, along with a gas
works, a fire brigade, a hospital, schools, a reading room and a market hall.
Old and New Swindon were separate places until they merged into one borough in
1900. **The works closed in 1986.**

The football club came out of that town, and the distinction this site draws
elsewhere applies here too: **Swindon Town was not a works team**. It was
convened in the autumn of 1879 by the **Rev. William Pitt** at a meeting in
King William Street School, with workers from the railway factory among the
founders. A clergyman gathering the men the works employed is not the same
thing as the company founding a club, and Swindon took the town's name rather
than the works' from the start. Pitt left for a living at Liddington in 1881
and severed his ties.

The name settled slowly — Swindon AFC, then Spartans after amalgamating with a
cricket club of that name, then a merger with a young men's friendly society
in 1881, and Swindon Town from 1883. Even the founding year is not fixed: the
club has adopted 1879, reverted, and adopted it again, and the evidence for it
is partly an after-dinner speech Pitt gave in 1911 saying he had "formed the
present club back in 1879."

The railway is in the badge and not in the nickname. The crest carries a steam
locomotive and the town motto, *Salubritas et Industria*. The club are **the
Robins**, not the Railwaymen.

## Trajectory

**Swindon Town won promotion to the First Division on 28 May 1990 and were not
promoted.**

They beat Sunderland 1–0 in the Second Division play-off final at Wembley
through Alan McLoughlin, under Ossie Ardiles. It would have been the club's
first season in the top flight. Ten days later, on **7 June 1990, Swindon
pleaded guilty at a Football League hearing to thirty-six breaches of its
rules, thirty-five of them illegal payments to players** made between 1985 and
1989 — cash outside contracts, without the League's knowledge and without tax
or National Insurance deducted. The League denied the promotion **and demoted
the club two divisions, to the Third**.

The sanction was reduced on appeal, and the shape of the reduction is the part
most retellings get wrong. **The demotion was cut from two divisions to one,
which meant Swindon stayed exactly where they had started: the Second
Division.** This site's own records confirm it — Swindon appear in the second
tier in 1989/90 and again in 1990/91. Nothing was taken below their starting
point. What survived the appeal was the withdrawal of the thing they had won
on the pitch. **Sunderland, beaten at Wembley, went up in their place**, over
the protests of Newcastle, who had finished third and lost the play-off
semi-final to them.

Two further precisions, because the cases get merged. **The betting case was
the FA's and it was separate and earlier**: chairman Brian Hillier bet £6,500
on Swindon *to lose* an FA Cup tie at Newcastle in January 1988, and they lost
5–0. He was suspended, the club fined £7,500, and **Lou Macari fined £1,000**
for what the FA called a minor role. And **the criminal case ended in
acquittal for Macari**: at Winchester Crown Court in July 1992, Hillier and
the club's accountant were convicted over the payments scheme and Macari was
cleared. He had left for West Ham in July 1989, so the side punished in June
1990 was not the one that had benefited.

There is an earlier denial that pairs with it. **In 1969 Swindon won the
League Cup as a Third Division club**, beating Arsenal 3–1 at Wembley — and
were barred from the European place it carried, because the entry criteria
agreed with the Fairs Cup organisers restricted it to First Division winners.
The Anglo-Italian competitions were created partly in response, and Swindon
won the 1970 Anglo-Italian Cup.

The top flight came eventually, through the play-offs in 1993, and lasted one
season. **In 1993/94 Swindon finished bottom of the Premier League and
conceded 100 goals** — five wins, four clean sheets, no league win until the
seventeenth game. That was the Premier League record for thirty years, until
Sheffield United conceded 104 in 2023/24, and Swindon's came in a **42-game
season** rather than 38, which cuts both ways.

One further title needs its asterisk removed. **Swindon won League Two in
2019/20**, and they won it on **points per game** after the season was
curtailed — level on 69 points with Crewe but from a game fewer. This site
ranks curtailed divisions the way they were actually settled, so its table
says what the EFL's did.

## Ownership & Finance

Swindon have been in administration twice, in **January 2000** with debts of
£4m and losses of £25,000 a week, and again in **2002** — reportedly the first
English club to do it twice. The second is documented at Companies House by a
company voluntary arrangement **approved on 24 May 2002 and not completed
until 26 May 2010**: an eight-year workout, during which a missed £900,000
instalment in 2007 brought a threatened fifteen-point deduction.

The modern crisis was not insolvency but ownership, and it was settled in
court. **Lee Power** bought the club in 2013 and held it through a holding
company in which he was the legal owner of 85%. **Michael Standing**, a
football agent, claimed Power held half of those shares on trust for him on
the strength of a verbal agreement and around £3.8m of investment; Power's
position was that the money was a loan. **Clem Morfuni's Axis Football
Investments** held the other 15%.

In May 2020 the High Court granted interim injunctions blocking any sale of
the club's shares or assets without the claimants' consent, at a point where
Power told the court he could no longer fund the losses and had to sell or
enter administration. Then, in **June 2021, the court held that Axis had
validly served an acceptance notice over all of Power's shares**, obliging him
to sell to Morfuni. The mechanism is the memorable part: **Power's own attempt
to sell had triggered the pre-emption provisions in the club's articles**, and
produced precisely the outcome the litigation had been fought to avoid.

The summer it took to complete was the closest the club came to failing. June
2021 wages went unpaid; Swindon Borough Council began legal action over
County Ground rent, none of which had been paid since April 2020; a transfer
embargo was in force over defaults to HMRC; and **the EFL and the PFA
intervened to get players and staff sixty per cent of their delayed wages**.
The EFL later agreed a **three-point deduction, suspended** — it was never
activated, so it is not a deduction and this page does not record one.

A separate FA case ran to 2024 over the regulations barring agents from owning
clubs. Standing was suspended for six months and his company fined £40,000;
the club was fined £25,000, half suspended; **Power was cleared**, and the FA
lost its appeal against that.

Morfuni has owned the club since July 2021. By 2025/26 the supporters' trust
was publicly calling on him to sell and a separate group was organising
marches to force a public listing of the club; he has said he does not fully
agree with the concerns.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Swindon Town do not own the County Ground, and neither does the council any
more.**

Swindon Borough Council owned the land from 1927 and leased the ground to the
club — a relationship that produced the unpaid-rent litigation of 2021 — and
that arrangement ended on **24 March 2023**, when the ground was bought for
**£2.3m**. The buyer is the interesting part. It is **The County Ground
Stadium Custodians Ltd, a fifty-fifty joint venture between the football club
and the supporters' trust**, funded by a trust established by a lifelong
supporter who died in 2012, and it leases the ground back to the club for
**250 years**.

That structure matters for exactly the reason the previous decade
demonstrated. The ground now sits outside the club's own corporate structure,
half-owned by its supporters, and cannot be sold, charged or lost with the
club if an owner's finances fail again. Very few English clubs have arranged
their affairs that way, and Swindon did it having just watched what happens
when a club's fate depends entirely on who holds its shares.

The ground itself has been the problem since. The **Stratton Bank end was
closed on safety grounds in December 2023 and stayed shut for two years**,
reopening in December 2025 after a target date was missed and further remedial
work proved necessary. A supporter consultation on rebuilding the main stand
opened in 2025. The £22.5m of improvements announced alongside the 2023
purchase belong to 2023, and should be read as a plan of that date rather than
a description of what has happened.
