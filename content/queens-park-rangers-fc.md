## Origins

The club's official founding date is 1882 and its own name is a place it
almost immediately left, which is a fair summary of Queens Park Rangers.

The 1882 belongs to **Christchurch Rangers**; the other parent, **St Jude's
Institute**, is dated to 1884 in most accounts; and the **merger that actually
created the club was in 1886**. QPR backdate themselves to the older half. The
name came from the **Queen's Park district** of north-west London, where most
of the players lived — not from any ground, and the club has never played
there.

The merger produced a schism almost at once. When the combined side initially
played under the St Jude's name, the Christ Church men said it was a takeover
rather than a merger and walked out, re-forming as Paddington. In 1892 QPR
adopted hoops for entry to the West London League, and the first hooped shirt
was worn against Paddington — the estranged half of their own founding. Those
hoops were **green and white**, and stayed green and white for thirty-four
years; **blue and white arrived in 1926-27**. The club is known for a shirt it
did not wear for its first four decades. There is a story that green was
dropped for being unlucky; it appears only in later kit writing and is best
treated as folklore.

Then the moving. QPR have used more home grounds than almost any English
professional club — Welford's Fields, the London Scottish ground, the Gun
Club, Kilburn Cricket Ground, Kensal Rise twice, Latimer Road, the Royal
Agricultural Society ground, Park Royal, Loftus Road, White City, back to
Loftus Road, White City again, and back again. Counts of how many range from
a dozen to twenty depending on whether returns and temporary venues are
included. The cause is simple and runs through everything else on this page:
QPR were a **tenant club**, never a landowning one. They only got into Loftus
Road in 1917 because Shepherd's Bush FC folded and left it empty. Both spells
at White City were attempts to trade up to the 1908 Olympic stadium, and both
times they came back because the crowds could not fill it.

## Trajectory

QPR were the **first Football League club to install an artificial pitch**,
and the reason was not the one usually given. The grass at Loftus Road had
been ruined by the winters and dry summers of the late 1970s and turned to
mud every year; the £300,000 Omniturf surface, laid in summer 1981 after a
fact-finding trip to the United States, was a response to a pitch that would
not grow. Multi-use revenue was the secondary argument, not the first. The
first Football League match on an artificial surface was QPR against Luton on
1 September 1981. The FA licensed FA Cup ties on it a year at a time rather
than accepting it, and QPR at one point threatened to withdraw from the
competition. It came up at the end of 1987-88, seven seasons in — first to lay
one, first to take one out. Luton, Oldham and Preston followed them onto
plastic; the top flight barred it from 1991.

The financial history has two near-death moments and they rhyme. In April
2001 Loftus Road plc was placed in administrative receivership by its own
largest creditor and shareholder, losses running at £570,000 a month. The
rescue was a **£10m loan from a Panama-registered company, secured on the
stadium**, at a rate reported between ten and just under twelve per cent —
roughly £1m a year in interest against a turnover then in the low teens of
millions. It was still undischarged in 2008. The administration was
discharged by the Chancery Division in May 2002.

The second was regulatory. QPR won promotion in 2013-14 with a wage bill
**above £75m against turnover of £38.6m — around 195%** — in the second tier.
The Football League treated a £60m owner injection as an exceptional item
rather than income, assessed the loss at £69.7m, and fined the club. An
independent arbitration panel upheld it in October 2017, rejecting arguments
that the penalty was disproportionate and that the rules breached competition
law. The 2018 settlement is usually reported as £17m and that is only part of
it: **£17m in cash payable over ten years, £3m toward the League's costs, and
£21.965m achieved by converting shareholder loans into equity** — a
balance-sheet conversion, not a payment. A transfer embargo followed. The
cash fine is still being paid.

There was an earlier regulatory outcome in the same shape. In 2011 the FA
charged QPR over third-party ownership and agents in the signing of Alejandro
Faurlin; two charges were found proven, and the commission imposed **£875,000
in fines and no points deduction**, finding the club had not acted in bad
faith. QPR went up that season too.

## Ownership & Finance

The ownership sequence runs: Jim Gregory to **Marler Estates** in 1987 for
£5,865,510; a twenty-four-year-old chairman in Richard Thompson from 1988;
**Chris Wright** in 1996, floating Loftus Road plc on AIM and bolting Wasps
rugby club onto it; receivership in 2001; **Flavio Briatore and Bernie
Ecclestone** buying 69% in 2007, with the Mittal family taking 20% that
December; **Tony Fernandes** buying 66% for £35m in 2011; and Fernandes
disposing of his entire holding in 2023, leaving Ruben Gnanalingam and the
Bhatia/Mittal interest.

One caution on the 2007 figure: the headline was £14m, but the shares
themselves cost about £690,000 between them. The rest was debt.

The most telling recent item is a bond. Having nearly been killed in 2001 by a
secured loan at around ten per cent, QPR now finance themselves partly by
**selling bonds to their own supporters** — the 2021 issue funded a £20m
training complex at Heston, paying five per cent gross plus three per cent in
club credit, with a bonus if the club reached the Premier League within five
years, minimum subscription £500. A second issue followed in 2026.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Loftus Road holds 18,439, which made it one of the smallest grounds used in
the Premier League during QPR's seven seasons there. The club has monetised
it by letting other people use it: **Wasps** shared it from 1996 to 2002, and
when Wasps left, **Fulham** rented it for two seasons while Craven Cottage
was rebuilt — the club QPR had nearly been merged with, now paying rent.

The attempts to leave have all failed in the same way. A 40,000-seat stadium
at **Old Oak Common**, announced in 2013 as part of a regeneration scheme,
stalled because the landowner would not sell and was shelved after
relegation. A proposal to demolish the **Linford Christie Stadium** in
Wormwood Scrubs and build around 30,000 seats, described by the club as a
last chance to stay in the borough, was abandoned. The current position is a
scheme to rebuild on the Loftus Road site itself; nothing is approved,
funded or under construction, and the only confirmed work expands rail
seating without adding capacity.

The one genuinely unusual thing the club has done with the ground is give
away its name. From 2019 it was **The Kiyan Prince Foundation Stadium** — the
naming rights donated to an anti-knife-crime charity founded by the father of
a QPR academy player stabbed outside his school in 2006, aged fifteen.
Supporters chose it in a vote, with the foundation taking over sixty-three per
cent. The rights reverted in 2022 and were sold commercially in 2023. For
three seasons a football club with a Panamanian loan in its past and a
£17m fine in its present had a ground named after a dead schoolboy, and had
not been paid a penny for it.
