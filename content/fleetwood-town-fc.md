## Origins

Fleetwood Town is the fourth football club the town has had, and the second
phoenix. The sequence matters because the club's own founding date points at a
different club from the one that exists.

**Fleetwood Rangers** played professionally in the 1880s and 1890s and
dissolved. **Fleetwood Amateur** was founded in **1899** specifically to bring
association football back afterwards, dropped "Amateur" in **1908** to become
plain **Fleetwood FC**, won the Lancashire Combination in 1923/24, was a
founder member of the Northern Premier League in 1968 — and **folded in 1976,
under a winding-up order over unpaid tax.** A **Fleetwood Town** was formed in
**1977** to replace it, reached the FA Vase final at Wembley in 1985, and
**folded in 1996** after continued financial trouble. The present club was
formed in **1997 as Fleetwood Wanderers**, became **Fleetwood Freeport after
two matches** under a sponsorship deal, and took the name Fleetwood Town in
2002.

The club presents itself as the fourth incarnation of a club originally formed
in 1908. Three dates are therefore in circulation and each is defensible about
something different: **1997 is the entity, 1908 is the claim, and 1899 is the
actual founding of the club the 1908 claim points at** — 1908 being a renaming,
not a founding. This site records phoenix clubs by the entity, so the date at
the top of this page is 1997.

**The Cod Army** is the town's fishing industry, and the ordering is worth
noticing. Fleetwood was a planned Victorian town, laid out from 1836 on a
radial fan by Decimus Burton for the landowner whose name it carries, and
became England's third-largest fishing port by the 1930s, with around a hundred
and twenty trawlers in the 1950s. **The 1976 Cod Wars settlement removed the
North Atlantic grounds the deep-sea fleet depended on, and the last deep-sea
trawler left Fleetwood in 1982.** The present club was founded fifteen years
after that. The nickname is heritage rather than survival, and "the Fishermen"
is described as the older name with the Cod Army a supporters' coinage.

One coincidence is documented and worth stating as exactly that. **Fleetwood FC
was wound up in 1976, the year of the Cod Wars settlement.** Kit and local
history sources note the two together; nobody has established causation, and
the winding-up order was over tax.

## Trajectory

Fleetwood climbed from the tenth tier to the third in fifteen seasons, and this
site's data only picks them up for the last part of it. **The standings begin
in 2011, at the fifth tier**, so the four promotions before that rest on
external sources rather than on anything here:

- **1998/99** — champions of the North West Counties League's second division;
- **2004/05** — champions of its first division;
- **2005/06** — runners-up in the Northern Premier League's first division;
- **2007/08** — champions of the Northern Premier League;
- **2009/10** — up from Conference North through the play-offs.

Then the two that are in the data. **2011/12: champions of the Conference on
103 points**, five clear of Wrexham, and into the Football League for the first
time in the club's history. **2013/14: fourth in League Two and up through the
play-offs**, beating Burton Albion 1–0 at Wembley.

Nine consecutive seasons in League One followed, from 2014/15 to 2023/24, and
ended with **relegation on the last day of 2023/24** — twenty-second on 43
points, staying above Port Vale on goal difference right up until they did not.
Two mid-table League Two seasons since.

**They have never had a point deducted**, and there has been no insolvency
event at the present club. There was an EFL **transfer embargo in 2021**, for
not fully repaying a loan taken on top of the previous season's grant aid,
which capped incoming wages at £2,500 a week — a restriction, not a sanction on
the table.

## Ownership & Finance

**The man who funded Fleetwood's rise was convicted of a £15m fraud and jailed
for thirteen years, and the club passed to his son.**

**Andy Pilley** became chairman in 2003 and held the club for around twenty
years. His money came from energy supply — BES Utilities and associated
companies, based in Fleetwood — and those companies also sponsored the shirt.
The club was structurally loss-making and openly owner-dependent: accounts
showing losses of about **£6m** carried a going-concern note recording that
directors' loans would not be recalled until the company could repay them,
which is the documentable form of the dependency.

**At Preston Crown Court in May 2023 Pilley was convicted on four counts** — two
of fraudulent trading, one of fraud by false representation, and one of being
concerned in the acquisition, retention, use or control of criminal property.
The conduct was between 2014 and 2016, and it had nothing to do with the
football club: through a web of interconnected energy companies he and others
targeted small businesses and charities — guest houses, children's charities,
disability support providers — as they moved premises or came up for renewal,
and deceived them into long-term contracts. The total was around **£15m**. He
was sentenced in early July 2023 to **thirteen years' imprisonment and a
thirteen-year director disqualification**, with a **£11.6m confiscation order
and £4.3m in costs**. Nine sales agents were separately sentenced. **The Court
of Appeal dismissed his application on 25 February 2026**, holding there was no
arguable basis for it: the prosecution case rested on extensive documentary
evidence which he had been able to engage with.

What happened to the club is a distinct question, and the answer is that it
survived and stayed in the family.

Pilley resigned as chairman and director on conviction and announced the club
was for sale. **No third party bought it.** The chief executive ran it as
interim chairman through 2023/24, and in **May 2024 the 98% shareholding moved
to a new company whose sole shareholder is Jamie Pilley, the convicted owner's
son.** The EFL cleared the transfer under its Owners' and Directors' Test
subject to conditions, including undisclosed financial requirements and the
appointment of the community trust's chairman to the board as an independent
director. The same handover happened at the Irish club Pilley had bought in
2022.

**The EFL did not merely wave it through.** Before the sale, Andrew Pilley had
directed that his son be appointed a director of both the club and its holding
company and had given him power of attorney carrying the club's voting rights.
Jamie Pilley was investigated for a breach of the Owners' and Directors' Test,
**a charge was accepted, and he was given a three-month suspended ban from
acting as a club director**, live until June 2025.

So: ownership changed hands and did not leave the family, the club dropped a
division and a wage bill, and there was no insolvency, no deduction and no
expulsion. Whether the shares themselves were caught by the confiscation order
is not established in the public record, and this page does not guess at it.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Fleetwood Town does not own Highbury Stadium — Wyre Borough Council does, and
the club pays rent.** That is the fact underneath the whole climb, because the
ground that got Fleetwood into the Football League was built up on land the
club does not own.

The redevelopment was explicitly a grading exercise, and it was done **ahead of
the promotion rather than after being refused one**. A three-phase plan
approved in 2007, budgeted at around £2m, built new terraces at both ends and
then a new main stand; contemporary coverage described the object plainly as
raising capacity to 5,000 **to meet Football League requirements**, at a point
when the club was still in the sixth tier. The Parkside Stand opened in April
2011 and took the ground to **5,327**. When the Conference title came the
following year, the ground already qualified.

This site's ground-grading theme collects clubs refused promotion for the state
of their stadium — Kidderminster, Macclesfield, Stevenage. **Fleetwood is the
counter-example: a club that saw the constraint coming and built for it.** The
qualification is that it built on somebody else's asset.

Separately, **Poolfoot Farm**, the training complex opened by Alex Ferguson in
April 2016 at a reported £8m, is a facility of a scale that League Two clubs do
not usually have — and, like everything else here, it was paid for out of the
energy business.
