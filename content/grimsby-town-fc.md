## Origins

Grimsby Town was founded in 1878 at the Wellington Arms on Freeman Street by
members of the Worsley Cricket Club, who wanted something to do once the
cricket season ended. It was called **Grimsby Pelham** — after the family name
of the Earls of Yarborough, the dominant landowners in the district, which
makes it one of the few English clubs named for a landlord rather than a
place, a trade or a church. Grimsby Town followed within a year or so.

They were a founder member of the Football League's Second Division in 1892,
and their early history contains an administrative exit and return that
belongs on a site like this: **Grimsby failed re-election in 1910, spent a
season in the Midland League and won it, and were readmitted in 1911 by a
single vote — at Lincoln City's expense.** The Lincolnshire argument described
below is a good deal older than the county boundaries usually blamed for it.

## Trajectory

**Grimsby Town's ground has been in Cleethorpes since 1899.** It is a
different town, and the club has never played in it.

The move had an off-pitch cause of the sort this site collects: the owner of
Abbey Park sold the ground for housing, so the club took its fixtures,
fittings and two of its stands two miles down the road. The claim usually
attached to this — that Blundell Park is the only Football League ground not
in the town its club is named after — does not survive contact with the map.
**Nottingham Forest's City Ground is in West Bridgford**, outside the
Nottingham boundary since a 1950s revision; Old Trafford is in Trafford, not
Manchester. What is unusual about Blundell Park is that the host settlement is
a **separate, separately named town**, and has been since 1899 by original
design rather than by a later boundary change. Since 1996 both places have sat
in the same unitary authority, so even that is a claim about civic identity
rather than administration.

The name is a landholding story in its own right. **Blundell Park is named
after Peter Blundell**, a sixteenth-century Tiverton clothier whose bequest
funded a Cambridge college's purchase of land on the Lincolnshire coast in
1616 — land whose later development as a seaside resort became Cleethorpes.

The rest of the trajectory is two departures from the Football League and two
returns. **Relegated in 2009/10**, twenty-third on 44 points, ending roughly a
century of membership; six seasons in the Conference including four
consecutive play-off campaigns, and up through the play-off final in 2016.
**Relegated again in 2020/21**, bottom on 43. And back at the first attempt in
2022 — **from sixth**, the lowest position from which anyone had won promotion
under the restructured National League play-offs.

Then the run that made them famous. **In 2022/23, as a fourth-tier club,
Grimsby reached the FA Cup quarter-finals for the first time since 1938/39**,
beating Plymouth, Cambridge and Burton from the division above, Luton from the
Championship, and **Southampton away from the Premier League**. Every one of
those five clubs was in a higher tier that season — this site's own standings
confirm it — and Grimsby were eleventh in League Two at the end of it. In
August 2025 they knocked Manchester United out of the League Cup on penalties
at Blundell Park.

**Grimsby have never had a point deducted, in any season in this site's
records, and have never entered administration.** The 2000s were bad enough —
around £2m of debt after the ITV Digital collapse and roughly £700,000 owed to
HMRC — but it was settled by a repayment schedule. A £20,000 fine in September
2025 for fielding a player whose registration was submitted one minute after
the deadline is the only regulatory sanction of note, and it was a fine, not a
deduction.

## Ownership & Finance

The fishing industry ran through this club without ever quite owning it.

Grimsby was the largest fishing port in the world in the late 1950s, and the
**Cod Wars settlement of 2 June 1976** — Britain limited to twenty-four
trawlers inside Iceland's 200-mile limit and a 50,000-ton catch, fifteen
thousand tons less than Iceland had offered at the outset — removed the
distant-water grounds the fleet had been built around. Roughly four hundred
trawlers were based at the port in 1970; five by 2013. Contemporary job-loss
estimates ranged from nine thousand to a hundred thousand and both were
advocacy numbers; the hard figure is that **more than five thousand former
trawlermen were eventually paid compensation** by the government decades
later.

What the record will not support is the tidy version in which the port's
collapse emptied Blundell Park. Nobody has published the attendance-and-
employment series that would make it causal, and the club's record crowd
(1937) and highest finish (fifth in the First Division, 1934/35) both come
*before* the port's peak. The two ran alongside each other, and the football
club is what the town has left of its public institutions.

The documented links are commercial and personal, and they are good ones.
**Young's Seafood, a Grimsby processor, sponsored the shirt for eighteen
consecutive seasons** from 2004 to 2022 — one of the longest corporate
sponsorships in British football, and for one season the away shirt carried
the logo of a product called Mariners Pie. And **John Fenty, who controlled the
club for around two decades, made his money in Grimsby fish processing**,
selling a business he had built from nothing for £20m in the same year he
became chairman. The industry did not sponsor the club so much as fund the man
who ran it.

Fenty's departure took years. A consortium came close in 2019; a deal
collapsed on Boxing Day 2020 after a Christmas Eve deadline passed; and
**Jason Stockwood and Andrew Pettit, who had been part of that consortium,
completed the purchase in May 2021 — the week the club was relegated.**

Their stated approach is unusually explicit. Pettit framed the investment
around passion, philanthropy and pride and described the aim as developing the
club "as a community asset which can be a significant catalyst for the
regeneration of the area." Stockwood: *"We see ourselves as part of the
community, being called owners feels a little bit odd"*, and *"It's not a
financial investment, it's not a money-making machine."* On the longer term:
*"I think if we can create financial sustainability, the logical step would be
to get clubs into fan ownership."* The **Mariners Trust holds about 13.5%**
alongside the owners' 63%.

The accounts are the honest counterweight, and the club has said so itself. A
£245,000 profit in the FA Cup year gave way to a **£1.68m loss in 2023/24**
on turnover down to £5.72m, with net liabilities of £2.3m — the club's own
framing being that the results "show how challenging it is for lower league
clubs to find a route to financial sustainability." The owners subsequently
converted £2.7m of debt into shares. Stockwood left the board in September
2025 on becoming a government minister and taking a seat in the Lords.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Blundell Park's **main stand dates from 1901 and is described as the oldest
stand in the Football League**, though only its central section is original.
The record crowd is 31,651, set against Wolverhampton Wanderers in the FA Cup
in 1937 — not to be confused with the 76,962 who watched Grimsby play Wolves
in a 1939 FA Cup semi-final at Old Trafford, which remains **Old Trafford's**
record and is routinely misattributed to this club.

The new stadium is the thing that has not happened, repeatedly. A site at
Great Coates failed to secure council backing. A £55m, 14,000-capacity scheme
at Peaks Parkway with an ice rink, retail and up to 1,600 houses was **taken
off the table by the council leader in 2016**, against resident opposition and
a council preference for siting a stadium in the East Marsh to drive
regeneration there instead. The fishing docks were looked at, then Freeman
Street, then East Marsh again. The record on where it stands now is
contradictory — commitments to improve Blundell Park in one place, a revived
East Marsh scheme in another — and this page will say only what is reliably
true: **announced repeatedly, relocated repeatedly, never built.** The club is
still in Cleethorpes.
