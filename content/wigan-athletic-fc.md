## Origins

Wigan Athletic was founded in 1932, which makes it one of the youngest clubs
ever to reach the English top division — and it was the town's fifth attempt.

**Wigan County** came first, formed in May 1897, the original tenants of
Springfield Park, and liquidated within three years. **Wigan United** took the
lease in 1901 and lasted two seasons in the Lancashire League. **Wigan Town**
ran from 1905 to 1908 and folded, the record says, because it could not pull
crowds away from the rugby club — the earliest documented instance of the thing
that has shaped this club's whole existence. **Wigan Borough**, formed in
December 1920, was a founder member of Football League Division Three North in
1921 and the only one of the four to get that far.

Borough died of the Depression. Home gates fell to a few thousand — six hundred
for Gateshead in a snowstorm in January 1931 — and by the end of that season it
could not pay wages. Faced with a Football League ultimatum, **the directors
resigned from the competition in late October 1931** and put the club into
voluntary liquidation owing around £30,000. Twelve games of the 1931/32 season
had been played, and the record was struck out. This is worth distinguishing
from what happened to Leeds City in 1919: Borough were not expelled. They
chose to fold rather than be pushed.

Athletic was formed the following year at a public meeting in the Queen's Hall
chaired by the Mayor of Wigan. It inherited nothing legally — new club, new
company — and one thing physically: **Springfield Park, bought out of Borough's
liquidation for £2,850**. Its first application, to the Cheshire County League,
was rejected, and it got in only when Manchester Central resigned.

Then it spent forty-six years trying to join the Football League. Athletic
applied and failed **thirty-four times** by the club's own count, including an
application to the *Scottish* League Second Division in 1972, before being
elected at the League's AGM on **2 June 1978**. The first ballot tied, Wigan 26
and Southport 26; the second went 29–20. Southport were the last club ever
removed from the Football League by a re-election vote.

## Trajectory

Wigan Athletic did in twenty-seven years what the town had failed to do in
eighty: **Dave Whelan** bought the club in February 1995 in the fourth tier,
said the Premier League was the aim, and it arrived in 2005. Eight consecutive
top-flight seasons followed.

**In May 2013 the club won the FA Cup and was relegated from the Premier League
three days later** — beating Manchester City 1–0 at Wembley on the 11th, losing
4–1 at Arsenal on the 14th. No club had done both in the same season before.
The following season Wigan played in the Europa League group stage as a
second-tier club, which is a sentence with almost no precedent in English
football.

**Then the 2020 administration, which is the most consequential thing on this
page and remains unexplained.**

The sequence is short and it is the timing that does the damage. Hong
Kong-listed **International Entertainment Corporation** completed its purchase
of the club in November 2018. On **4 June 2020** it sold to **Next Leader Fund**,
a Cayman Islands vehicle incorporated about five months earlier, for £17.5m
plus repayment of a £24m loan — and **lent the buyer £28.77m to fund the
purchase of its own asset**, at 8% interest rising to 20% on default. IEC's
chairman and majority shareholder was disclosed to the Hong Kong exchange as
also being the intended majority shareholder of the buyer. In substance the
club moved between two vehicles connected to the same person, with the seller
financing the transaction.

On **23 June** the fund's minority shareholder spoke to insolvency
practitioners. On **24 June** he told the EFL he had acquired 51% and taken
control. On **1 July** administrators were appointed — twenty-seven days after
the takeover completed and seven after control changed hands. The
administrators' account is that he had made clear through his solicitors that
he would "not put another penny in the club," and no substantive business
reason was given. He later blamed the pandemic; the EFL's public response was
that evidence of the source and sufficiency of funding had been provided as
part of the change-of-control process weeks before.

**The deduction did not push Wigan over a line. It carried them across the
room.** Twelve points under the insolvency rule; because the event fell after
the March cut-off, the season it applied to could only be settled once the
final games were played. As recorded, Wigan finished 23rd of 24 on 47 points
and went down. **Restore the twelve and they finish thirteenth on 59** — twelve
points clear of the relegation places, in the middle of the table. An appeal
arguing the pandemic amounted to force majeure was dismissed in August 2020.

What happened next is what administration means in practice. The **Euxton
training ground, bought from Bolton Wanderers for about £2.2m in 2016, was sold
to Preston North End for £1.6m**; across assets previously valued at some £20m
the administrators realised £3.49m. A Spanish takeover was rejected by the EFL
in December 2020 over a disqualifying condition affecting a member of the
bidding team, and collapsed in January when the bid was cut by nearly half —
proceeding on those terms would have triggered a further fifteen-point
deduction. The club came out in March 2021 under Bahraini ownership.

One episode should be reported for exactly what it is and no more. The EFL's
chairman was filmed in an impromptu street conversation with a season-ticket
holder saying there were rumours of a bet placed in the Philippines on Wigan
being relegated, and that the previous owner had gambling interests there. The
EFL confirmed the recording was genuine and described the conversation as
private. **No investigation outcome, regulator finding or charge has ever been
published**, and nothing beyond the fact of the recording is established.

The 2021–23 ownership then produced deductions of its own, all for late wages
rather than insolvency: three points activated in March 2023, and **eight to
start 2023/24**, four of them for failing to lodge a deposit covering the wage
bill. Wigan were relegated in 2022/23 — though on the arithmetic the three
points would not have saved them.

## Ownership & Finance

Four owners in eight years, and the shape of each is worth stating plainly.

**Dave Whelan**, 1995–2018, funded the rise and built the stadium. **IEC**, a
listed Hong Kong company, bought the Whelan family's holding for £22m in a deal
agreed in October 2017 that took thirteen months to complete. **Next Leader
Fund**, June 2020, lasted twenty-seven days. **Phoenix 2021 Limited**, owned by
the Bahraini businessman **Abdulrahman Al-Jasmi**, ran the club from March 2021
to June 2023 — this, and not the current owner, is the ownership under which the
wage failures and the 2023 and 2024 deductions happened.

**Mike Danson**, a Wigan-born businessman, bought the club in June 2023 with
an HMRC winding-up petition over roughly £1m of unpaid tax listed for hearing
the following month and an EFL embargo running. The debts were paid on
completion and the petition never reached court. **There was only ever one
insolvency event at Wigan Athletic, in 2020.** In December 2023 Danson also
bought 100% of Wigan Warriors, so the same person now owns the football club,
the rugby league club and the operating interest in the ground the two share.

For a town whose football club died four times before this one, that is either
the most stable arrangement in its history or the most concentrated. It is
certainly both at once.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The stadium exists where it does because Wigan Athletic lost a bidding war to
a supermarket.

In January 1997 Whelan bid for **Central Park**, the rugby club's home since
1902, intending to redevelop it for both codes. Two months later the Warriors'
chairman accepted a rival bid from Tesco reported at roughly three times as
much. Central Park is now a Tesco and its car park, and the shared stadium was
built instead at Robin Park — opened in August 1999 at a cost of about £30m,
financed by Whelan and named the **JJB Stadium** after his sports retail chain.
Wigan Athletic left Springfield Park after sixty-seven years; the Warriors,
having considered ground-sharing in Bolton, moved in that September.

The names since have tracked the owner rather than the market. It became the
**DW Stadium** in 2009 not because a sponsor arrived but because Whelan's own
company changed its name. In **May 2024 it became the Brick Community
Stadium**, and the notable thing is that the naming rights went to **The Brick,
a local homelessness and poverty charity, rather than to a paying sponsor** —
an unusual decision, and one available only because both tenants now answer to
the same owner.

**Wigan Council owns the freehold**, and the head lease requires that two
sporting clubs be licensed to use the ground. The football club could not evict
the rugby club even if it wanted to, and the rugby club's tenancy was extended
by twenty-five years in 2007. In a town where football has died four times and
rugby league has not died once, the arrangement that keeps them in the same
building is written into the deeds.
