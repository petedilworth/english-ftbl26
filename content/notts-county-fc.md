## Origins

Notts County call themselves the oldest professional football club in the
world, and the claim needs its adjective. **Sheffield FC, founded in 1857, is
older, and FIFA recognises it as the world's oldest club** — but Sheffield was
founded amateur and stayed amateur. County's claim is to be the oldest of the
clubs that are professional, which is a claim about status rather than about
an event.

The evidence for 1862 is a newspaper report rather than a minute book. The
*Nottinghamshire Guardian* of 28 November 1862 records "the opening of the
Nottingham Football Club" at Cremourne Gardens in the Meadows, sides picked by
two named men, the result given as "two goals and two rouges against one" — a
scoring system that tells you this predates the Football Association, which
was not formed until the following year. A formal constitution followed at the
George IV Hotel in 1864.

This site has been sceptical of Stoke's 1863, and fairness requires applying
the same question here: whether the side playing in 1862 is continuous with
the club constituted in 1864. The difference is evidential rather than
logical. County have a contemporary printed source for 1862. Stoke have none
for 1863.

There is a cleaner formulation available, and it is the one this page prefers
where precision matters: **Notts County are the world's oldest Football League
club**, which can be checked against the founder list of 1888 rather than
against a definition.

**The black and white stripes went to Turin.** County adopted them around
1890, said to be after the racing colours of the Duke of Portland. Juventus
originally played in pink, and changed because the pink faded and could not be
replaced well locally. The intermediary was **Tom Gordon Savage**, a
Nottingham lace-trade businessman living in Turin, who joined Juventus in 1900
and wrote home; a shipment of black-and-white striped shirts followed.
Archival work by Roger Stirland and Luca Formara established Savage's
identity — he is often miscalled John Savage — and Juventus have themselves
published on him, which is unusually good corroboration for a kit story from
1901.

## Trajectory

**On 4 May 2019 Notts County became the first of the Football League's twelve
founder members to be relegated out of it**, losing 3–1 at Swindon on the
final day, twenty-third of twenty-four on 41 points with only Yeovil below
them. A hundred and thirty-one consecutive years of membership ended.

Two qualifications make that claim true rather than merely striking.

**Founder members had left before, but not this way.** Accrington resigned in
1893 rather than take a Second Division place; Stoke failed re-election in
1890 and resigned in 1908 after liquidation, spending seven seasons outside
before returning. Leaving and being relegated are different things.

**And the mechanism barely existed.** Automatic relegation out of the Football
League was only introduced for 1986/87, replacing the re-election ballot — a
change this site records on Lincoln City's page, Lincoln being the first club
it took. So County are the first founder member caught by a rule that had been
in force for thirty-two seasons.

The fall was fast. County finished **fifth in League Two in 2017/18 and were
in the play-offs**; one season later they were out of the League altogether.

Four National League seasons followed, and the third of them produced a
statistic worth stopping on. **In 2022/23 Notts County finished on 107
points — the highest total any club has recorded in any division in this
site's records without winning it.** Wrexham took the title on 111. County
went up through the play-offs instead, beating Chesterfield on penalties at
Wembley after a 2–2 draw, and both of those clubs have pages in this batch.
They came back to League One in 2026, through the play-offs again.

Across sixty-eight recorded seasons, **Notts County have never had a point
deducted** — through the 2002 administration, through the 2009 takeover
described below, and through the 2019 collapse. Twice near liquidation, never
once docked.

## Ownership & Finance

**In July 2009 Notts County were bought for £1 by a company that did not exist
in the way it said it did, and the Football League had checked.**

Munto Finance, a subsidiary of Qadbak Investments, was presented as a Middle
East consortium with Premier League ambitions. **Sven-Göran Eriksson** was
recruited as director of football and **Sol Campbell** signed a five-year
contract at a fourth-tier club. The League publicly confirmed that the new
owners had met its fit-and-proper-persons requirements and that it had seen
and approved extensive disclosure on the ownership structure.

The beneficial owners were never identified. The wealthy Middle Eastern
families Qadbak was said to be connected to denied any involvement. The
negotiator was **Russell King**, and the actual purpose of acquiring the club
was a scheme to list a fake mining company; King was later extradited from
Bahrain, charged in Jersey with twenty-five counts of fraud and larceny,
**sentenced to six years in 2019** and ordered to pay £320,000. Campbell played
one game. The backers withdrew by December.

The precise thing to say about the League is not that it failed to vet the
takeover. **It vetted the takeover, passed it, and said so publicly** — and
the owners were still never identified. The failure was of the test, not of
its application.

Ten years later the club came closer to ending. **Alan Hardy** bought it in
2017 with his own construction business funding the purchase, so the two sets
of finances were entangled from the start. In January 2019 he announced the
club was for sale; in March his company entered administration; HMRC issued a
winding-up petition over a missed payment that grew to around £800,000, and
the case was adjourned repeatedly, the club due back in the High Court for a
fourth time on 31 July facing liquidation. Staff and players were last paid in
May. The Lifeline membership scheme, which had supported the club for over
thirty years, gave £25,000 towards wages. Nottingham Forest's supporters held
a collection.

**Christoffer and Alexander Reedtz completed a takeover on 26 July 2019, five
days before the court date**, paid two months of overdue wages within three
days, and settled the tax debt in early August.

Their model is unusual enough to be worth setting out. The brothers own
**Football Radar**, a football data-analysis company whose business is
statistical analysis sold as betting advice, and the club said at takeover
that its data would be applied to the club as a matter of strategy. That is
not rhetoric: the head of analysis at Football Radar joined the board, and the
arrangement has since been formalised as a technical board on which the head
coach, his staff, the owners and the directors of football and performance
sit, with the coach holding final responsibility but explaining in-game
decisions at a post-match data review.

It is owner-funded. Losses ran to £1.7m in 2021/22 against a creditor bill
approaching £14m, and **in September 2025 the owners waived a £10.75m loan**
made to the club by Football Radar — producing a headline £9.7m profit that
is a roughly £1m underlying loss once the write-off is stripped out. Some
supporters describe the owners as secretive, which is a fair counterweight to
the rescue.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Meadow Lane opened in 1910 on land leased from Nottingham Corporation, and
**in 1920 the Corporation, as landlord, came close to removing the club from
the site to make way for an abattoir**. It is a reminder that the modern
pattern on this site — a club at the mercy of whoever owns the ground — is
not modern at all.

The lease has been an asset in its own right ever since. When the club came
out of administration in 2003, the buyer of a 49% stake bought **the Meadow
Lane lease along with it, for £3m**; a supporters' trust formed that year
raised £300,000 for a 30% shareholding and became majority owner when he sold
in 2007. Whether the club or the council holds the land today is not
established in the public record, and this page will not assert it.

The ground's most quoted feature is its neighbour. **Meadow Lane and the City
Ground are about three hundred yards apart, on opposite banks of the Trent** —
commonly described as the closest two professional grounds in England, and
second in the United Kingdom only to Dundee's pair. The proximity has been
used in practice: after a fire destroyed the City Ground's main stand during a
match in August 1968, **Nottingham Forest played six home fixtures at Meadow
Lane**.
