## Origins

Walsall was formed by a merger of two clubs that had been neighbours in the
most literal sense. **Walsall Town**, founded 1877, and **Walsall Swifts**,
founded in the 1870s as Victoria Swifts, had from 1881 played on **adjacent
pitches** at the Chuckery. In February 1888 they agreed to become **Walsall
Town Swifts** — the Swifts losing support, and Town having lost its secretary,
who had absconded with the club's books. The merged side first played that
April, drawing with Aston Villa. The name shortened to plain Walsall in the
1890s.

**The Saddlers** is the town's name before it is the club's. Walsall was the
centre of English saddlery: around 6,800 saddlers were working there in 1901,
and some ten thousand people in leather at the trade's peak. But the connection
is not purely civic, and the honest version has three parts. The **predecessor
club** is documented: Walsall Swifts was made up of workers in the town and
particularly in the leather industry, and played a full fixture list precisely
to generate income its players lacked from work. The **nickname** attaches to
the town's trade. And the **badge** only picked up leather imagery in the
1990s, when a design paired the swift with an animal hide.

## Trajectory

Walsall Town Swifts were founder members of the Football League's Second
Division in 1892, and then lost their place twice. **Voted out in 1895**, they
spent a season in the Midland League and were re-elected for 1896/97. **They
failed re-election again in 1901, polling seven votes**, and this time the
exile lasted twenty years — two seasons in the Midland League and then, from
1903, roughly two decades in the Birmingham & District League. They came back
as founder members of the Third Division North in 1921.

In January 1933 they beat Arsenal 2–0 at Fellows Park in the FA Cup third
round. Arsenal were top of the First Division and won the title that season,
and the aftermath was managerial: Herbert Chapman banned the player who had
conceded the penalty from Highbury and transferred him to Plymouth within
weeks.

The rest is a story about one division. **Walsall have never played in the top
flight**, have had seven seasons in the second tier, and have spent **forty-five
seasons in the third — more than any other club in this site's records**,
ahead of Bristol Rovers and Bournemouth on forty-one. Two-thirds of the modern
era in the same division is a kind of achievement.

They have **never had a point deducted**. Nor has the club itself been through
an insolvency process — the receivership of 1991 was of **Denglen Limited**, the
parent company that owned it, and that receivership is where the next section
starts.

## Ownership & Finance

**Walsall sold a ground it owned and moved into one it did not, and it took
thirty-two years to get back to where it started.**

The move is usually told as a Jeff Bonser story and the chronology says
otherwise. Plans to leave Fellows Park followed Terry Ramsden's takeover in
1986; a site at Bescot Crescent was identified in 1988; **Fellows Park was sold
to a supermarket chain for £5.75m**, which financed a **£4.5m** new stadium
built by a developer vehicle called Denglen Limited. Bescot opened in August
1990, and **the club was a tenant in it from the first day** — before Bonser
controlled anything. Denglen went into receivership in March 1991, and Bonser,
the only serious bidder, bought the club's **76% majority shareholding from the
receiver**. He inherited a separation; he did not create it.

What he then did with it is the part supporters objected to for a generation.
The land under the stadium had been retained by the water company that formerly
used the site, which charged the club around £75,000 a year. **In 1995 Bonser's
pension fund bought the freehold**, and the club began paying rent to its own
owner's pension. The rent started at about **£90,000** and reached **£440,000 a
year** by the end, rising by seven per cent of every investment made in the
ground — so improving the stadium increased the rent. The lease ran ninety-nine
years. The Guardian's David Conn reported in around 2011 that the pension fund
had by then taken £4.5m out of the club.

There is a flat contradiction on the record. In December 2009 Bonser said
"Walsall FC has never paid a penny into my pension fund." **In March 2011 he
acknowledged that the club paid rent to a self-invested pension fund owned by
him and his brother.**

Supporters organised. The trust applied in 2018 to have the stadium land listed
as an **Asset of Community Value**, which would give the community six months to
bid if it came to market — explicitly a safeguard rather than a bid. A petition
ran under the title "Reunite Walsall FC and Bescot Stadium." The ground went on
the open market in 2011 and did not sell.

**When Bonser sold the club in July 2019, the ground did not go with it.**
Leigh Pomlett bought the 76% shareholding and negotiated an **option to
purchase the freehold** at a later date. **Trivela Group**, of Birmingham,
Alabama, took 51% in June 2022 with a commitment to fund the exercise of that
option — and **on 16 December 2022 Walsall Football Club bought the freehold of
its own stadium**, funded by a mixture of American bank debt and Trivela
equity. Rent payments stopped that day.

Two precisions the shorthand loses. **Trivela did not buy the stadium** — the
football club did, exercising an option Pomlett had negotiated three years
earlier. And the price has never been disclosed; the £5m–£7m figure that
circulates is a valuation from 2011.

Pomlett described the purchase as protecting Walsall from an existential risk,
which is a fair description of a club that had not owned its home since 1990.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The Bescot Stadium opened on 18 August 1990 with a friendly against Aston Villa
and was opened by Stanley Matthews. It held 11,104 then; the two end terraces
were later seated, cutting the figure, and an extension in 2002–03 made the
ground all-seater at **11,300**.

Its two neighbours are its most distinctive feature. It sits within a mile of
junction 9 of the M6, with the motorway running immediately alongside — which
is why several generations of travelling supporters have seen the ground
without ever visiting it. And it has its own railway station: the existing
Bescot station was **renamed Bescot Stadium in 1990** to serve the new ground,
and stands within walking distance. Very few English league grounds have a
National Rail station named after them and adjacent to them.

The name on the stand has changed more often than the ground has. It was the
Bescot Stadium to 2007, the Banks's Stadium from 2007 to 2022 under a brewery
deal, then the Poundland Bescot Stadium from April that year — announced before
the American takeover, not after it — and now carries a haulage network's name.

**Fellows Park**, the club's home for ninety-four years, played its last league
game on 1 May 1990, was demolished from early 1991, and is a Morrisons about a
quarter of a mile away.
