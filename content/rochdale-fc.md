## Origins

Rochdale AFC was founded on 14 May 1907, at a public meeting at the Central
Schools on Fleece Street, and it was the third attempt.

The **first Rochdale AFC** lasted from 1896 to 1 January 1901, when it folded.
**Rochdale Town** was formed the same day, in the immediate wreckage, could not
play until it found a ground that June, and was dissolved in 1904. The club
that exists now shares a name and a ground with the first of them and no legal
connection to either; the record-keepers list them separately, and the club's
own history describes two previous attempts that foundered. Three clubs in
eleven years in one mill town is a fair measure of how thin the ground was.

What the 1907 club did differently was buy the freehold. **Spotland was bought
for £1,700 in 1914**, seven years after the club was founded and seven years
before it had a Football League place, and the club has held it ever since. A great many of the clubs on this site sold their ground in the 1980s
and are still tenants. Rochdale bought theirs before they had anything else.

Election came in **1921**, when the Third Division North was created and
Rochdale were invited in. There were fourteen years of non-league football
first, and the club has never been in the Football League continuously since
its founding — only since its election.

## Trajectory

**Rochdale have never played above the third tier.** Not in the sixty-eight
completed seasons this site records, and not before them: they entered the
League in 1921 at tier three and have spent every season since in the third,
fourth or fifth. It is the cleanest such record of any long-standing League
club, and it has an exact shape.

They were ever-present in the Third Division North for all thirty seasons of
that division's existence, went up to the new national Third Division in 1958
on merit, and came straight back down in 1958/59 — twenty-fourth, 28 points,
the first row of this site's data and a relegation. Then the long stay: **thirty-six
consecutive seasons in the fourth tier, from 1974/75 to 2009/10**, which is the
longest unbroken run in the bottom division of any club in this data, checked
against every club in it. Fifty of their sixty-eight seasons here are
fourth-tier seasons.

Survival in that era was a vote, not a result. Rochdale finished in the bottom
four often enough to need re-election repeatedly, and **in 1980 they were
re-elected by 26 votes to 25 over Altrincham** — one vote, and two clubs
pledged to Altrincham did not cast theirs, one delegate being in the wrong
room and another having the meeting time wrong. The published figure of four
re-elections between 1978 and 1984 does not sit easily with what this site's
data shows: seven bottom-four finishes across the re-election era. Rather than
pick a number, the honest statement is that Rochdale went to the ballot
repeatedly and once survived it by a single vote.

The membership finally went in **2022/23** — bottom of League Two, relegation
confirmed at home to Stockport in April 2023, ending 102 years in the Football
League. They came back at the third attempt, **second in the National League in
2025/26 on 106 points** and promoted through the play-off final at Wembley.

One thing the tables do not show. In October 2022 an EFL disciplinary
commission imposed **six points on the club, suspended for two years** — and
the suspension was never activated. Rochdale therefore belong nowhere on a list
of clubs that have served a points deduction, and this site's data records
none against them in any season.

## Ownership & Finance

For its whole history Rochdale had no majority owner. By 2021 the shares were
spread across more than three hundred holders, most of them supporters, and no
controlling stake existed. That absence is what made the next two years
possible, and then what made them end.

**On 1 July 2021 shares conferring Control were acquired by Morton House MGT
and First Form Construction Ltd**, bought directly from individual
shareholders, without the board's sanction and without the prior EFL consent
that the Owners' and Directors' Test requires. The club stated it had had no
contact of any kind with either company. The EFL opened an investigation that
August; Morton House withdrew from the approval process, said it would divest,
and declined to co-operate with the League. In January 2022 the two companies
brought a **High Court claim against the club's board and the supporters'
trust**, and the trust ran a public appeal for £130,000 in legal costs.

It ended in two parts. In **August 2022 the claim was settled** and Morton
House's 212,895 shares passed to a consortium of seven board members; the
chairman called the club "truly fan-owned", with the board on 43.3 per cent,
the Dale Supporters Trust on 13.9 and more than five hundred individual
supporters holding the rest. Then in **October 2022 the club and four
individuals pleaded guilty** to both EFL charges: the club took the suspended
six points, and the four took bans as Relevant Persons of between eighteen
months and two years. The finding was a governance breach — acquiring control
without consent — not a ruling that the purchases themselves were unlawful,
and the High Court claim was settled without judgment on the merits.

Then, having won, the club could not afford to stay that way. In **February
2024 the chairman said Rochdale needed a £2m injection by the end of March or
faced liquidation**; he had personally lent it £566,000. The money came from
**Sir Peter Ogden's family**, Rochdale-born, in an issue of nine million new
shares approved at an EGM by at least 81 per cent on every resolution. The
**Ogdens took roughly 68 per cent for £2m**, three siblings holding 23 per cent
each; two directors converted loans to equity for 21 and 2 per cent; and the
**Dale Supporters Trust went from 13.9 per cent to 1.6**.

What the trust kept instead is a **golden share**, of the kind the Fan-Led
Review recommended: its consent is required before the club's name, colours or
badge change, or before the ground is sold. The supporters spent a year and
£130,000 defending an ownership structure, won, and two years later exchanged
almost all of their equity for a veto in order to keep the club alive. It is
the clearest case on this site of what fan ownership is actually worth when the
alternative is liquidation, and of what a golden share is for.

Rochdale have never been in administration, never had a winding-up order, and
never entered a CVA. The 2024 episode was a going-concern warning answered with
new equity.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The ground bought for £1,700 in 1914 was not wholly the club's for most of
living memory. **From 1988 Spotland was owned in equal thirds by Rochdale AFC,
Rochdale Borough Council and Rochdale Hornets rugby league club**, through a
stadium company, with the Hornets moving in as co-owners and tenants in the
same year. It is an unusual arrangement and it is still printed in the present
tense in a good many ground guides.

It ended in **March 2016**, when the club bought out the council's and the
Hornets' shares in the stadium company and took full ownership, with the
Hornets signing a long-term lease that guaranteed their continued use. An
earlier attempt in 2014 had stalled. Five months later the ground was renamed
the **Crown Oil Arena** under a six-figure deal with a Bury fuel company,
extended in July 2025 to run to at least the summer of 2028.

Capacity is 10,249, all seated. The record attendance is **24,231**, against
Notts County in the FA Cup in December 1949 — more than twice what the ground
now holds, which is the ordinary arithmetic of the all-seater era in the lower
divisions.

The last line of that story is the one worth holding onto. Rochdale bought
their ground in 1914, spent twenty-eight years owning a third of it, bought it
back in 2016 — and since 2024 cannot sell it without the permission of the
supporters' trust that owns 1.6 per cent of the club.
