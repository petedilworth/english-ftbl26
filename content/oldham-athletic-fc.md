## Origins

Oldham Athletic began in a pub, which is ordinary, and inherited its place in
the game from a club set up to convert a rugby town, which is not.

Pine Villa F.C. was formed on 4 July 1895 by John Garland, landlord of the
Featherstall and Junction Hotel on Featherstall Road North, and took its name
from the Pine Mill, the cotton mill in whose shadow the team played. The pub
was demolished in 1971. In the same year, 1895, a separate club called Oldham
County was founded with an explicit purpose: to bring the association code to
a town that played rugby. County lasted a handful of seasons in the
Combination and the Lancashire League, folded, and Pine Villa took over its
ground and its professional standing in 1899, renaming itself Oldham
Athletic. Exactly when County ceased is not settled — the club's own account
and most secondary sources say 1899, while the specialist league records have
them dissolving after the 1896-97 season — and the two accounts have never
been reconciled in anything that could be checked here. The safest version is
the one both support: Pine Villa took on a defunct predecessor's Sheepfoot
Lane ground and its ambitions at the end of the 1890s, and the Boundary Park
that stands there now dates from the mid-1900s rather than from 1899.

The colours moved the other way from most clubs' — Oldham started in red and
white, close to the town's rugby side, and blue took over gradually, becoming
the settled identity for about a century. There was a tangerine interlude in
the mid-1960s before a return to all-blue in 1972. The Football League
elected them in 1907 and they were First Division runners-up by 1914-15,
losing the title by a point, which remains the club's highest finish.

"The Latics" is simply "Athletic," contracted the way a Lancashire crowd
would contract it. Wigan Athletic use it too, and the shared nickname is
sometimes taken for borrowing in one direction or the other; the dates make
that unlikely, since Oldham took the name Athletic in 1899 and Wigan Athletic
were not formed until 1932. Two Lancashire clubs shortened the same word
independently. When the contraction was first used is not recorded, and it is
better to say so than to invent a first sighting.

## Trajectory

Oldham were founder members of the Premier League in 1992 and are the only
club to have played in it and then been relegated out of the Football
League. That is the headline, and the more interesting fact is how slowly it
happened.

They went down from the Premier League on the last day of 1993/94, finishing
twenty-first with 40 points, and out of the second tier in 1997. What
followed is the longest unbroken run any club in this database has had in a
single division below the top flight: twenty-one consecutive seasons in the
third tier, from 1997/98 to 2017/18. Fifth place and a play-off defeat in
2002/03 was the closest they came to leaving upwards. Twenty-one years, no
promotion and no relegation, in the same division. It is the shape of a club
that had stopped falling and had no mechanism to rise, and it makes the
eventual collapse look less like an event than an exhaustion.

The fall from there was fast. Relegated to the fourth tier in 2018 for the
first time since 1971, and out of the Football League on 23 April 2022,
beaten 1-2 at home by Salford City after 115 years of membership. The match
itself became part of the record: a pitch invasion in the 79th minute took
the players off, and after roughly an hour's stand-off the EFL required the
game to be restarted, so the last minutes of Oldham's Football League
history were played behind closed doors from 18:28.

Only two clubs in this database have played in both the first and the fifth
tier — Oldham and Luton Town — and they did it in opposite directions. Luton
were in the fifth tier from 2010 to 2014 and the Premier League a decade
later; Oldham went the other way, top flight in 1993/94 and fifth tier from
2023 to 2025. Falling that far after the Premier League, rather than climbing
that far towards it, has happened once.

The return took three seasons and cost, on the club's own estimate, about
£7.5m in lost revenue. They came back at Wembley on 1 June 2025, beating
Southend United 3-2 after extra time in the National League play-off final in
front of 52,115 — a record attendance for a National League match. In
2025/26, their first season back, they finished tenth in League Two with 68
points, the club's highest league finish since 2018.

## Ownership & Finance

For nineteen years Oldham Athletic did not own the ground it played in, and
the ground was owned by people who owned part of the club.

The split was created in the 2004 rescue. Simon Blitz, Simon Corney and Danny
Gazal bought the club out of insolvency through a new company, Oldham
Athletic (2004) AFC Ltd; separately, Blitz and Gazal bought Boundary Park
through their company Brass Bank Ltd. From that point the club was a tenant,
paying rent to a landlord that shared its shareholders. Every subsequent
ownership change moved the club and left the stadium behind — Abdallah
Lemsagam's takeover was ratified by the EFL on 26 January 2018, ending
Corney's fourteen years, and the ground stayed with Brass Bank.

The North Stand is where the arrangement became visible. The old Broadway
Stand was demolished in May 2008, the replacement was delayed by the
financial crisis, and Boundary Park stood as a three-sided ground for six
years. A new North Stand was approved by the council in 2013, built from
2014, and completed on 17 October 2015 — as the property of Brass Bank, with
the football club deriving no income from it. Oldham Athletic (2004) AFC Ltd
brought a civil claim against Brass Bank seeking ownership of the stand,
which was live through 2021 and 2022 and was reported as complicating the
sale of the club. In May and June 2022 the club announced the stand would be
closed for the following season, citing the absence of any income from it
against the costs it carried, and safety-certificate concerns after the April
pitch invasion, which had originated there.

It was resolved by consolidation rather than by judgment. Frank Rothwell, the
founder of a Manchester cabin manufacturer, completed the purchase of the
club on 28 July 2022; his family agreed a deal for Boundary Park, its car
parks and the surrounding land the following month, and that purchase
completed on 24 March 2023, putting club, ground and land under a single
ownership for the first time since 2004. The club company is now 97% owned by
the Rothwell family's holding vehicle and 3% by the Oldham Athletic
Supporters Foundation. Whether the stadium sits inside the football company
or beside it is a distinction that mattered enormously for nineteen years,
and the public reporting of the 2023 purchase does not settle it, so this
page does not assert it either.

The supporters' 3% is worth its own paragraph. It was bought for £200,000 and
it carries a seat on the club's board, and it has survived every ownership
change since — including the years when supporter groups were running match
boycotts and an organisation survey reported 97% of respondents wanting the
club sold. In December 2021 the same body nominated Boundary Park and the
club's training ground as Assets of Community Value under the Localism Act
2011, and Oldham Council granted the listing: the owner cannot dispose of
either without notifying the council, and a community group then has six
weeks to declare an interest and six months to prepare a bid. A shareholding
too small to control anything, a board seat, and a statutory brake on the
ground being sold out from under the club — that combination outlasted three
owners.

The finances of the fall are a sequence of near-misses rather than a single
failure. An HMRC winding-up petition in 2016; a transfer embargo that
December; a further HMRC attempt to wind the club up dismissed by the High
Court in December 2019; an administration application in March 2020,
connected to debts owed to Brass Bank, adjourned after a £570,000 payment
covering back rent — with EFL rules at the time making the difference between
an immediate twelve-point deduction and one carried into the next season turn
on the date of 26 March 2020. The club did not enter administration and no
deduction was applied. A further winding-up petition was dismissed in July
2020 once the debt was cleared, and a transfer embargo followed in 2021.

The 2023/24 accounts show a pre-tax loss of just under £3.2m, funded by
borrowing, with total liabilities around £10m and more than £7.1m of it lent
by the owner. Player purchases that season came to £110,000. There is no
turnover figure in this database for Oldham and none in the public reporting
of those accounts, which is a limit of the record rather than a gap in the
data.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Boundary Park sits at roughly 525 feet above sea level on exposed ground at
the north-western edge of the town, and is commonly described as the
second-highest ground in the Football League after The Hawthorns — a ranking
worth attributing rather than asserting, since at least one compilation of
ground altitudes puts Vale Park above it. The reputation for cold is
anecdotal rather than measured, and the nickname "Ice Station Zebra", after
the 1968 film set at an Arctic drift station, is consistently attributed to
Joe Royle without any contemporary source for it. It is folklore, and good
folklore, but folklore.

The plastic pitch was not. Oldham laid an artificial surface at Boundary Park
in 1986 explicitly to earn money from the ground on days when there was no
football, one of four Football League clubs to do so — QPR from 1981, Luton
from 1985, Oldham from 1986 and Preston, the last to remove one, from the
same year. It came up in 1991 on promotion to the First Division, because
artificial surfaces were barred from the top flight from 1991/92. The years
it was down were the best of the club's modern history, taking in a League
Cup final and an FA Cup semi-final in 1990, which drew the accusation of home
advantage that any plastic pitch drew.

The escape that never happened was Failsworth: a roughly £20m, 12,000-capacity
stadium on a thirty-acre site off Broadway, developed with the council under
Simon Corney's chairmanship and abandoned in favour of redeveloping Boundary
Park, with the chairman stating publicly that the club was bitterly
disappointed not to be able to build there.

What has happened instead is a slow municipal reinvestment in the ground that
already exists. Oldham Council put £1m into a hybrid pitch — 97% natural
grass, 3% synthetic fibre — with works starting in May 2023, specifically to
allow football and rugby league to share it. Oldham R.L.F.C. had left
Watersheddings in 1997 and ground-shared at Boundary Park until 2009; with
the new surface they returned for first-team matches from February 2024, this
time as a settled arrangement. In March 2025 the club and the council
announced SportsTown, a £70m sport, education and health campus around
Boundary Park, seeded with £5m from the Government's Community Regeneration
Fund.

A town that had association football imported into it in 1895 by a club
founded for the purpose now has the two codes sharing one council-improved
pitch on the highest ground in the borough. It is a tidier ending than the
nineteen years of divided ownership deserved.
