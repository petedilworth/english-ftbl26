## Origins

Wealdstone were formed at the start of the **1899/1900 season**, out of a
series of local mergers whose roots run back to **1887** and the works sides of
the gunmakers **Cogswell & Harrison** — the blue-collar team played as **The
Oaks**, the white-collar team under the company name.

The place is named after a rock. **The Weald Stone** was a sarsen boundary
marker between the parishes of Harrow and Harrow Weald; a farm took the name,
then a house, then a village, then an urban district created in 1894 and
abolished into Harrow in 1934. The club is **the Stones**, and it is now a
Harrow club that plays in Hillingdon. The name stopped describing the location
in 2008 and nobody has changed it.

## Trajectory

**In 1984/85 Wealdstone became the first club ever to win the non-League
double** — the Alliance Premier League title and the FA Trophy in the same
season, beating Boston United 2–1 at Wembley in front of 20,775 on 11 May 1985.

They were not promoted, and the reason is not the one usually given.

**There was no promotion place.** Automatic promotion from the Alliance to the
Football League did not begin until the end of **1986/87**; entry was still by
**election**, with the Alliance nominating one club to stand against the bottom
four of Division Four at the League's AGM. The top three — **Wealdstone,
Nuneaton Borough and Dartford — all failed the Football League's
requirements**, so the right to stand passed down to fourth-placed **Bath
City**. Bath stood, and lost: all four Fourth Division clubs were re-elected.
Nobody went up from the Alliance in 1985 at all.

So Wealdstone were not refused promotion. **They were ineligible to be
nominated for an election that the nominee then lost.** And they have never
been members of the Football League, before or since.

There is a further twist, and it is already documented on this site's
points-eras page. For three seasons from 1983/84 the Alliance paid **two points
for a home win and three for an away win** — the only English national division
ever to do so. Wealdstone's 62 checks out exactly: eight home wins, twelve away
wins, ten draws. **Under flat three-points-for-a-win, Bath City would have been
champions and Wealdstone third** — and Bath City are precisely the club that
inherited the election application.

What the double did not do was hold anything together. Relegated from the
Alliance in **1987/88**; **the ground sold in 1991**; relegated again that
season; out of the Southern League and into the ninth tier by 1995/96. **Four
levels down in ten years, and thirty-two years to climb back.** They returned to
the fifth tier in 2020/21 as National League South champions, a title decided on
points per game after the season was suspended — which this site's data cannot
see, because it stops at the fifth tier. The gap in the record runs from 1988 to
2021 and is **the second longest of any club here**, behind Accrington
Stanley's forty-one.

**Wealdstone have never had a point deducted.** They lost a Wembley final again
on **17 May 2026**, 0–0 against Southend United and 4–2 on penalties, forty-one
years and six days after winning one.

## Ownership & Finance

The club's entire modern history follows from a single transaction and the way
it went wrong.

**Lower Mead** had been Wealdstone's ground since the early 1920s and the club
owned the freehold. At the end of **1990/91**, following what the club's own
history describes as financial mismanagement by the then owner, **Wealdstone
were forced to sell the freehold to Tesco in order to survive**. The ground was
demolished in June 1991 and a superstore opened on it in 1992.

And then the sentence that makes this page: **the company handling the sale went
into liquidation, and after lengthy court proceedings the club received only a
fraction of what Tesco had paid.** They sold their ground and did not get the
money. Neither the amount nor the identity of the individual responsible is
established in any source this page would rely on, and neither is asserted here.

That is the first of three liquidations, and **none of them was Wealdstone's
own**. The second was the developer building their replacement stadium at the
Prince Edward Playing Fields, which went under in 2004 with the work about
thirty per cent done. The third was **Ruislip Manor FC**, whose collapse in
January 2008 freed the lease at Grosvenor Vale that let Wealdstone come home.
Every insolvency in this club's ground story happened to somebody else, and two
of the three cost Wealdstone a ground.

The club itself has never been in administration, a CVA or liquidation.

**Nick Semaca**, an American investor, agreed a majority takeover in October
2025 and completed it in **April 2026** with 50.1 per cent, through Lakefront
Football Partners LLC; he has previously been a director and investor at Leyton
Orient and holds interests in clubs in Germany and the United States. **The
takeover was held up specifically by the Grosvenor Vale lease** — his
exclusivity period lapsed while the new lease was being signed. Thirty-five
years on, the ground was still the thing that determined what could happen to
the club.

The supporters kept a stake through it. **Shares of more than ten per cent are
held by Lakefront and by the Wealdstone FC Supporters Club**, which is
controlled by supporters and their elected committee, and **more than eight
hundred supporters and investors hold shares** through the club's share scheme.
The Supporters Club was founded in 1958 and has a documented history of
opposing chairmen, including over the sale of Lower Mead.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Wealdstone still do not own a ground.**

They took over a **lease** at Grosvenor Vale in 2008 when Ruislip Manor
collapsed, and they have renewed it twice — an initial five years in 2018, and
in **March 2026 an extension to 31 May 2036 with strong incentives to vacate by
31 May 2031** if a new stadium is ready. **The freehold belongs to the KSIMC of
London**, a Muslim community trust. The club is a tenant of a religious
charity, in a borough it is not named after, thirty-five years after selling the
freehold it did own.

The exile before that ran to **seventeen consecutive seasons and four
landlords**: Watford at Vicarage Road for two seasons, expensive and short;
Yeading for two; **Edgware Town for ten**; Northwood for three. The move to
Edgware in 1995 came with a **voluntary drop in level** — out of the Southern
League and into Isthmian Division Three — partly to cut travel costs. None of
the four is more than five miles from Wealdstone. This is what an exile looks
like when the thing lost is an asset rather than a location.

The numbers at Grosvenor Vale need both halves. Nominal capacity is **4,085 with
709 seats**; the **National League operating capacity for 2024/25 was 1,599**.
Work has been done to meet the division's grading criteria, and a page quoting
only the first figure would mislead.

The next ground is council-identified rather than club-owned. In **November 2023
Hillingdon Council named an eighteen-acre site at Freezeland Way** for a
stadium of about 6,300, and in March 2025 agreed a three-year exclusivity
period for the club to investigate two sites. **No timeline has been set.**
Freezeland Way is about six miles from Wealdstone — further away than Grosvenor
Vale is.

One more thing belongs here, because this site records it from the other side.
On **6 September 2025**, at Scunthorpe, Wealdstone's **Daniel Nkrumah
dislocated his shoulder deep into added time** with Scunthorpe leading 2–1;
paramedics gave him gas and air and three doses of morphine on the pitch, and
after a long delay the referee abandoned the match. The National League awarded
it to Scunthorpe under **rule 8.38** — three points to the home club, **and
goals expressly not added**, which is why the widely reported "3–0" is wrong.
**Wealdstone did not appeal**, saying they had made clear that awarding
Scunthorpe the points was the correct outcome. In the same week the league
ordered a different abandoned Scunthorpe match replayed. Two abandonments, one
league, two rulings.
