## Origins

Huddersfield Town was founded on 15 August 1908 in a town that had invented a
different sport and was not especially interested in this one.

The Northern Union split — the birth of rugby league — happened at the George
Hotel in Huddersfield in 1895, and by 1908 the town's football was rugby's.
When the new association club looked for a ground it was **refused the use of
Fartown**, the established rugby ground, and had to take the recreation fields
at **Leeds Road** instead, where it stayed for eighty-six years. A ground
company was formed with £500 of capital, and the first match there, on 2
September 1908, drew about a thousand people.

The junior status lasted decades. Between the wars the football club was
straightforwardly the poor relation: Fartown averaged over nine thousand
before the First World War, and Second Division Huddersfield took seven or
eight thousand **mainly when the rugby club was playing away**. When the
fixtures clashed, the football club lost thousands at the gate. Everything
that follows — the near-death, the three championships — happened to a club
that was its own town's second team.

It was elected to the Football League in 1910, in place of Grimsby Town.

## Trajectory

**In November 1919 Huddersfield Town's own chairman tried to abolish it.**

The circumstances were dire and the club was not, as the story is usually
told, a First Division one — it was in the Second Division, averaging under
4,500, with about £200 raised in season tickets against weekly costs of £400,
and it had just sold its most popular player. It owed its chairman and
financial backer, **J. Hilton Crowther**, a reported £25,000.

Thirteen miles away a vacancy had opened. Leeds City had been expelled from
the Football League in October 1919 for irregular wartime payments and wound
up, their fixtures handed to Port Vale, and a new Leeds club was forming with
a good ground and no team. Crowther's proposal was to supply one: move
Huddersfield Town to Elland Road and merge it into Leeds United. He made the
offer **without consulting his fellow directors**, and at a meeting in Leeds
that December the resolution to merge carried unanimously.

It was stopped in Huddersfield. A solicitor and former director, **Amos Brook
Hirst**, led the resistance; supporters protested in front of the directors'
box at a reserve match on 8 November, and the next day around **three
thousand people met to reject the merger** — more than had watched the first
team beat Fulham the previous month, which is the detail that says most about
the state of the club. The terms agreed were that the town had **one month** to
raise what was owed to Crowther. It did, through a **public share issue**,
converting the club into broadly held public ownership. Crowther left, became
chairman of Leeds United, lent it £35,000, and put it in blue and white
stripes.

No football authority ever ruled on any of this. League approval was regarded
as a formality; the plan died locally, of a share issue, which is a more
interesting way for a football club to be saved than by a governing body.

Then the improbable part. Having been within a month of ceasing to exist in
November 1919, Huddersfield were promoted as Second Division runners-up that
same season and in the 1920 FA Cup final. Herbert Chapman arrived in 1921 and
won the FA Cup in 1922 and the First Division in **1923/24 and 1924/25** — and
then left for Arsenal on 11 June 1925, before the third. **Cecil Potter** won
1925/26, finishing five points clear of Chapman's Arsenal.

Three consecutive English league titles had never been done before, and it has
been done only four times since — by Arsenal, Liverpool, Manchester United
twice, and Manchester City, who went on to a fourth. **Huddersfield were the
first, and they are the only one of them never to have won the title again.**
Those three remain the club's only championships, and it has won no major
honour since 1926.

The fall was correspondingly complete. Huddersfield were **champions of the
second tier in 1969/70**, and were playing in the **fourth tier by 1975/76** —
five seasons, three relegations. In this site's records they are **the first
former English champion to reach the fourth division**, and by a distance:
Portsmouth followed in 1978/79, Sheffield United in 1981/82, Preston and
Burnley in 1985/86, Wolves in 1986/87. Their last top-flight season before
2017 was 1971/72, a gap of forty-five years.

## Ownership & Finance

The club has been rescued twice by local money and sold twice to people who
did not want its debts.

**Ken Davy** took it out of administration in August 2003, reportedly a day
from liquidation, with debts near £20m — and in 2005 moved the club's 40%
stake in its own stadium out of the club into a company he controlled,
Huddersfield Sporting Pride, on the stated grounds of insulating it from the
ground's debts. He owned the rugby league club that shared the stadium at the
same time. The stake did not come back until **2013, for £1**, after a
four-year stand-off with the next owner.

**Dean Hoyle**, who founded Card Factory, bought the club in June 2009 and
funded it with interest-free loans, reported to exceed £50m and confirmed in
the accounts at £35m owed at one point. He took it to the Premier League
through the 2017 play-off final, held there for two seasons, and then sold
**75% to Phil Hodgkinson in May 2019 for £5.025m, of which £2.525m was paid up
front** — a Premier League club, a year out of the Premier League, for the
price of a squad player.

Hodgkinson's tenure ended in November 2021 when nine companies in his PURE
group entered administration. **The football club did not** — its shares sat
in a separate entity outside the insolvency — but Hodgkinson stepped away and
Hoyle, who had kept 25%, resumed funding it.

The final act is the number that explains the last five years. **Kevin Nagle**
bought 100% of the club in June 2023, and the deal was only possible because
**Hoyle wrote off around £40m owed to him**; the incoming owners would not
take the debt on. What the accounts show since is a club living far beyond its
income: turnover £18.2m against staff costs of £23.0m in 2023/24 — **126% of
turnover** — and £10.6m against £16.9m in 2024/25, **159%**, with a £22.4m
pre-tax loss and net debt of £82.6m. Those figures are press-reported rather
than audited here, and they are the arithmetic of a club that was in the
Premier League recently enough to still be paying for it.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Huddersfield left Leeds Road on 30 April 1994 after eighty-six years, and the
building that replaced it is the only football ground in England that is
better known to architects than to football supporters.

The **Kirklees Stadium** opened in August 1994 with two stands, gained a third
that year and a fourth in 1998, and **won the RIBA Building of the Year Award
for 1995** — the judges singling out the curved tubular trusses spanning 130
metres, which removed the roof supports and left every seat with a clear view.
It is worth being exact about the award, because the usual shorthand is wrong:
the Stirling Prize is its *successor*, launched in 1996, and this is not one.

The ownership of it is the club's history in miniature. From 1994 it was held
by a joint venture — **Kirklees Council 40%, Huddersfield Town 40%,
Huddersfield Giants 20%** — and the football club's share was the one that went
walkabout under Ken Davy between 2005 and 2013. The council agreed to hand
over its own interest in October 2024, and in **September 2025 Huddersfield
Town took 100% ownership** of the stadium and its 53-acre site on a 300-year
leasehold, with the council writing off £4.3m of loans. The rugby club, whose
sport got there first and kept the football club out of Fartown in 1908, is
now its tenant.

The name has changed four times — Alfred McAlpine, Galpharm, John Smith's,
and since 2025 the Accu Stadium — with the first of them written into the
construction contract as part of the payment. **Kirklees Stadium** is the legal
name throughout, which is why this page uses it.
