## Origins

Preston North End began as a cricket club that needed money, and the whole
institution grew out of the search for something to do in winter.

Cricket was being played on the Marsh in the Ashton area by about 1863. In
1867 the club rented a wicket at Moor Park, on the north side of town, and
took the name **North End** as a plain description of where it now played —
which is worth noting, because the name has nothing to do with a Preston
district and predates both the football club and the ground it is now
attached to. In January 1875, in continuing financial difficulty, the club
leased a field at Deepdale Farm. In 1877-78 it started a **rugby** team,
explicitly to earn extra income and provide winter fitness. The rugby failed.
The club turned to association football in 1878, played its earliest known
match that October, and in May 1880 voted unanimously to adopt the
association code. The crest still reads 1880.

Then Preston changed English football. Under **William Sudell** the club paid
its players at a time when the FA forbade it. In January 1884 Upton Park
complained after an FA Cup tie; Sudell admitted the payments and argued that
everyone did it; the FA expelled Preston from the competition. That October
Preston, with Aston Villa, Sunderland and others, threatened to break away
and form a rival British Football Association. The FA formed a sub-committee
— with Sudell on it — and on 20 July 1885 **legalised professionalism**. Three
years later Preston were founder members of the Football League, and in
1888-89 they won it without losing a match and took the FA Cup without
conceding a goal in it: the first Double.

The money came from somewhere. An audit at John Goodair & Co., the Preston
cotton mill Sudell managed, found **£5,326 appropriated between 1889 and 1894**
and diverted to the football club. Sudell pleaded guilty, was convicted in
1895 and served three years' penal servitude, emigrating to South Africa on
release and working as a sports journalist until his death. The team that
forced English football to admit it was paying its players was itself paid
for, in part, with a mill's money.

"The Lilywhites" comes from the shirt, but the shirt took three goes to
arrive: blue and white hoops from 1880, red and white stripes from 1884,
white only from 1887. The name therefore cannot predate the club's peak,
which is when it attaches. Tottenham took white in 1898 and the nickname with
it, and Preston's claim to be the original rests on a decade's priority in
the colour — solid enough as colour history, though no first printed use of
the nickname for Preston has been produced.

## Trajectory

Preston were the first champions of England. **They have not played in the
first division since 1960-61**, and no club currently in the top five tiers
has been away longer — the next nearest, Leyton Orient, dropped out two years
after them.

The descent is documented in stages. Relegated from the First Division in
1961, four months after the abolition of the maximum wage began concentrating
talent in the cities that could pay for it. Down to the Third Division for
the first time in April 1970, on the night at Blackpool that made the
rivalry. Down to the **Fourth Division in 1984-85** — and here a claim usually
made for Preston needs correcting, because Burnley, another founder member,
went down in the same relegation. The two of them arrived in the fourth tier
together in 1985-86. What is Preston's alone is what came next: they finished
twenty-third of twenty-four on 43 points and had to **apply for re-election**
in May 1986, alongside Exeter, Cambridge and Torquay. All four were
re-elected. Burnley followed them into the re-election zone a year later.

The **plastic pitch** was a money decision, laid in 1986 to make the ground
usable seven days a week, with a council deal for community use. It stayed
eight seasons, produced goals at 3.07 a game against 2.7 on the grass before
it, and came up in the summer of 1994 — making Preston the last Football
League club to play on an artificial surface, and the lowest-placed of the
four clubs that tried one.

## Ownership & Finance

Trevor Hemmings, a director in the 1970s, took control in 2010 after HMRC
served a winding-up petition over unpaid tax, buying 51% at **five pence a
share**. Some of those shares had been bought by supporters in two share
offers in the mid-1990s, so a fan shareholding was effectively extinguished
at 5p. Hemmings died in October 2021; his son Craig became chairman, and the
family converted **£30m of shareholder debt into equity** in June 2024,
bringing loans down from a projected £63.8m to £33.8m.

The number that explains the club is the wage ratio. Preston spent **£19.2m on
wages against £16.9m of turnover in 2023-24 — 130% of income**, and total
staff costs the following year came to about 153% of it. This is not a club
living beyond its means in the ordinary sense; it is a club whose ordinary
means do not cover a Championship squad, sustained by owner subsidy of
around £11m a year. Craig Hemmings stepped down as chairman in October 2025
while remaining owner, with the club explicitly seeking new investment, and a
sale process was live as of early 2026.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Preston have played at Deepdale since 1878, which makes it one of the oldest
continuously used grounds in the world. The club's claim to be *the* oldest is
disputed — Bramall Lane has a rival case, and Deepdale's wartime use as a
prisoner-of-war camp is held against its continuity, with the counter that
prisoners played football on it.

Little changed between the 1930s and the 1990s, and the record attendance
still stands from 1938: **42,684 against Arsenal**, in a town whose entire
population today is around 163,000 and which did not become a city until 2002.
The club was a national institution long before its town was a city.

The Taylor Report triggered the rebuild, and it went up one stand at a time
across thirteen years — the Sir Tom Finney Stand in 1996, the Bill Shankly
Kop in 1998, the Alan Kelly Town End in 2001, the Invincibles Pavilion in
2008. The distinctive feature is that the faces of Finney, Shankly and Kelly
are rendered in the seat colours of the stands named for them. Note who is
commemorated: a winger, a defender who became a manager, and a goalkeeper.
All club servants, no directors.

The **National Football Museum** opened at Deepdale in 2001, built into the
voids beneath two of the new stands with a £7.5m Heritage Lottery grant — the
museum and the stands were financially entangled, and it is part of why the
ground was rebuilt as it was. It drew about a hundred thousand visitors a
year and never covered its costs; in 2007 income of £791,256 met outgoings of
£1,231,195. In 2009 the trustees approached Manchester, which offered a
package worth £2m a year. Preston and Lancashire improved their offer to
£400,000. The trustees voted to move, the Preston site closed in April 2010,
and the Manchester museum opened in 2012. The national museum of football
left the ground where the first champions played because a bigger city could
pay five times more for it.
