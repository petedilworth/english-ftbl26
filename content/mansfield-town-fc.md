## Origins

Mansfield Town began in 1897 as **Mansfield Wesleyans**, a chapel side from
Bridge Street, and the most interesting thing about its early history is that
**the church walked away**.

The club was formed by Frederick Abraham and Thomas Cripwell, played its first
match on 4 September 1897, and entered the Mansfield & District Amateur League
in 1902. For the 1906/07 season that league **dropped the word "Amateur" from
its name**. The Wesleyan chapel would not be associated with the professional
game and abandoned the club, which renamed itself Mansfield Wesley and joined
the Notts & District League. It became **Mansfield Town in the summer of
1910**.

That is a small event with a large amount inside it. English football is full
of clubs founded by churches, and the standard telling stops at the founding.
Here the record carries the divorce as well: the moment a chapel side had to
choose between its origins and the direction the sport was going, and the
choice was made for it.

The name changes were not the only thing forced on the club from outside. It
had to leave its Newgate Lane ground in the summer of 1912 because the **Great
Central Railway put a line through the middle of the pitch**, and fell back on
a ground so poor that players changed in the local pub.

The identity settled late, and in the wrong order from the way it is usually
told. The **amber and blue** were adopted in May 1919 by the reconstituted
club, and the nickname they first produced was **"the Ambers"**. "The Stags"
arrives around 1924, from the **stag on the old borough coat of arms** — which
is itself a reference to Sherwood Forest. The forest connection is real, but
it runs through the town's heraldry rather than through the club, and the
extra step is the part worth keeping.

## Trajectory

Mansfield were **elected to the Football League in 1931**, into the Third
Division South — they applied to the southern section deliberately, to court
the northern clubs' votes rather than compete with them — and took the place
of Newport County, who had lost re-election.

They stayed for seventy-seven years. The club's single season in the second
tier came in **1977/78**, immediately after winning the Third Division, and
ended in relegation from twenty-first place on 31 points; it remains the only
time in the sixty-eight seasons this site records that Mansfield have played
above the third tier.

**Relegation out of the Football League came on 26 April 2008**, twenty-third
on 42 points with Wrexham below them, and the manner of it belongs in the
next section rather than this one, because what the supporters did that
afternoon was not about football. Five seasons in the Conference followed —
twelfth, ninth, twelfth, third, and then **champions in 2012/13 on 95 points**,
two ahead of Kidderminster Harriers — and the club came back up at the fifth
attempt. Promotion to League One followed in 2023/24, third on 86 points
behind Stockport and Wrexham.

One deduction appears in the whole of the club's recorded history: **four
points in 2008/09**, in the Conference, for fielding an ineligible player.
They finished twelfth, so it cost them nothing. There is no insolvency event.
For a club whose ownership history is what it is, Mansfield have been
remarkably free of the sanctions that usually accompany it — which is worth
saying plainly, because the trouble here was of a kind the football
authorities have no mechanism to punish.

## Ownership & Finance

**In December 2008 Mansfield Town's outgoing owner used the club's own money to
buy the club's ground, and then charged the club rent for it.**

**Keith Haslam** had bought Mansfield in 1993, aged 34, the youngest chairman
in the Football League, and over his tenure accumulated personal and
inter-company loans from the club that peaked above £1m. The supporters
organised against him — **TEAM Mansfield** formed in 1999, later becoming the
supporters' trust **Stags Fans United** — and on the day of relegation in April
2008 they stormed the boardroom, and Haslam was assaulted and required
hospital treatment. He agreed to sell that July, to three local businessmen
and lifelong supporters, and completion came on **9 December 2008**.

That same month, the club **declared a dividend of £2.44m, of which £2.36m went
to a company Haslam owned**, and **£1.9m of it was used to buy Field Mill**. The
incoming owners challenged the legality of the dividend on the basis that the
club had insufficient net assets to declare it. The record does not fix the
order of the two events within the month, and this page will not guess at it.
What is clear is the outcome: the buyers got a football club and the seller
kept the ground.

The lease terms were **£90,000 a year escalating at ten per cent**, around
£110,000 by 2010, with a provision that would have taken it above £200,000 on
promotion. **John Radford** bought the club for **£1 in 2010** and did not get
the ground with it. In December that year, after the club withheld about
£95,000 of rent during negotiations over a new lease, **Haslam locked it out
of Field Mill for sixteen days**, posted a notice on the gates, and the club
went looking at Alfreton's and Ilkeston's grounds as alternatives.

It took until **March 2012** to agree a seventeen-year lease with a purchase
agreement attached, for under £3m payable in annual instalments, and until
**March 2019** for John and Carolyn Radford to make the final payment and take
the freehold — eleven years after the transaction that separated ground from
club, and a decade after Haslam last had anything to do with the football.

Two things about this are worth stating carefully.

The first is what the authorities did, which was nothing that appears on any
record. Written evidence submitted on Mansfield's behalf to the **Culture,
Media and Sport Committee's football governance inquiry in 2011** argued that
the FA and the Football League had failed to intervene despite documented poor
governance and alleged breaches of company law, and that this failure allowed
the stadium to be removed from the club's ownership. That is an allegation on
the parliamentary record. No inquiry, sanction or ruling either way appears in
the public record. Likewise the challenge to the dividend's legality: the
challenge is recorded, and its resolution is not. The 2012 agreement reads as a
commercial settlement rather than a legal victory.

The second is that "the ground was returned to the club" is not established.
The 2019 purchasers were **John and Carolyn Radford personally**, and the
stadium has been reported since as sitting within Radford's wider business
interests rather than inside the football club, with the rent renegotiated
sharply downward accordingly. If that is right, the structure Haslam built
still stands and only the landlord has changed. The naming is consistent with
it: **Field Mill became the One Call Stadium in April 2012**, after One Call
Insurance, the company Radford founded and runs. That is a related-party
naming arrangement, not a sponsorship won on the open market.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Field Mill's claim is that it is the oldest ground in the world hosting
professional football, and in 2015 the club put up a plaque and a sign across
the Quarry Lane Stand saying so. The claim is defensible, definition-dependent,
and much weaker than the way it is usually repeated.

Football at Field Mill dates from **1861**, when four brothers of the
Greenhalgh family — owners of the Field Mill cotton-doubling business — formed
a works side for the mill's employees. That is a year *after* **Sandygate** in
Sheffield, which Guinness recognises as the oldest football ground in the
world, so Field Mill does not win that title. Against **Bramall Lane**, which
opened in 1855 but first staged football in December 1862, Field Mill wins if
you date by football and loses if you date by the ground.

The word doing the most work is "continuous". Football has been played at
Field Mill by a succession of different clubs since 1861, but **Mansfield Town
did not arrive until 1919**, the site was still in use as a cricket ground into
1921, and for its first half-century it was primarily a cricket venue where the
footballers were not allowed in the dressing rooms. The honest version is that
this is a very old piece of ground with a long and interrupted footballing
history, and that the interruptions are what the claim elides.

How the club got there is a better story than the plaque. The landlord, the
sixth Duke of Portland, evicted Mansfield Mechanics for rent arrears and
rented the ground to a group of local **disabled ex-servicemen**, who sublet it
to Mansfield Town. When the Duke sold the ground in **1921 he attached a
condition that it could only ever be used for sport** — a restrictive covenant
that has outlived every owner since and is the reason the site was never
worth more as anything else.

The modern ground is three new stands from 1999 to 2001, opened by the Deputy
Prime Minister in the summer of 2001 after plans to relocate were dropped, and
one long absence: the **Bishop Street Stand was condemned on safety grounds in
2006** and left derelict for nearly two decades, so that the ground stood
visibly lopsided through the whole of the ownership dispute above. It reopened
with 727 seats in 2024 and was **renamed the Radford Family Stand in September
2025**.
