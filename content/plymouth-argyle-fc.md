## Origins

The club was founded in September 1886 by young men who had left school and
wanted to carry on playing, as the football section of an all-round sports
body called the **Argyle Athletic Club**. The interesting question is where
the word came from, and the honest answer is that nobody has proved it.

What *is* documented is the meeting. It was held at the Borough Arms Coffee
Tavern on Bedford Street in early September 1886, two names were put to it —
**"Pickwick" and "Argyle"** — and Argyle won on the grounds of its **"local
application."** That phrase, recorded at the time, is the whole crux: it points
at something in Plymouth, and it does not say what.

Three candidates are usually offered.

**The Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders**, the most repeated version, is also
the weakest. The regiment was not garrisoned in Plymouth in 1886 — the 2nd
Battalion South Staffordshire Regiment had been there since that March — and
its footballing reputation rests on winning the first Army Cup in **1889**,
three years after Argyle first played. The claim is traceable to about fifty
years after the founding, and it fails the "local application" test on the
simplest possible ground: the regiment was not local. Note also the spelling.
The regiment is Argyll; the club is Argyle.

**Argyle Terrace in Mutley** was built around 1882, four years before the
club, during the vogue for Scottish naming that followed Queen Victoria's
enthusiasm for the Highlands. Naming a small Plymouth club after a local
street was ordinary practice at the time. This fits the evidence best and is
not proven by it.

**A pub of the name** is the version the club's own heritage archive rejects
outright, on the basis that no Argyle Tavern or Argyle Arms existed in
Plymouth in 1886.

The Pilgrims comes from the Mayflower, which sailed from Plymouth in 1620 and
is on the crest. The green and black, contrary to the tartan story that
attaches itself to everything else about the name, are **the colours of the
Borough of Plymouth**.

## Trajectory

Two structural facts define this club, and neither is about a result.

The first is that **Plymouth Argyle have never played in the top division of
English football** — and among clubs that can say that, they have spent more
seasons than any other in the division immediately below it. Twenty-five
second-tier seasons in the sixty-eight this site records, and not one above.
No English club has been closer, for longer, without arriving.

An early version of the same thing is worth having, because the cause was a
rule rather than a shortcoming. Between **1921/22 and 1926/27 Plymouth
finished runners-up in Division Three (South) six seasons in succession**, at
a time when only the champions were promoted. Six second places, no
promotions. They finally won it in 1929/30.

The second structural fact is **2011**, and it is the clearest case on this
site of an off-pitch event deciding a sporting outcome.

The context is a stadium that was never built. In August 2009 the club
unveiled a Home Park redevelopment — 46,000 all-seater, hotel, indoor arena,
at least £50m — designed around England's bid for the 2018 World Cup, and that
December Home Park was **selected** as one of seventeen bid stadiums, at a
proposed capacity of 43,874. It is important to get this the right way round:
Plymouth were chosen, not rejected. What happened was that **England lost the
vote on 2 December 2010**, and the consortium's reason for funding the club
went with it.

By then the money had already gone. Neither players nor staff had received a
full wage since **November 2010**, before the vote, and the club had faced
**five winding-up petitions in the preceding eighteen months**. The proximate
trigger was an HMRC petition it could not settle, and Plymouth entered
administration on **4 March 2011** with the bank account frozen, £3.6m owed to
trade creditors and total debts around £13m.

The ten-point deduction was applied to the season in progress. Plymouth
finished **23rd on 42 points**. Restore the deduction and they finish **19th on
52, four points clear of the relegation places**. The administration relegated
them, and the arithmetic is not arguable.

What followed was eight months of near-death. Sixteen staff were laid off and
the club shop closed. Players deferred wages repeatedly through the summer,
with the PFA negotiating the terms. A takeover by a Gibraltar-registered
company was reported as complete in July 2011 and then collapsed on the
Football League's rule against involvement in two clubs; further interested
parties came and went. **James Brent's** purchase completed on **31 October
2011**, and the Football League, approving it, called doing so "a leap of
faith."

## Ownership & Finance

Plymouth Argyle is not fan-owned, and the record is unambiguous about it. The
club's own company information gives **Simon Hallett 86.99%, Argyle Green LLC
11.84%, and third parties 1.17%** — Argyle Green being a US investment
consortium, not a supporters' vehicle. In May 2021 Hallett announced an
*intention* to offer shares to supporters that would have taken him from 97%
down to 75%. It was never implemented, and the 20% that was subsequently sold
went to Argyle Green. The direction of travel since has been back toward
Hallett, not away. The fan-owned club in Devon is the other one.

The rest of his tenure has been unusually well aligned with the ground.
Hallett became a director on acquiring about 30% in 2016, and became majority
shareholder in **August 2018 specifically by providing the money needed for
the Mayflower Grandstand redevelopment to proceed as planned** — a rare case
where the ownership change and the building work are the same decision. He
bought out Brent's remaining shares in June 2019, and in 2020 **cancelled a
£4m loan to the club in exchange for shares**, which is the cleanest thing an
owner-creditor can do. In November 2023 a £7m rights issue formed part of an
£11m commitment toward a £21m training facility at Brickfields.

Home Park's ownership is the tangled part, and this page will not pretend to
resolve it. The club bought the freehold from Plymouth City Council for
**£2.7m in December 2006**, and lost it again in **October 2011**, when the
council's **£1.6m repurchase was made an explicit precondition of Brent's
rescue** — the club leasing it back at £135,000 a year over thirty years. The
lease carried a buy-back option exercisable every five years at twelve times
the annual rent, first available in **October 2016**; the board said it had
unanimously agreed to exercise it, funded by a share issue, and the Argyle
Fans' Trust publicly opposed under a press release headed "NEVER AGAIN."
Whether it completed is not established in the public record, and it is
recorded here as unresolved rather than guessed at.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The most consequential thing about Home Park is where it is.

**Carlisle United away is 389 road miles, the longest journey in the Football
League**, and a midweek fixture there has been documented at a 779-mile round
trip; a Championship season at Newcastle produced 832. Completing the 92 Club
from Plymouth requires **37,416 miles, roughly five thousand more than from any
other club in the country**. In 2026/27, with Exeter no longer in the
division, Plymouth's *shortest* League One away trip is 396 miles, to Oxford
or Reading, and the longest is 649 to Bradford. The club has chartered flights
to away games. Supporters have been profiled travelling twelve thousand miles
a season at £80 to £90 a match.

This is not colour. It is a permanent cost line that no other English club
carries at this scale, borne by a club that has never been in the top division
and spent 2011 unable to pay its staff. Geography is one of the off-pitch
forces this site is interested in, and Plymouth is the purest example of it in
the country.

The ground itself has been in use since the club took the lease in 1900. The
**Mayflower Grandstand** was its oldest surviving part and was rebuilt in 2019,
fully reopening on 1 January 2020 at a cost the club's accounts put at
**£7.7m**, lifting fixed assets from £8.2m to £12.7m. Capacity is 17,900.
