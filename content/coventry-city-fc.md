## Origins

Coventry City began in 1883 as Singers F.C., a works side formed by employees
of the Singer cycle company, in a city whose industrial identity ran from
bicycles through motorcycles to motor cars. The club played first at Dowell's
Field off Binley Road, moved to an enclosed ground at Stoke Road in 1887, and
took the name Coventry City in 1898. Highfield Road opened in time for the
1899–1900 season. The "Sky Blues" nickname dates precisely to 18 August
1962, when new manager Jimmy Hill relaunched the club in an all-sky-blue
kit for a 2–0 win over Notts County, part of a wider rebrand — including a
"Sky Blue Special" supporters' train and a Sky Blue Song adapted from the
Eton Boating Song — that replaced the club's earlier nickname, the
Bantams.

## Trajectory

Highfield Road was home for 106 years and produced one genuine national
first: in 1981 it became the first all-seater stadium in English league
football, an experiment abandoned within a few years. It staged its last
match on 30 April 2005 and was demolished the following year for housing.

What came after is the clearest case in English football of a club's fortunes
being decided by who holds the deeds to its ground. Coventry moved into a
stadium it did not own, fell into a dispute with the people who did, and
twice ended up playing its home matches in other cities — once in
Northampton, once in Birmingham. The relegations and the attendance collapse
that followed were consequences of that dispute, not of anything that
happened on a Saturday afternoon.

## Ownership & Finance

The hedge fund SISU Capital took control in December 2007. Throughout the
fifteen years that followed, the club did not own its stadium: the Ricoh
Arena was held through Arena Coventry Limited, split evenly between Coventry
City Council and a local charitable trust, with Coventry City as tenants.

A dispute over rent arrears led the stadium company to pursue the club, and
in March 2013 Coventry City Football Club Ltd entered administration on the
application of a SISU-connected creditor. The Football League imposed a
ten-point deduction, upheld after a High Court hearing. When creditors
rejected the proposed company voluntary arrangement, the League transferred
its share to a new company on terms including a further ten-point deduction
the following season — twenty points across two campaigns, none of them lost
on the pitch.

Separately, SISU pursued judicial review proceedings arguing that the
council's £14.4m loan to the stadium company, and the later sale of that
company, breached state aid rules. Those challenges were unsuccessful at
every level; the High Court found for the council, the Court of Appeal
dismissed the challenge to the loan, and permission to appeal further was
refused. Doug King acquired a majority stake in November 2022 and bought out
SISU's remaining holding in January 2023.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The stadium changed hands repeatedly around the club: to the rugby union side
Wasps, who bought out both halves of the arena company in 2014, repaid the
council loan and extended the lease to 250 years; then, amid Wasps' own
financial collapse, to Frasers Group in November 2022.

In between, Coventry twice left the city. The first exile, to Northampton's
Sixfields for 2013–14 and the opening weeks of 2014–15, is the starkest
illustration in modern English football of what a groundshare costs a club.
Average attendance collapsed to 2,222; the ten lowest home league gates in
the club's history were all recorded that season. The first match back at the
Ricoh drew 27,306 — the same club, the same division, twelve times the crowd.
The second exile, to Birmingham's St Andrew's for 2019–20 and 2020–21, saw
the average league gate fall from 12,363 to 6,677.

The story has an ending its supporters had waited two decades for. On 23
August 2025 the club completed the purchase of the arena from Frasers Group,
owning its home for the first time since the ground opened in 2005.
