## Origins

Harrogate Town's founding date is 1914, and in 1914 the club played no
football at all.

**Harrogate AFC** was constituted for the 1914–15 season and entered the
Northern League. Its first fixture, away at Bishop Auckland on **5 September
1914**, was called off when most of the team enlisted. The club re-formed in
1919, joined the West Riding League, and **the first ball was kicked in anger
on 30 August 1919**, a 1–0 win over Horsforth. In 1920 it moved to Wetherby
Road, where it has played ever since. Both dates are defensible; 1914 is the
constitutional one and 1919 the operative one, and the club's real record
begins with the second.

There is a second gap. The club **disbanded at the end of 1931/32** for lack of
funds and support. **Harrogate Hotspurs** were formed in 1935 and **took the
name Harrogate Town in 1948**, to reflect their standing as the town's senior
club. Whether that makes one club or two is not settled by anything in the
public record — no company or affiliation trail either way — so this page
treats the continuity as claimed rather than proved.

The spa town supplies the rest. The nickname **"the Sulphurites"** comes from
the sulphur springs that made Harrogate's name from 1571, and the yellow of the
shirts is explicitly tied to the colour of the water. The crest, first worn in
2001, is not a football badge at all but the town's coat of arms: the Ripon
hunting horn, the Yorkshire rose, the lion of the Royal Forest of Knaresborough
and Knaresborough castle.

## Trajectory

Everything about Harrogate's rise is recent. This site's record of them begins
in **2018/19**, their first season in the fifth tier, and their highest tier
ever is the fourth.

The promotion to the Football League came out of a season that was stopped.
National League clubs voted on 22 April 2020 to curtail 2019/20 and settle the
table on **unweighted points per game**; Harrogate were **second on either
measure**, 66 points from 37 games at 1.784 against Barrow's 1.892, with Barrow
promoted as champions. The play-offs were then played in full behind closed
doors, with second and third seeded straight into the semi-finals, and
Harrogate beat Boreham Wood at Wetherby Road and **Notts County 3–1 at Wembley
on 2 August 2020**.

That was the whole of it: **six seasons in League Two, 2020/21 to 2025/26**,
never higher than thirteenth, and **relegation on the final day of 2025/26 by a
single point** — twenty-third on 39, with Crawley Town twenty-second on 40.

The interesting part is the rule they ran into on the way up, which had nothing
to do with results. **EFL regulations require a surface that is predominantly
natural grass**; artificial pitches are permitted from the National League
down. Harrogate had installed a 3G surface in 2016 and won *both* of their
promotions on it — the National League North play-off final in 2018 and the
2020 semi-final. And the rule bites from below as well as above: National
League regulations provide that a club which wins promotion and does not
convert is **demoted to National League North**. A promoted club on an
artificial pitch is not offered a choice between converting and staying put. It
is offered a choice between converting and dropping two divisions.

**Harrogate have never had a point deducted in any season this site covers.**
The one deduction in their history — three points in October 2013, in the
Conference North, for fielding ineligible players in the opening match of the
season — happened two tiers below the bottom of this data.

## Ownership & Finance

The assumption about Harrogate Town is that a wealthy local father bought a
football club and installed his son as manager. The order of events is the
other way round.

**Simon Weaver was appointed player-manager in May 2009**, under a chairman who
by later accounts was barely keeping the lights on. **Irving Weaver, a
Harrogate property developer, took control in 2011** — two years afterwards,
and at his son's urging. Simon Weaver was still in post for 2026/27, having
been the longest-serving manager in the top four divisions of English football
for the whole of the club's Football League spell.

The finances are as clear a case of benefactor dependence as this site records.
Irving Weaver owns **96 per cent of the shares**. Turnover was **£3.1m in
2023/24** against a pre-tax loss of £2.5m, and **£4.1m in 2024/25** — up nearly
a third on an FA Cup tie with Leeds United and higher attendances — against a
loss of £2.2m. The amount the club owes him went from **£2.46m to £5.25m to
£6.4m** across those years. **He charges no interest**, has stated he will not
seek repayment within the forecast period, and has said he intends to keep
making funds available: the going-concern basis of the accounts rests on one
person's stated intention.

Both men have been public about the perception, and the club has been public
about the exposure. Weaver was **openly seeking outside investment** while the
club was in a relegation fight, and the board said after relegation that it was
proactively looking for investors to future-proof the club. A **£2.75m training
ground** at Wetherby Racecourse — the club has been looking for a training site
since the day it went up — was applied for in December 2025 and **withdrawn in
May 2026** after revised flood mapping put the clubhouse in a flood zone.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Wetherby Road has been the ground since 1920 and the club owns it. What
happened to it between 2016 and 2024 is the story of what the Football League
costs a club that reaches it from below.

The **3G pitch went in during the summer of 2016**, headlined as part of about
a million pounds of investment in community football and paired with a separate
grant-funded artificial pitch at a local school, with a usage plan agreed with
the Football Foundation and the county FA. It was not a gimmick. It was used by
the club's age groups from eight to sixteen, by schools, by youth teams,
walking football and a mental-health programme, and the chairman's figure for
losing it was that **revenue "might go from £200,000 to £150,000"**.

Then they won promotion, and had to dig it up. The conversion cost **"upwards
of £200k"** by the chairman's account, plus roughly **£20,000 a year** in
groundsman and maintenance costs that an artificial pitch does not incur, and
the club paid for it — no grant was found for the work. The offset he named was
that the EFL's basic award is substantially larger than the National League
grant. Nobody had done this before: the BBC, reporting Sutton United going
through the same thing a year later at a cost of around £500,000, described
them as the second club in two years to be forced to swap an artificial pitch
for grass, after Harrogate.

Two other conversions ran alongside it, both to meet the League's ground
criteria: capacity from about **2,800 to over 5,000** in 2020, with new
terracing, a two-storey clubhouse and new floodlights; and then a **minimum of
2,000 seats by 30 April 2024**, met by converting two terraces and taking the
seated capacity from **1,193 to 2,060**. For the first three of their six EFL
seasons Harrogate were playing to a second grading deadline they had not yet
satisfied.

The ground has carried four sponsors' names in thirteen years. Wetherby Road is
the only one that has stayed put.
