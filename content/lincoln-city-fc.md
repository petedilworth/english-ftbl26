## Origins

Lincoln City were founded in 1884, and for the first century of their
existence the defining fact about them was not where they finished but
whether they would be allowed to stay.

Before automatic promotion and relegation reached the bottom of the Football
League, the clubs finishing last applied to their fellow members for
re-election, and the members voted. It was a system that rewarded being
liked, being useful to visit, and being from somewhere that other clubs
wanted a fixture in — and Lincoln, out on the eastern edge of the country
with a long journey attached, was none of those things reliably. The club had
to seek re-election **six times between 1893 and 1908**. In 1908 the vote was
tied on the second ballot and resolved in Tottenham Hotspur's favour, and
Lincoln were out. They were out again in 1911, and again in 1920.

The nickname arrived from the cathedral, and is younger than it sounds. The
**Lincoln Imp** is a small grotesque carved into the Angel Choir of Lincoln
Cathedral in the thirteenth century, one of hundreds of such figures and for
six hundred years no more famous than any of them. The story attached to it —
a demon sent by the devil, turned to stone by an angel for making mischief
among the choir — **has no traceable existence before the late nineteenth
century**, and the name appears in print in an 1897 pamphlet. The legend and
the football club are therefore near-contemporaries: a city inventing its own
folklore at almost exactly the moment it invented its own team. The Imp
reached the shirt in 1971, and by then everyone had long since stopped asking
which came first.

## Trajectory

**On 9 May 1987 Lincoln City became the first club ever relegated
automatically out of the Football League.** That is the sentence the club is
known for, and every part of it needs its date.

Until that season the bottom four applied for re-election. From 1986/87 the
League replaced the ballot with a single automatic relegation place and a
promotion from the Conference below it — a genuine opening of the trapdoor,
and the reason Scarborough came up as champions that year. The first club to
go down through it finished twenty-fourth of twenty-four on **48 points,
level with Torquay United and four goals worse off**. In this site's records
1986/87 is the first season in which any club is marked as relegated from the
fourth tier at all, and Lincoln are the only club marked that way in it.

The final afternoon is the most-told story in the club's history and the
detail is documented: Torquay, needing something at home to Crewe, had a
player — Jim McNichol — bitten by a police dog patrolling the touchline, and
the added time for his treatment produced the equaliser that kept them up.
The fate of a Football League membership held since 1892 turned on the
minutes added for a dog bite. It is an absurd story, and it is also the
clearest possible illustration of what the rule change had done: under the
old system the League would simply have voted, and Lincoln, on their record,
would probably have been voted back.

Then they did the other half of it. **Lincoln won the Conference at the first
attempt in 1987/88** — 82 points, two clear of Barnet — and went straight
back up, the first club to be relegated out of the League and return
immediately. Ejected in 2011, they took six seasons over it the second time,
coming up as champions in 2017 with 99 points.

Two longer patterns are worth stating plainly, because they place all of the
above. **Lincoln City have never played in the top flight** — not once,
in the whole of the club's history. And the second tier had been a memory for
most of living recall: after 1960/61 the club did not appear in it again
until 2026/27, a gap of sixty-five seasons, closed by winning League One in
2025/26 with 103 points.

## Ownership & Finance

The most consequential thing that ever happened to this club's balance sheet
was a cup run, and the club spent it correctly.

In 2016/17, in the National League, Lincoln reached the **quarter-finals of
the FA Cup** — the first non-league club to do so in 103 years — beating
Ipswich, Brighton and Burnley on the way. The run generated over £1m. What
makes it a cause rather than an anecdote is what happened to the money: it
went into a **£1.3m Elite Performance Centre**, opened in 2018, and the FA
subsequently published the club as a case study in converting cup income into
permanent infrastructure. Most windfalls of that size in the lower divisions
are consumed by the wage bill within eighteen months and leave nothing behind.
This one left a building.

Ownership has moved a long way in a decade. A South African-led investment
group chaired by Clive Nates put money in from 2016 and steered the club
through the promotions; from December 2025 **Liquid Investments, Inc.**,
owned by the American businessman Ron Fowler, moved above twenty-five per
cent of the holding company, took EFL clearance in January 2026 and control
in February, with Fowler as chairman. Nates remains a substantial shareholder
in a vice-chairman's role.

The supporters have a real stake in this and it is regularly overstated. The
**Red Imps Community Trust** — born, like a great many English supporter
trusts, out of the ITV Digital collapse — holds shares in both the holding
company and the football club, amounting to just under six per cent, and is
represented on both boards. That is a meaningful minority position with a
seat at the table and a vote that can be counted. It is not fan ownership,
and the club should not be described as fan-owned.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Sincil Bank has been the ground since 1895 and is named for the drain it sits
beside — the Sincil Dyke, a medieval channel cut to take water out of the low
ground south of the city. As with a good deal else in Lincoln, the football
follows the drainage.

The ownership of it tells the club's financial history in two transactions.
In **1982 the ground was sold to the city council for £225,000**, with the
club taking a 125-year lease back — the standard move of a lower-division
club that needs cash now and has only one asset, and one that at least kept
the ground in public rather than speculative hands. In **2000 the club bought
it back**. Between those dates lies most of what went wrong and then right,
and the fact that the second transaction happened at all distinguishes
Lincoln from a long list of clubs that sold a ground in the eighties and are
still tenants, or are no longer clubs.

The stadium now carries a sponsor's name, as the ground of a club of this
size generally must; it has been Sincil Bank for a hundred and thirty years
and everyone calls it that.
