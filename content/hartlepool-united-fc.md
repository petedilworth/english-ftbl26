## Origins

The club was founded on **1 June 1908** and named for a plural. There were two
towns — **Old Hartlepool** and **West Hartlepool** — and the club, based in the
second, took the name of both: **Hartlepools United**. Elected to the Football
League in 1921 as a founder member of the Third Division North, it kept that
name for sixty years.

What changed it was local government. **On 1 April 1967 the two county boroughs
merged into one**, and the plural town stopped existing. **The club dropped the
"s" in 1968**, a year later, and became Hartlepool United in 1977. The name is
downstream of a boundary review.

The monkey needs handling carefully, because the club has built an identity on
it. The story — a shipwrecked ship's monkey in uniform, hanged as a French spy
during the Napoleonic Wars — **has no contemporary documentary record**.
Shipwreck records for Hartlepool Bay in that period list fourteen ships lost and
none of them French, and **the earliest known trace of the legend is an 1854
music-hall song** by a Newcastle entertainer, decades after the war it is
supposed to describe. There is no strong evidence the tale circulated in
Hartlepool before he performed it there. The nickname is a Victorian invention
the town adopted, and the football club adopted after that.

## Trajectory

**Hartlepool United applied for re-election to the Football League fourteen
times, and were re-elected fourteen times.** Three of those applications were to
the Third Division North and eleven to the Fourth Division, which is the
divisional record. No club in any source has more.

The eleven Fourth Division applications can be rebuilt exactly from this site's
data, which is a better check than a citation. From 1958/59 the bottom four of
Division Four went to a ballot at the League's AGM in late May, where the members
voted either to keep them or to admit a non-league applicant instead. Hartlepool
finished in the bottom four in **1960, 1961, 1962, 1963, 1964, 1970, 1971, 1977,
1978, 1983 and 1984** — eleven seasons, no more and no fewer, before the system
was abolished.

Three of those ballots removed a club, and it was never this one:

- **1960.** Hartlepool finished **twenty-fourth of twenty-four — bottom of the
  entire Football League — on 27 points, and were re-elected.** **Gateshead, who
  finished twenty-second, two places and six points above them, were voted
  out**, and Peterborough United came in. Nothing in football makes the point
  more cleanly: the ballot did not reward league position, and was not intended
  to.
- **1977.** Hartlepool twenty-second. **Workington, bottom, were voted out** and
  Wimbledon elected — the same vote that Carlisle's page describes from the other
  end, and the reason Carlisle became the most isolated club in the League.
- **1978.** Hartlepool twenty-first. **Southport, who finished twenty-third, were
  voted out** after tying with Wigan Athletic on twenty-six votes in the first
  round and losing the re-vote 29–20. **Rochdale finished bottom that year and
  were re-elected** — the same pattern again, one place further down.

In **1963** Hartlepool finished bottom again and nobody lost their place at all.

Re-election was replaced by automatic relegation to and promotion from the
Conference **from 1986/87**, and this site's data shows the switchover in a
single row: at the end of that season **Lincoln City went out and Scarborough
came in**, the first automatic exchange in the record.

The rest of the trajectory is the shape that ballot produced. **Hartlepool have
never played above the third tier**, and **48 of their 69 seasons here are
fourth-tier seasons** — second only to Rochdale's 50, with Darlington third on
47. Their longest unbroken run in the bottom division is **twenty-two
consecutive seasons, 1969/70 to 1990/91**, again second to Rochdale. The best
finish in the whole record is **sixth in the third tier**, in 2003/04 and again
in 2004/05.

They finally went out in **2016/17**, twenty-third on 46 points, ninety-six
years after election — and they went out on someone else's goal, with Newport
County scoring against Notts County in the last minute of normal time to survive
in their place. They came back through the **2021 play-off final at Ashton Gate**,
1–1 with Torquay after the opposition goalkeeper headed a 95th-minute equaliser,
and **5–4 on penalties**. They went down again in **2022/23**, twenty-third on
43, and the club was put up for sale that April.

Across all sixty-nine seasons, **Hartlepool have never had a point deducted** —
and this is checkable rather than merely unrecorded: match-derived points equal
table points in every single season, at two points for a win before 1981 and
three after.

## Ownership & Finance

The near-death was 2018, and the club was days from liquidation when it was
sold.

Through the winter of 2017/18 Hartlepool faced **three winding-up petitions**;
the chairman said afterwards she had stopped the club going into administration
several times; a potential investor withdrew in January; **supporters raised over
£13,000**; and a bid was mounted with a well-known broadcaster fronting it. In
**April 2018 Raj Singh completed a takeover**, approved by the FA and the
National League on the 21st, with the club under a transfer embargo, and
committed £1.2m.

The exit took two years and nine months. The club was put up for sale in April
2023 and every bid in the interim collapsed, including one brokered by the
supporters' trust, which set a deadline in **January 2024** for an improved
offer; the owner said no viable agreement could be reached and the trust's board
disputed his account of why. **No golden share and no trust shareholding was
ever created.** The accounts for the year to 31 July 2024 show a **pre-tax loss
of £1.26m** and **net liabilities of £3.96m**, up £1.25m in twelve months.

**On 31 December 2025 the club was bought by Landon Smith**, an American
investor and co-founder of a Minnesota real-estate firm, taking a full
controlling stake and ending almost eight years of the previous ownership.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Hartlepool have played at the same ground for their entire history, which very
few English clubs can say. The land was bought from the **North Eastern Railway
Company in 1886** by West Hartlepool Rugby Football Club; when the rugby club
went bankrupt, the new football company bought the stadium in 1908.

**On 27 November 1916 a German Zeppelin bombed Hartlepool and destroyed the main
stand.** The club pursued the German government for compensation and **never
received a penny**, and the "temporary" wooden stand that replaced it stood
until the mid-1980s. It is the best single fact about this ground and it is a
straightforwardly financial one: a small club absorbed the cost of an act of war
for seventy years because nobody would pay for it.

**The ground is Hartlepool Borough Council's.** The council's asset register
records the site as leased to the football club on a **seventy-year lease running
from June 1997**, and when the club **offered to buy the freehold in 2010 the
council refused**. A memorandum of understanding on a long-term redevelopment —
demolishing the neighbouring leisure centre to free land for a 15,000-capacity
community stadium — has produced nothing built, and the owner said publicly he
was frustrated at the pace. Capacity is 7,856, a substantial part of it still
terracing, which is comfortable at this level and would not be higher up.

And then the geography, which is worth correcting because it gets written
backwards. **Hartlepool are not an isolated club.** Measured from this site's own
coordinates, **five League clubs sit within twenty-six miles**: Middlesbrough at
7.7, Sunderland 17.1, Darlington 18.6, Gateshead 23.6, Newcastle 25.6. Compare
Carlisle, whose nearest is 51.7 miles away.

What Hartlepool have instead is **divisional isolation**. Their neighbours play
in the first and second tiers and they have spent seventy years in the fourth
and fifth, so their actual opponents are in the Midlands and the south: a career
mean distance to divisional opponents of **154.4 miles**, eighth highest of all
clubs with twenty-five seasons or more, well behind genuinely remote clubs like
Plymouth and Torquay. The travel burden is real, and it is a product of the
league table rather than the map.

The sharpest illustration is next door. **Hartlepool have never played a league
match against Middlesbrough** — not once in sixty-nine seasons of this data, and
not before it. Middlesbrough's only two third-tier seasons are 1966/67 and
1986/87, and Hartlepool were in the fourth tier for both. One has never been
below the third tier and the other has never been above it. The closest the two
have come was **August 1986, when Middlesbrough — locked out of their own ground
by bailiffs — played a home fixture at Victoria Park.** It remains the only time
their league football has been played on the same pitch.
