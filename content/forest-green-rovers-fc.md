## Origins

Forest Green Rovers were founded in **October 1889** by the Nonconformist
minister of a hamlet on a hill, and played friendlies for five years before
joining a league at all.

Almost everything about the club's early identity was later undone and redone.
In **1911 it merged with Nailsworth FC** and became **Nailsworth & Forest Green
United**, taking Nailsworth's blue and white; reconstituted after the war in
**1919**, it registered black and white. In **1989**, in the middle of a
financial crisis, it renamed itself **Stroud FC** in an attempt to widen its
catchment, alienated its supporters, and **changed back in 1992**. None of that
is modelled in this site's data, which has the club only from 1999 and under
one identifier throughout.

The size is the thing everyone quotes and it is usually rounded. **Nailsworth's
population was 5,794 at the 2011 census and 5,688 at the 2021** — the town got
smaller between the two — and strictly the club is in Forest Green, a hamlet
within the Nailsworth ward, not in the town. When Forest Green reached the
Football League in 2017 it was reported as the smallest place ever to host a
League club, displacing Irthlingborough. That is a well-sourced media claim
rather than an official record, and it is worth stating as one.

The green came last. The traditional colours were black and white, registered
in 1919 and surviving in the chant long after the shirts went; **a new crest
arrived in 2011/12 and the switch to lime green and black followed a season
later** — five years into the current ownership. The colour is not inherited.
It is a rebrand.

## Trajectory

**Forest Green reached the Football League on 14 May 2017**, beating Tranmere
Rovers 3–1 in the National League play-off final at Wembley in front of 18,801,
in the club's 128th year. They won **League Two in 2021/22** on 84 points, level
with Exeter City and ahead on goal difference. Then it came apart in
twenty-four months.

**2022/23 is the club's only season in the third tier: twenty-fourth of
twenty-four, 27 points, relegated.** It is not the worst season League One has
seen — Stockport in 2010, Leyton Orient in 1995 and Stockport again in 2005 were
all lower, and Oxford in 2001 finished on the same 27 — but what follows is
distinctive. Checked across every club in this data, **Forest Green are the only
club whose single third-tier season was followed straight out of the Football
League.** Four other clubs have exactly one third-tier season and nothing higher
— Barnet, Dagenham & Redbridge, Macclesfield and Rushden & Diamonds — and each
of them either stayed in the fourth tier for years afterwards or had a season in
between. Forest Green went through.

The off-pitch account of the collapse is unusually candid, and it is about
people leaving rather than money. The head coach went to Watford in May 2022,
the director of football left that September, and the chief executive in
January 2023. The chairman's own statement named the cause: *"Our best efforts
have not been good enough, in recruitment primarily — it's not a budget
issue."* Seven of the ten players signed that summer made fewer than twelve
appearances. Relegation out of the League followed in **2023/24**, bottom of
League Two on 42 points — a total that would have kept a club up in other
seasons, and did not here.

One deduction sits in the record. **Three points in 2014/15, for fielding an
ineligible player at Southport on the opening day**, a match Forest Green won.
It cost them 82 points and left them on 79, fifth, still in the play-offs, and
beaten in the semi-final. The better frame is that it was not an isolated
lapse: **the same division docked three points off three clubs that season for
the same offence**, and a fourth club in the Football League as well. The
Conference had a registration-compliance problem that year.

## Ownership & Finance

Forest Green is **95.7 per cent owned by Ecotricity New Ventures Limited**,
whose ultimate parent also owns the energy company. It is not a fan-owned club
and never has been.

**Dale Vince took majority control in August 2010** and became chairman a few
months later, after the outgoing chairman had disclosed serious debt and called
the club's position "a crisis". It is worth being exact about that, because it
is usually written as a rescue from liquidation: **no insolvency event is
documented — no administration, no CVA, no winding-up order.** What is
documented is a transaction and a disclosed debt.

The funding has been continuous and large, and it has been publicly defended.
Losses of £2.5m in 2013/14 and £2.9m in 2014/15 left the club nearly **£5.4m in
debt**; the response at the time was that these were peak losses and that
infrastructure spending distorted them. Whether the money goes in as loans or
as equity is not established from the public record, and this page does not
guess. What the accounts show is the shape of the fall:

| Year to 30 June | Turnover | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | £5.05m | loss £785,167 |
| 2023 (League One) | £8.4m — a record | loss over £1.3m |
| 2024 (relegated from League Two) | £7.94m | loss after tax £3.63m |
| 2025 (first year back in the fifth tier) | £7.25m | loss just under £3m |

The 2019 loss has a named cause the club gave itself: **a transfer to Bolton
Wanderers that collapsed**, worth a fee believed to be around £1m that never
arrived. And the parent is under pressure too — a **£7.3m pre-tax loss** for the
year to April 2024, against a £49.7m profit the year before.

The consequences of that are now being stated in public in a way most
benefactor-funded clubs avoid. Announcing that there would be **no Forest Green
Rovers women's side for 2026/27**, the owner put the reason in cash terms: a
third season outside the Football League meant facing **"the full £1.5 million
loss of income"** as solidarity payments ended, and a planned partnership had
collapsed late, leaving a six-figure cost. The women's team had lost one league
match the previous season, finished second in the fifth tier, and had a place
in the pyramid allocated to it.

## Infrastructure & Environment

This is the club that made an environmental position into an identity, and the
certifications are real and datable. Meat and dairy came off the matchday menu
in **2015**; the club became **the first football club to receive the Vegan
Society's Vegan Trademark in 2017**, which is what "the world's first vegan
football club" actually rests on; FIFA described it as the greenest club in the
world the same year; and in **August 2018 it was certified by the UNFCCC's
Climate Neutral Now scheme as the world's first carbon-neutral football club**,
won a UN Momentum for Change award, and became a founding member of the UN
Sports for Climate Action framework. The two 2017 and 2018 dates are constantly
merged; they are different bodies and different claims.

The New Lawn itself opened in **2006** at about £3m, two hundred metres from the
ground the club had used since 1890. **Solar panels went on in December 2011** —
180 of them, supplying around a tenth of the stadium's electricity — followed by
a **solar-powered robot mower** in 2012 and, that December, an **organic pitch**:
no chemical pesticides, herbicides or fertilisers, with irrigation run-off
captured and recycled beneath it. The ground has carried three sponsors' names
in five years. It also, briefly, housed a refugee: **Gloucester City spent
2007/08 there** after their own ground went under eight feet of water in the
2007 floods.

And then **Eco Park**, which is where the page has to be blunt. An all-timber,
5,000-seat stadium by **Zaha Hadid Architects** — the practice, commissioned
after Zaha Hadid's death in 2016 — at the centre of a hundred-acre development
at Junction 13. **Refused by Stroud District Council in June 2019** on noise,
traffic and landscape grounds; a revised application **approved that December**;
a call-in request from the parish council **refused in March 2020**; further
plans in 2022; **boreholes sunk in February 2023**, widely reported as breaking
ground; **detailed designs approved in December 2024**.

**As of the most recent reporting, no stadium construction has begun.** Most of
the thirty-odd planning conditions are discharged and the drawings are ready,
and the blocker is a **highways requirement for a dual carriageway** to handle
matchday traffic. The only physical work at the site is investigation,
landscaping and training pitches — and even a proposal to move temporary
first-team facilities there was refused. The **£100m figure belongs to the whole
development**, not the stadium.

There is one more planning file worth knowing about, because it explains what
the delay is holding up at the other end. **The New Lawn already has outline
permission to be demolished and replaced with up to 95 homes** — conditional on
the Eastington stadium being operational first. The club cannot leave until the
new ground is built, and the old ground is already sold, in principle, to the
thing that will pay for it.
