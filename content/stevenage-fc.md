## Origins

Stevenage FC is the third football club of that name, and it is not the
continuation of either of the others.

**Stevenage Town** was founded in 1894, reached the Southern League Premier
Division in 1967 and folded at the end of the following season, unable to meet
the costs of the semi-professional game. **Stevenage Athletic** was formed
immediately afterwards, in 1968, rose as far as Southern League Division One
North, finished bottom of it in 1975/76, was declared bankrupt, and resigned
from the league on **13 August 1976** — days into a season it had already
entered.

Then the part that everyone in the town remembers. The ground at Broadhall
Way was sold after the collapse to a local businessman, who **dug a trench the
full length of the pitch** to make certain no football could be played on it.
It is one thing for a club to die; it is another for someone to take a
mechanical digger to the reason it might come back.

**Stevenage Borough** was founded later that same year by volunteers, with
Keith Berners as chairman, and for its first three seasons it had no ground at
all — it played in the Chiltern Youth League on a roped-off pitch at the King
George V playing fields. The council eventually took the sabotaged site back
and the club moved into Broadhall Way in 1980. The name became **Stevenage FC**
on 1 June 2010, on promotion to the Football League, with "Boro" kept as the
nickname.

There is a civic continuity here, of a town and a ground and a set of people.
There is no corporate continuity whatever: three legally distinct clubs, and
the company that runs the present one was not incorporated until 1994.
Stevenage was also designated the **first New Town** under the New Towns Act,
on 11 November 1946, which makes the club something like the football
equivalent of the place — assembled from scratch, twice, on ground somebody
else had cleared.

## Trajectory

**In 1995/96 Stevenage Borough won the Conference by eight points and were not
promoted.** That is the season this club is known for, and it is worth setting
out exactly, because most retellings get at least one part of it wrong.

The Football League required a ground with a 6,000 capacity including 2,000
seats, demonstrated by a deadline **in December 1995**. Broadhall Way's new
all-seater West Stand held **460**. Separate financial criteria applied to
clubs coming in and not to the Third Division clubs already there. Stevenage
finished the season on 91 points to Woking's 83 and stayed where they were;
**Torquay United, bottom of Division Three, were reprieved**, and this site's
records show **no club at all relegated from the fourth tier that season**.

Stevenage took the Football League to court. In *Stevenage Borough Football
Club Ltd v The Football League Ltd*, decided by Carnwath J on 23 July 1996,
the judgment split three ways:

- The League **was** entitled to impose a ground-capacity criterion. It was
  not irrational, arbitrary or capricious.
- **But** requiring a club to demonstrate compliance *before it was known
  whether it could qualify for promotion* was open to challenge as a restraint
  of trade — as was applying financial tests to entrants that incumbents did
  not have to meet.
- **Relief was refused anyway**, for delay and for the prejudice it would cause
  to Torquay. The criteria had been published in May 1995; the judge held that
  Stevenage could have challenged the rules at the start of the season and that
  the challenge had come too late.

So Stevenage lost, and it cost the club a reported £200,000. What changed the
rules was a single sentence of *obiter*: *"There are features of the current
arrangements which in my view do require reconsideration if they are to resist
challenges on restraint of trade principles in the future."* The League
relaxed the entry criteria and the timing for the following season, and
promotion and relegation between the Conference and the Football League
resumed in **1996/97** — Macclesfield Town up, Hereford United down — and has
run every year since.

The database makes the case better than the litigation did. **Stevenage were
the third Conference champion in three consecutive seasons to be refused
promotion** — Kidderminster Harriers in 1994, Macclesfield Town in 1995,
Stevenage in 1996 — and in all three of those seasons this site's records show
zero relegations out of the fourth tier. For three years the bottom of the
Football League was sealed. Stevenage were not the only club it happened to.
They were the last, and the only one that sued, and the case is why it stopped
happening to anybody.

The club finally came up as Conference champions in **2009/10** with 99 points,
went up again through the play-offs a year later — beating **Torquay United**
1–0 at Old Trafford, which is either a coincidence or the longest-delayed
punchline in English football — and reached the third tier in 2011.

One later season deserves a note, because it is the same mechanism pointing
the other way. In the COVID-curtailed **2019/20** League Two season Stevenage
finished bottom of the table and **stayed up**, because Macclesfield Town
accumulated seventeen points of deductions across four separate sanctions for
unpaid wages and unfulfilled fixtures and finished below them. The club that
was kept out of the League by an administrative rule was kept in it by one.

## Ownership & Finance

**Phil Wallace** bought 90% of Stevenage Borough in 1999, when the club was by
some accounts about a day from closing, and has since increased his holding to
around **98%**. That is not a consortium or a fan-ownership structure; it is
one man, and the club's continued existence has depended on him for
twenty-seven years.

The stadium sponsorship makes the point neatly. Wallace was the owner of the
Lamex Food Group, and from 2009/10 the ground was the **Lamex Stadium** — the
owner's own company holding the naming rights to the owner's own club's
ground, for fifteen years. The arrangement ended in October 2025 when Wallace
retired from Lamex; the club put the naming rights out to the market that
November and, in August 2026, signed a ten-year deal with a financial-services
start-up. For a decade and a half the ground's commercial name was an internal
transfer.

Wallace is unusually blunt in public about what that model means. He describes
the third tier's cost base as "completely and totally unsustainable", puts
collective League One losses at over £120m in a season, and argues that "the
reliance on owner funding to keep our sport competitive at our level needs to
be replaced by sustainable clubs able to function without owner funding."
That is a benefactor owner explaining why benefactor ownership is a problem,
which is rarer than it should be.

The club has never had a point deducted, and there is no insolvency event in
the record of the 1976 club. The 1999 crisis was resolved by a share sale.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Broadhall Way was built in 1960 and opened in 1961, which makes it older than
any of the three clubs that have used it — Town from 1961, Athletic from 1968,
and the present club since 1980, with the trench in between. It holds 7,318,
and its record attendance is **8,040**, set on 25 January 1998 for an FA Cup
fourth-round tie against Newcastle United, for which a temporary stand was put
up to get the figure over eight thousand.

That tie is worth keeping alongside the 1996 case, because it is the same
argument in a different register: **Newcastle's manager tried to have the match
switched to St James' Park on safety grounds**, and failed. Twice in three
years, Stevenage's infrastructure was used as a reason they should not be
allowed to play the fixture they had earned. They drew 1–1 and lost the replay.

The ground is the council's, and the club is its tenant — there is no record of
a freehold purchase, and the chairman's own public remarks refer to renewing a
lease. That is a common enough position for a club of this size, and it is the
same relationship that failed catastrophically in 1976, when the site passed
into private hands and came back with a trench in it.
