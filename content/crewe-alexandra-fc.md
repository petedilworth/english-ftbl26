## Origins

Crewe Alexandra began in August 1877 as the football division of **Crewe
Alexandra Cricket Club**, which had been founded eleven years earlier by
employees of the **London and North Western Railway's locomotive works**. The
first match was a draw against a Basford side that December, and the first
ground was the Alexandra Recreation Ground beside Crewe station.

The name is Princess Alexandra of Denmark, Princess of Wales from 1863 — and
worth getting right, because the common version has her as one of Queen
Victoria's daughters. She was Victoria's **daughter-in-law**.

This site has drawn a careful line for several clubs between a works team and
a club that happens to be in a works town, and Crewe needs the line drawn
harder than most, because the railway company turns out to have been an
**antagonist rather than a patron**. Works employees founded the parent cricket
club in 1866. But when the football club split away in 1891 and turned
professional, **Francis Webb**, the LNWR's chief mechanical engineer and in
practice the man who ran Crewe Works, was strongly opposed — and the company
stated that it would **refuse to find employment in the works for any
professional football player**. Contemporary comment in the town was
unfavourable. Webb's successor as chief engineer was more relaxed about it and
became the club's president.

So: founded out of a works cricket club, then threatened with the loss of works
employment for the sin of being paid to play. "The Railwaymen" is the town's
name, not the company's.

## Trajectory

Crewe were founder members of the Football League's Second Division in 1892,
lost their place in 1896 after three re-election applications in four seasons,
and did not return until 1921. In the sixty-nine seasons this site records they
have **never played in the top division**, spent **eight seasons in the second
tier** — all between 1998 and 2006 — and forty-four in the fourth. Their
highest finish is **eleventh in the second tier, in 1997/98**.

**Dario Gradi managed the club from 1983 to 2007**, at the time the longest
unbroken tenure at any English league club, and built a youth system that
became the club's business model: develop players, sell them, use the money to
run the club. The BBC put the academy's transfer income at around £25m. Neil
Lennon went to Leicester for £750,000 in 1996, Dean Ashton to Norwich for £3m
in 2005, Nick Powell to Manchester United for an initial £3m in 2012. Four of
Crewe's promotions came under Gradi, two of them into the second tier.

**In April 2026 the club announced it would downgrade the academy from Category
Two to Category Three**, ending fourteen years at the higher grade, citing
rising costs, falling grant funding and the under-21 games programme.
Redundancies followed. The model that defined the club for a generation was
retired on cost grounds.

**Crewe have never had a point deducted and have never been through an
insolvency process.**

### The Bennell case and the Sheldon Review

**Barry Bennell was employed by Crewe Alexandra as a youth coach and youth
scout, from January 1985 to September 1989 and from August 1990 to January
1992.** Those dates are the Sheldon Review's.

He was convicted of child sexual offences on five occasions across three
decades — in Florida in 1994; at Chester Crown Court in 1998, when he pleaded
guilty to twenty-three counts and was sentenced to nine years; in 2015; at
Liverpool Crown Court in February 2018, when he was convicted at trial of
forty-three charges and sentenced to thirty-one years, the Court of Appeal
refusing to reduce it that June on the ground that the scale of the offending
made interference wrong; and at Chester Crown Court in October 2020, when he
admitted nine further offences and four years were added. He died in prison in
September 2023. This page records the convictions and the courts that made them
and nothing about the offences themselves.

**The Sheldon Review** — Clive Sheldon QC's independent review for the FA into
non-recent child sexual abuse in football, covering 1970 to 2005 and published
on 17 March 2021 — examined Crewe among eight professional clubs it identified
as having failed to act adequately on complaints or rumours. As reported, its
findings on Crewe were that the club **should have done more** to safeguard
young players during Bennell's two periods there, and that doing so **might
have led to his abuse of boys at the club being uncovered**; that **rumours
about his sexual interest in children circulated and were heard by some club
staff, in particular the coaching staff**; that **it is likely that three
directors discussed concerns about him which hinted at that interest**; and
that **there is no evidence that a senior police officer's advice to the club's
then chairman to keep a watching brief on him was heeded**.

On Dario Gradi the Review found in both directions: that he should have done
more to investigate or escalate what he heard, and that he knew boys regularly
stayed overnight with Bennell and ought to have checked on them — but also that
**he was not aware of any allegations of abuse, did not see anything done by
Bennell, and was not involved in a cover-up.**

**Two accounts of what the Review concluded are on the record and they do not
sit together.** The club's then chairman, resigning eight days after
publication, said he was satisfied with findings that **"the club did not have
any knowledge of Barry Bennell's heinous crimes."** The Review's findings as
reported are that rumours were heard by staff and that three directors likely
discussed concerns hinting at his sexual interest in children. This page sets
the two side by side rather than reconciling them, and notes that the second is
a description of what the Review found while the first is a characterisation
of it offered by the club.

What the club did in the years before is also on the record. In **November 2016
Crewe publicly committed to an independent review at the earliest
opportunity.** In **March 2018 it announced there would be no review**, citing
the conclusion of the police investigation, and denied a cover-up; the local MP
and the leader of Crewe Town Council both criticised the decision. **The club
never conducted its own review.** In March 2019 it settled a former player's
civil claim for an undisclosed sum, understood to have been paid by its
insurers, without an accompanying apology; its statement said it did not
consider it appropriate to comment on cases handled by insurers and that it
sincerely regretted the abuse Bennell committed. An out-of-court settlement
funded by insurers is not a finding of liability, and this page does not
present it as one.

The club's statement on the day after the Review was published said it was
**"truly sorry if there were in fact any warning signs that ought to have led
the club to do more."** The Offside Trust, founded by survivors, called that
belated, and said the emphasis on the club's lack of culpability made it sound
begrudging.

Dario Gradi was placed under FA interim suspension in December 2016, retired
from his director-of-football role in September 2019, and was **stripped of his
MBE in August 2023**. Whether he is banned from football is publicly disputed:
the FA's chief executive described him as effectively banned for life, and
Gradi has said he is suspended only from specified activities with players
under eighteen and was disappointed by the FA's public declaration. No
regulatory decision text is public. **No FA or EFL sanction was imposed on
Crewe Alexandra as a club.**

## Ownership & Finance

Crewe was, for four decades, one of the most closed ownership structures in
English football, and it opened only after the Review.

**John Bowler** joined the board in 1980 and was chairman from 1987. **Norman
Hassall** was the long-standing majority shareholder. In December 2017 the club
issued a claim against Hassall for £390,988, part of it relating to a 2006
agreement over a £1.325m loan he had taken from the club and part to money
alleged to have been taken between 2014 and 2016; **the claim was dropped in
May 2018** and no findings were made.

**The Hassall buyout completed in February 2020**, and the structure that
replaced it was deliberately designed against a repeat. His shares went to
supporters and six other investors, and the articles of association were
written so that **no single investor may hold more than 12.5%**. Alongside it,
the **Railwaymen Supporters' Society** raised **£250,000 in three months from
more than four hundred subscribers** through a community share issue, taking
roughly a seventh of the club, with influence deliberately decoupled from the
size of the investment.

**Bowler resigned on 25 March 2021, eight days after the Sheldon Review was
published**, after forty-one years on the board and thirty-four as chairman.
He apologised personally to survivors and apologised to supporters that the
club's name had been tarnished. He died the following February. His successor
as acting chairman said the club wanted to engage with survivors and learn very
painful lessons.

The club's ground and its car park sit inside a holding company along with the
football club, and it turned over £5.3m in the year to June 2025 against a
pre-tax loss of £1.2m.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Gresty Road has been home since 1906 and **the club owns it**, which after six
batches of this site's pages is worth saying plainly.

The main stand was a wooden structure from 1932, built after fire destroyed its
predecessor, and stood until it was replaced for the 1999/2000 season at a cost
of about **£5.2m**, seating 6,809 and housing the club's offices. The ground
went all-seater through the 1990s and holds **10,153**. The away end carries
the name of an ice-cream van manufacturer; the ground has been the **Mornflake
Stadium** since 2021, under a deal reported at around £500,000 with the Crewe
oat miller that had long sponsored the shirt.

Its relationship to the railway is proximity rather than tenancy. The ground
sits beside Crewe station and the railway lands, and one of its ends is called
the Railway End, but there is no record of the LNWR or its successors owning
the site or the club occupying works land. Given what the company said in 1891
about employing professional footballers, that is about what you would expect.
