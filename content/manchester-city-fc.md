## Origins

Manchester City began in 1880 as St Mark's (West Gorton), a church side in
one of the densest industrial districts of east Manchester — a place of
locomotive works and railway sheds, and one of the documented heartlands of
"scuttling," the territorial gang fighting by working-class youths that
troubled Manchester and Salford from the early 1870s until the end of the
century. St Mark's Church, under its rector Arthur Connell, ran the sort of
parish social provision that the Victorian rational-recreation movement
produced everywhere in cities like this one.

The story usually told from here is that the rector's daughter, Anna
Connell, founded the football club specifically to give young men in Gorton
something to do other than fight each other. It is repeated almost
universally, and it is the reason City are sometimes described as the only
professional club founded by a woman. The historical record does not
support it. Gary James, whose *The Emergence of Footballing Cultures:
Manchester, 1840-1919* is the peer-reviewed academic treatment of the
subject, has established that no publication before 1983 credits Anna
Connell with the club's formation, and that no contemporary report from the
1880s connects her to the football team at all. What she demonstrably did
found, in 1879, was a working men's meeting at St Mark's — a real and
substantial piece of parish work that appears to have been conflated
afterwards with the separate club that emerged around the same time.

That club came out of the church's cricket team. St Mark's had a cricket
side active through the 1870s, and the football team began as an off-season
activity for men who were already members of it, with the churchwarden
William Beastow prominent among them and William Sumner believed to have
been the first captain. The earliest match anyone has evidenced is 13
November 1880, a 2-1 home defeat to a Baptist church side from Macclesfield
— though James is careful to note that contemporary reports do not describe
it as a first game, and earlier fixtures against closer opposition are
likely to have happened.

So the scuttler context is real and the church context is real, but the
causal link between them and the founding of the football club is a later
interpretation rather than a documented intention, and the woman at the
centre of the popular version was running a different institution. Neither
extreme is right: Anna Connell did not found Manchester City, and she was
not irrelevant to the parish life it grew out of.

The name changed three more times before it settled. The club became West
Gorton and then Gorton A.F.C. in the early-to-mid 1880s, following dealings
with neighbouring Belle Vue; then Ardwick A.F.C. in 1887, when it moved to
a new ground at Hyde Road that lay in Ardwick rather than Gorton — the name
simply followed the geography. Ardwick joined the Football League's Second
Division in 1892 and ran out of money within two years. On 16 April 1894,
Manchester City Football Club, Limited was registered as a company, driven
by Joshua Parlby and with the explicit ambition of representing the whole
city rather than one district. The distinction matters: City is a new legal
entity incorporated in 1894, continuous with Ardwick in its players, ground
and support, but not in its corporate identity.

## Trajectory

The fall of the late 1990s is the part of City's history the modern club is
least often read through, and it was steep: two relegations in three
seasons took them out of the Premier League in 1996 and out of the second
tier in 1998, into the third for the first time in their history. It is
worth resisting the temptation to call this unique — thirty-eight clubs in
this database have played in both the top flight and the third tier since
1993/94, and several of them are also former champions. What was unusual
was the size of the crowd watching it happen, and the speed of the exit.

They went up at the first attempt, through the 1999 play-off final at
Wembley against Gillingham, a game City trailed 2-0 until stoppage time and
won on penalties in front of 76,935 people. A second promotion followed
immediately, then relegation again in 2001, then the Second Division title
in 2002 with 99 points. City have been in the Premier League continuously
since 2003 — but the four seasons before that contain three promotions and
two relegations, and the club that the Abu Dhabi purchase arrived at in
2008 had been in the third tier within the previous decade.

## Ownership & Finance

Peter Swales's twenty-year chairmanship ended in 1993 under a sustained
supporter campaign, and Francis Lee — a former City player turned
businessman — took a controlling stake in February 1994. David Bernstein
chaired the club through the recovery from 1998 to 2003 and negotiated the
stadium move; a 1999 rights issue made John Wardle and David Makin, the
co-founders of JD Sports, the largest shareholders.

In July 2007 the former Thai prime minister Thaksin Shinawatra completed a
purchase of the club for a reported £81.6m, having been cleared by the
Premier League's owners' and directors' test. Fourteen months later, on 1
September 2008, he sold to the Abu Dhabi United Group, owned by Sheikh
Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, for a reported figure of around £200m.
Khaldoon Al Mubarak became chairman that month and remains in post.

City Football Group was created in 2013 as a holding company above
Manchester City, coinciding with the founding of New York City FC and
followed by the acquisition and rebranding of Melbourne City in 2014. The
model is common ownership across leagues and continents, sharing scouting,
coaching methods, commercial infrastructure and player pathways; the group
has since taken stakes in clubs including Girona, Palermo, Troyes, Mumbai
City, Lommel, Yokohama F. Marinos, Montevideo City Torque and Bahia,
though the exact roster and the size of each holding change often enough
that any list dates quickly. Two minority investments in CFG itself are
documented: a consortium led by China Media Capital paid US$400m for 13% in
December 2015, valuing the group at around US$3bn, and Silver Lake paid
US$500m for just over 10% in November 2019, at a US$4.8bn valuation.

City's £715.0m turnover in 2023/24 is the highest figure anywhere in this
database. The following year it fell to £694.1m — the first non-pandemic
year-on-year decline under the current ownership, with broadcast income
down from £295m to £279m — and the club posted a pre-tax loss of £9.9m,
its first since 2019/20. Even so, the wage bill of £408m represents 59% of
turnover, among the lowest ratios in the Premier League and roughly a third
below the level at which clubs in the divisions below routinely operate.

Two regulatory matters have concluded and can be stated precisely. In July
2020 the Court of Arbitration for Sport set aside the two-year European ban
UEFA's Club Financial Control Body had imposed that February. The panel
found that the charge of disguising equity funding as sponsorship
contributions was not established, that a number of the alleged breaches
fell outside the five-year limitation period, and that the club had failed
to cooperate with the investigation — a finding it upheld. Judging a
competition ban disproportionate for failure to cooperate alone, it reduced
the €30m fine to €10m. Separately, in October 2024 a Premier League
arbitration tribunal ruled on the club's challenge to the League's
Associated Party Transaction rules, finding the exclusion of shareholder
loans unlawful along with amendments made in March 2024, and setting aside
two 2023 decisions as procedurally unfair; a February 2025 follow-up
declared the 2021-2024 APT rules void, and the parties later settled, with
a revised regime now in place.

A third matter is unresolved. In February 2023 the Premier League referred
the club to an independent commission over alleged breaches of its
financial regulations covering 2009 to 2018, commonly reported as 115
charges. The club denies all of them. A hearing ran from September to
December 2024. As of August 2026 no decision has been published and no
sanction imposed.

## Infrastructure & Environment

City used six pitches in eight years before settling at Hyde Road in 1887,
their first permanent home and the ground that gave them the Ardwick name.
It was cramped, boxed in by railway land, and in November 1920 its main
stand burned down, destroying the club's records and accounts along with it
— one reason the origins story above rests on reconstructed evidence rather
than a continuous archive.

Maine Road followed in 1923: a Moss Side brickworks site bought for £5,500
and built by Sir Robert McAlpine, who were putting up Wembley at the same
time, for around £100,000 in roughly 300 days. It opened with a capacity
near 80,000 and was nicknamed the Wembley of the North. On 3 March 1934 it
held 84,569 people for an FA Cup tie against Stoke City, which remains the
record attendance at any English club ground.

The move to the current stadium is the most consequential institutional
fact about the modern club, and it is a public-money story. The ground was
built as the main venue for the 2002 Commonwealth Games at a cost of around
£110m, of which roughly £77m came from Sport England through National
Lottery funds and the balance from Manchester City Council. After the Games
the running track was removed, the pitch lowered and a new lower tier dug
in, converting a 38,000-seat athletics venue into a football ground of
around 48,000. City moved in for 2003/04.

They do not own it. Manchester City Council does, and the club holds a
250-year lease on a fully repairing basis — it takes the stadium's revenues
and carries its operating, maintenance and capital costs. The terms were
renegotiated in October 2010: an earlier revenue-share arrangement, under
which the council took a proportion of gate income above a threshold pegged
to Maine Road's capacity and received roughly £2m a year, was replaced by a
flat index-linked annual fee of around £3m, and the stadium's naming rights
were transferred to the club. Etihad Airways signed a ten-year agreement
the following July covering those naming rights, shirt sponsorship and
funding toward a new training complex; the City Football Academy opened on
around eighty acres of adjacent derelict land in 2014, a piece of east
Manchester regeneration brokered through the council.

The stadium has grown twice since. A third tier on the South Stand opened
in August 2015, taking capacity to roughly 55,000. A £300m North Stand
expansion approved by the council in July 2023 added around 7,900 seats
along with a hotel, museum, club shop and a covered fan zone, bringing
capacity to about 61,900 for 2025/26. It is an unusual arrangement to have
produced that: a club among the richest in the world, expanding a stadium
it leases from a local authority, on a site the public paid to build.
