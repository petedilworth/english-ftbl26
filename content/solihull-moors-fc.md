## Origins

Solihull Moors were formed in **2007**, which makes them the youngest club on
this site by some distance, and the story of the merger is a story about two
grounds changing hands twice.

**Solihull Borough** were founded in **1953**, originally as Lincoln FC, and
played at Widney Lane. **Moor Green** were founded in **1901** by players from a
cricket club and played at **The Moorlands** in Hall Green from 1930. They were
local rivals.

In **1989**, in financial difficulty, **Borough sold Widney Lane to property
developers**. A planned relocation fell through, and Borough became **tenants of
Moor Green at The Moorlands** — while using the proceeds to buy a former golf
driving range on Damson Parkway. **Damson Park opened in August 1999** and
Borough left the groundshare.

Then it reversed. In **January 2005 The Moorlands was destroyed by arson** — the
grandstand, the changing rooms and the social club — with a second suspected
arson attack that April. Moor Green were homeless, and **ground-shared at Damson
Park** for two seasons. Landlord and tenant had swapped places.

In **January 2007** Moor Green's directors concluded that rebuilding The
Moorlands or developing a new ground was **financially unviable**, and that
continuing meant merging. They held talks with three clubs before applying to
the FA. On **30 March 2007** the merger was confirmed; the new club was formed
on 1 June and announced on 10 July.

The placement followed the higher-placed predecessor rather than the one with
the ground: **Solihull Moors took Moor Green's Conference North place**, and
Borough's Southern League place lapsed. Every record-keeper treats the merged
club as new — the Football Club History Database keeps three separate entries —
and this site's data, which begins with Solihull Moors in 2016/17 and holds no
row for either predecessor, does the same.

The name is a compound: Solihull from one, Moors from the other. So is the
kit. The home colours are yellow and blue, and **the away kit alternates each
season between Borough's red and white and Moor Green's two blues** — a merged
club rotating its parents' colours, which is a neater solution than most merged
clubs manage. The Moorlands was demolished and is now a housing estate of
eighty-one homes called, inevitably, The Moorlands.

## Trajectory

Nine seasons in the sixth tier, then **National League North champions in
2015/16** by nine points, and the fifth tier ever since — **eleven consecutive
seasons, no gaps, and no promotion or relegation recorded in this site's data at
all.**

That last figure needs handling honestly. This site records tiers one to five,
so Solihull's promotion from the sixth tier is invisible here, and their zero-
and-zero is a coverage artefact rather than a fact about the club. It is worth
noticing who else it applies to: filtering for clubs with no promotion, no
relegation and at least ten recorded seasons returns exactly four — **Arsenal,
Everton, Eastleigh and Solihull Moors.** The first two have never moved because
they have never left the top flight; the last two because the floor of the data
sits underneath them.

What is real is how close they have come and how often. **Second in 2018/19 on
86 points**, beaten in the play-off semi-final. **Third in 2021/22 on 87** — the
club record — and then the **2022 National League play-off final at the London
Stadium**, where they led Grimsby Town at half-time and lost 2–1 to a goal in
the 119th minute, in front of 22,897.

Then 2024, which was crueller. Fifth in the league, they reached the **play-off
final at Wembley on 5 May and drew 2–2 with Bromley**, who won 4–3 on penalties
and entered the Football League for the first time in 132 years. **Six days
later, on 11 May, they were back at Wembley for the FA Trophy final, drew 2–2
with Gateshead, and lost 5–4 on penalties.** Two Wembley finals in six days,
both drawn, both lost on penalties, both to clubs whose own pages sit on this
site.

There is no regulatory footnote to any of it, and that is the finding.
**Damson Park was awarded FA Grade A status in April 2017**, and the fact that
Solihull were permitted into the play-offs in 2019, 2022 and 2024 is itself
proof that the grade and the 500-covered-seat requirement were certified by 31
March in each of those years. With a capacity around five thousand and roughly
2,100 seats, the ground clears the EFL's entry thresholds on paper. **Had they
won either final they would have gone up.** On this site, where several clubs
were stopped by their stadium, Solihull are a club stopped only by penalties.

Their two best seasons also ran into the wrong era. 87 points and 86 points
would have won the division comfortably in most years; they arrived in a decade
when National League champions were posting 111, 108, 107 and 106.

**Solihull Moors have never had a point deducted**, and no insolvency event
appears anywhere in the record.

## Ownership & Finance

The corporate structure is unusual and worth setting out, because it says
something the league table does not.

The football club sits inside a **community interest company**, which is owned
by a second CIC, which is owned by **Solihull Moors Group Limited**. The
shareholders holding more than ten per cent are **Darryl Eales** — formerly
chairman of Oxford United — and an investment vehicle; his partner in the
takeover was the automotive-trade businessman **Calvin Barnett**. The two are
reported to have put millions into the squad and into redeveloping Damson Park,
including new stands and a 3G pitch for community use.

So this is **benefactor funding wrapped in a community-benefit legal form**.
That combination is rare on this site and it cuts both ways: the CIC structure
puts a brake on anyone extracting value from the club, and it does not change
the fact that the money comes from two people.

One detail hints at the deeper lineage. The intermediate holding company was
**incorporated on 31 March 1970** — a date that fits Solihull Borough's history
and nothing in Moor Green's or Solihull Moors' own. The likely reading is that
the merged club is a new sporting entity built on Borough's corporate shell and
Borough's ground, but occupying Moor Green's place in the pyramid. That is
consistent with everything else here, and it is not confirmed, so this page
notes it rather than asserting it.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Damson Park was built on a former golf driving range that Solihull Borough
bought in 1989 with the money from selling Widney Lane, and it opened in
**August 1999**. Capacity is about five thousand with roughly 2,100 seats,
including a full-length double-decker main stand. It has carried four names in
twelve years, the current one under a five-season deal.

Two things about it are worth more than the specification.

**It is a women's football ground as much as a men's.** Birmingham City's women's
team have played home matches at Damson Park since 2014, an arrangement
announced in October 2013 and running through their WSL seasons; the ground also
hosted Birmingham's reserve fixtures before reserve football was reorganised.

**And it may not be there forever, for a reason that has nothing to do with
football.** Damson Park sits next to Jaguar Land Rover's Lode Lane plant, which
employs around five thousand people and has proposals to grow. Solihull
Council's Local Plan describes the plant's future as critical to the borough,
and reporting in 2022 stated that the authority is able to require the club to
relocate to enable expansion, with a corresponding obligation to help it move.
The club said publicly that it could move; the council leader then wrote to the
chairman and said publicly that it was not eyeing up the stadium.

Which is where a club formed out of two ground disasters currently stands: on a
site it built after selling one ground, next to a car plant, in a Local Plan.
