## Origins

The club dates itself to **1899**, when **Brumby Hall FC** — already playing on
the Old Show Ground — merged with a second local side and took the name
Scunthorpe United. In **1910** it amalgamated again, with **North Lindsey
United**, and became **Scunthorpe & Lindsey United**; the "and Lindsey" was not
dropped until the close season of **1958**, in the same summer the club was
promoted to the Second Division. The name shortened at the club's high-water
mark, forty-eight years after the merger that produced it.

The nickname is the town's, and the distinction matters. Scunthorpe grew from
farmland into an iron and steel centre after ironstone was found east of the
town in the mid-nineteenth century and the railway reached it in the 1860s —
but **Scunthorpe United was not a works team**. The steel company's side was
**Appleby Frodingham FC**, a separate club with its own history. Nor were the
Iron always the Iron: supporters knew the team as **"the Knuts"** from around
the 1910s until about 1950, and the ironworking nickname arrived at almost
exactly the moment the club got its Football League place. The badge — a gloved
fist gripping an iron bar — came later still.

## Trajectory

**Scunthorpe were elected to the Football League in 1950, and the club they
beat to it was Wigan Athletic.**

The League expanded from 88 clubs to 92 that summer. The bottom four were
re-elected without a ballot and four new members were voted in, regionally:
Shrewsbury Town and Scunthorpe & Lindsey United into the Third Division North,
Gillingham and Colchester United into the South — the same election that
appears on three other clubs' pages on this site, from their side of it.
Scunthorpe needed three ballots. Shrewsbury went in first; Wigan and Workington
tied on the opening round with Scunthorpe on 17; the second round left
Scunthorpe and Wigan level on 15; the third went **30–18 to Scunthorpe**. Wigan
Athletic waited until 1978.

The peak came quickly and then stopped: Third Division North champions in
1957/58, six seasons in the Second Division, and **fourth in 1961/62**, which
is still the best finish in this site's record of the club. In 1962 they became
the first fourth-tier side to reach a major English final, losing the League
Cup to Norwich over two legs — a distinction they held alone until Bradford
City matched it in 2013.

Two second-tier spells in the 2000s, in 2007/08 and 2009–11, were the last
time the club was anywhere near the top half of the pyramid. Then:

**2021/22.** Twenty-fourth in League Two on **26 points, from four wins in
forty-six matches, with a goal difference of −61**. Relegation ended
seventy-two consecutive years of Football League membership.

**2022/23.** Relegated again, from the National League — though not, as is
often written, in bottom place. This site's table has Scunthorpe **twenty-third
on 34 points**, with Maidstone bottom on 25.

The recovery took two attempts at the same obstacle: second in National League
North in 2023/24 and beaten in the play-off semi-final on penalties, then
second again in 2024/25 and up through the final. Fifth in the National League
in 2025/26, and out in the play-off semi-final at Rochdale.

**Scunthorpe have never had a point deducted.** Where the record shows an
unusual points total it is because points were *given*: two awarded in 1973/74
when Exeter City did not fulfil a fixture, and three in September 2025 when an
abandoned match against Wealdstone was awarded under National League rule 8.38.
The club has also never been in administration. What it had instead was a
**winding-up petition from HMRC in January 2023 over about £190,000**, settled
within weeks, and a transfer embargo.

## Ownership & Finance

The thing that happened to Scunthorpe United happened while they were still in
the Football League, a year before the relegation everyone remembers.

**In May 2021 the chairman told supporters that Glanford Park, the training
ground, the car park and the surrounding land had been transferred to
Coolsilk Property and Investment Ltd — another company he owned — in exchange
for writing off £11m of loans he had made to the club.** It was a related-party
transfer, not a sale on the open market. He said the arrangement would protect
the club for **"999 years"**, a figure later amended to 99. The amendment is
more revealing than either number.

What followed ran in a straight line. Relegation in April 2022. An HMRC
winding-up petition in January 2023, and a takeover days later by a buyer who
settled it — while **Coolsilk kept the ground**. By August 2023 the dispute
over Glanford Park was heading for trial; by September the new owner had put
the club up for sale, withdrawn funding, and become the subject of an FA
investigation, disclosing that he had served nine months of a two-year sentence
for fraud as the former chairman of another club and saying the convictions
were spent and that he had passed the Owners' and Directors' Test. Staff went
unpaid. **Supporters raised more than £50,000, later reported above £70,000,
through fan podcasts and supporters' groups, to cover wages** — which is not
the same thing as buying a club, and should not be described as if it were.

With the ground's owner demanding vacant possession, Scunthorpe played their
last home match at Glanford Park on **7 October 2023** and moved the rest of
the season's fixtures thirteen miles to **Gainsborough Trinity**.

Then it reversed, in two moves. A club director and former commercial manager
bought the controlling stake in **October 2023**. And on **29 November 2023 the
ground was bought back from Coolsilk for around £3m by The New Show Ground
Community Interest Company** — a not-for-profit set up by a Scunthorpe-born
barrister, reportedly incorporated in forty-eight hours, with funders including
North Lincolnshire Council. The structure is the point: **the ground is held by
a community interest company and can never again be owned by a single
individual**. In June 2024 shares were allocated to three more people, leaving
four equal owners of the football club.

So the club and its ground are now in different hands on purpose, having been
in different hands by accident. The Football Supporters' Association has used
the case as its example of why the owners' and directors' test needed to be
stronger.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**The first cantilever stand at any British football ground was built in
Scunthorpe, out of Scunthorpe steel, in 1958** — designed by the chief designer
of the United Steel Structural Company, built by that company in under five
months to replace a stand lost to fire, and opened by a works boss that August.
Sheffield Wednesday's North Stand of 1961 was the second, and the first to run
the full length of a pitch, which is why both clubs can claim a first without
contradicting each other. The Old Show Ground's record attendance was **23,935**
against Portsmouth in the FA Cup in January 1954.

Thirty years later the club demolished it. Contracts on the site were signed in
**August 1987** and the club left on 7 May 1988; the land went to the **Argyll
Group**, which built a Safeway there — the store passed to Sainsbury's in 2004
— for a figure reported at £2.3m, though £2.5m also appears in the record. The
proceeds paid for the new ground **and cleared the club's debts**. This was an
asset sale, not a benefactor, and it is why Scunthorpe moved when almost nobody
else was moving.

**Glanford Park, opened in August 1988 at a cost of around £2.5m, was the first
purpose-built new ground for a Football League club since Southend's Roots Hall
in 1955** — a gap of thirty-three years, and the start of the trend that
emptied a generation of Victorian grounds. It predates Hillsborough by eight
months and the Taylor Report by two years, so it was not built in response to
either; the reasons were commercial.

The name has never meant what it appears to. **Glanford Borough Council bought
the naming rights for ten years; it did not build or fund the stadium.** Three
sponsors have followed — the Sands Venue Stadium from 2019, the Attis Arena
from January 2024 — and the ground record is 9,077, against Manchester United
in 2010.

The steelworks and the football club are usually written as one story and they
are not. Employment at the plant fell from about 30,000 in the 1950s to around
4,000 by 2015, through Corus, Tata, Greybull and Jingye; **Parliament was
recalled on a Saturday in April 2025 — only the sixth such sitting since 1945 —
to pass emergency legislation keeping the country's last blast furnaces
running**. But the club's worst years, 2019 to 2023, were years in which the
steelworks survived, and the steelworks' near-death came the same spring the
club was climbing out of the sixth tier. The documented cause of Scunthorpe's
collapse is what happened to its ground. The relationship the record actually
shows runs the other way: in 2025 it was the football club **selling £10
tickets to British Steel employees**.
