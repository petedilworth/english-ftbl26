## Origins

Salford City is a club whose name is younger than its nickname. It was founded
in **1940 as Salford Central**, a side for local boys in the Eccles and
District leagues; became **Salford Amateurs in 1963**, which is where the
Ammies comes from; and became **Salford City only in 1989**.

For most of that time it was a very small football club. It won the Lancashire
Amateur Cup three times in the 1970s, moved up through the Cheshire County and
North West Counties leagues, and reached the Northern Premier League's first
division in 2008, where it stayed for six seasons. In the season the club was
bought it finished twelfth of twenty-two.

**Crowds hovered around a hundred.** That is the number the contrast rests on,
and it is worth holding on to.

## Trajectory

**Four promotions in five seasons**, which is the accurate version of a claim
usually inflated to five:

- **2014/15** — champions of the Northern Premier League's first division;
- **2015/16** — up through the play-offs, having finished third on 90 points
  behind a Darlington side that took 104;
- **2016/17** — *no* promotion, beaten in the National League North play-offs;
- **2017/18** — champions of National League North;
- **2018/19** — third in the National League, and up through the play-off
  final at Wembley, 3–0 against AFC Fylde, into the Football League for the
  first time in the club's history.

Gary Neville's own words on the pitch that afternoon are the cleanest source
for the correct figure: *"We had an aggressive plan of four promotions in
eight years and we have done it in five."*

**Then it stopped.** Salford have played eight seasons in League Two without
going up: tenth, eighth, tenth, seventh, twentieth, eighth, and — in
2025/26 — **fourth on 81 points, and a play-off final at Wembley lost 3–0 to
Notts County**, who also appear in this batch. The stated ambition at takeover
was the Championship within fifteen years. Seven of those have gone.

There is no points deduction anywhere in the club's record.

One caveat on the record above: this site's standings pick Salford up only in
2019, at the fifth tier. **The four non-league promotions cannot be checked
against our own data** and rest on external sources, which is worth knowing
before reading them in the same voice as the League Two figures.

## Ownership & Finance

**The Class of '92 bought Salford City in March 2014, and Peter Lim bought half
of it from them six months later.** The two transactions are routinely
described as one; they were not. Gary Neville, Phil Neville, Ryan Giggs, Paul
Scholes and Nicky Butt agreed the takeover first, and in **September 2014 the
Singaporean businessman Peter Lim took 50%**, leaving the five with ten per
cent each. Neither price was ever disclosed.

David Beckham did not arrive until **January 2019**, and bought his ten per
cent **from Lim**, not from the five.

The shareholding has since turned over completely. Neville bought **Lim's
40% in August 2024**, making him briefly the majority owner and ending Lim's
decade at the club. In **May 2025 a nine-shareholder consortium took over** —
Beckham and Neville retained, Kelly and Davies as board co-chairs, with
others — and **Phil Neville, Scholes, Giggs and Butt ceased to be
shareholders**, keeping roles rather than stakes. In **December 2025 AIG
became the largest shareholder**, in a partnership running to 2030. So of the
five men the club is still known for, two remain owners.

The funding model has been consistent throughout and it is benefactor funding.
Turnover has run in the £3–4m range against annual losses of £3–5m: **over
£4.7m lost in the year to June 2021**, £3.2m in 2022, **over £5.3m in 2024**,
and **cumulative losses above £28m since 2014**. Lim's exit was followed
immediately by a public search for a strategic partner, which is what the
accounts would predict.

The sharpest measure of what that money meant is on the record from someone
else. In July 2018, when Salford were still a National League club, they
signed a striker from the Scottish Premiership, and **Accrington Stanley's
owner responded by revealing that Accrington's entire operating budget for
their League One campaign was £1.1m** and accusing Salford of buying their way
into the League. Two divisions below, and outspending them.

**And a good deal of it happened on camera.** *Class of '92: Out of Their
League* ran for two series on BBC One from October 2015, and a Sky Sports
strand has run most years since December 2017 — including a dedicated piece on
Neville negotiating transfers and dealing with agents. The documented effect
is revenue in and attention in: while other clubs in a crowded region
advertise by flyer and word of mouth, Salford City gets national coverage. What
is *not* documented, despite being widely assumed, is that the television
exposure raised the club's wage bill or the prices sellers charged it. Nobody
at the club, at Sky or at the BBC has said so, and this page will not.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Moor Lane was rebuilt to make the Football League possible.** Redevelopment
began in January 2017 and took the capacity from **1,600 to 5,108** — a
threshold, not an aspiration, since a ground below the League's requirement
would have stopped the climb whatever the team did. It cost a reported £5m,
funded by the owners with Peter Lim named as the principal source, and was
renamed the **Peninsula Stadium** under a sponsorship deal in October 2017.

Put that beside **Broadhurst Park**, three miles away, opened in 2015 at
£6.3m and funded through a community share scheme that raised around £2m from
FC United's own supporters. Two new grounds in Manchester non-league football,
two years apart, one paid for by a billionaire and one by a share issue to the
people who stand in it. Neither club has said a word against the other, and
this page records that as a finding rather than a gap: there is no
documented rivalry between them — no feud, no banner, no incident on either
side — only a contrast the press draws repeatedly and a single real
grievance, which is about attention. FC United and Stockport County receive
nothing like Salford's national coverage, and that is a consequence of who
owns Salford rather than of anything it has won.

**The colours were changed by the owners and changed back by a vote.** In 2014
the tangerine and black the club had worn were replaced with red and white,
the lion on the badge recoloured and the word "Ammies" removed; it was
presented to supporters at a meeting after the fact, and the response included
chants of "we are tangerine" and a boycott. In **October 2025 the club ran a
heritage consultation and 77% of those voting chose to go back to orange**,
with 72% backing a new badge, both from the start of 2026/27.

This site has written about Cardiff City's blue-to-red rebrand as an ownership
decision, and the same frame applies here with an unusual ending: the reversal
was as much an owners' decision as the original change, because the owners are
the people who called the vote. What supporters got back, they got back by
being asked.
