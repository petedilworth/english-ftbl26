## Origins

Founded on **27 August 1895 as Yeovil Casuals**, playing at the Pen Mill
Athletic Ground; Yeovil Town by 1907; **Yeovil & Petters United** after a
merger with the works side of **Petters Ltd**, the town's oil-engine makers,
whose aircraft offshoot became Westland and the town's dominant employer; and
Yeovil Town again from 1946/47.

The nickname is an industry that has gone. Yeovil was a centre of **glove
making** in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and the club are **the
Glovers**. The ground it plays on now sits on Lufton Way, next to the aircraft
works — so the club is named for the industry that preceded the one it lives
beside.

## Trajectory

**Yeovil Town have played one season in the second tier**, in 2013/14, finishing
twenty-fourth on 37 points. It is the highest level the club has ever reached,
and it came **118 years after they were founded**.

Everything below it has moved constantly. Forty-two recorded seasons here:
twenty-five completed in the fifth tier, nine in the third, six in the fourth,
one in the second, and six missing entirely. **Three promotions and six
relegations** — though the promotions figure is a coverage artefact and should
not be printed as a fact, because the **2023/24 National League South title is
not in this data at all**, and nor are the returns of 1988 and 1998.

The way up was promotion, not election. Yeovil won the **2002/03 Conference with
95 points, a seventeen-point margin, 100 goals and an unbeaten home record** —
this site's match data confirms the home record independently, twenty-one games
without defeat — and went up automatically. Election had ended sixteen years
earlier. Ten seasons later they beat Brentford 2–1 at Wembley in the **League
One play-off final on 19 May 2013** and reached the Championship.

The way down was quicker, and both drops have an off-pitch cause on the record.
**Out of the Football League in 2018/19**, after sixteen years, in a season
during which **multiple takeovers collapsed** — an American-led deal confirmed
in February and called off, a consortium bid that failed in May. **Out of the
National League in 2022/23**, into regionalised football for the first time
since 1997, in a season in which **staff were not paid at the end of April**
while a takeover pending since March remained uncompleted. They came back at the
first attempt as champions.

And there is one more thing in the record that was not in this site's data
until this page was written. **Yeovil were deducted a point in the 1994/95
Conference.** Our table had them twenty-first on 38 with Stafford Rangers
twenty-second on the same total; the published table has **Yeovil bottom on 37**
and Stafford above them. The deduction is recorded by three independent sources
and none of them gives a reason, so this page does not invent one. Both clubs
went down either way — but the club had been described as never having had a
point deducted, and it had.

## Ownership & Finance

This is a page about a ground being moved out of a football club, twice, thirty
years apart.

**The first move nearly ended them.** Negotiations to sell the old Huish began
in **January 1985** and were backed by shareholders in 1987. The sale realised
**about £2.5m, plus a further £400,000 from the developer** described as a
gesture of goodwill — **£2.8m in all**. By May 1989 the cost of the new stadium
had risen to **£3.5m**. Yeovil moved into a ground they could not pay for, about
**£700,000 short before a ball was kicked**, and by 1991 the chairman who drove
the deal had been forced out and replaced by a board led by the Supporters' Club
chairman. The old Huish site is a Tesco in the town centre.

**The second move was quieter and is still running.** In **2010** the chairman
and owner created **Yeovil Town Holdings Limited** and transferred into it the
freehold of **the land bordering the stadium** — the astroturf, the car parks,
the top pitches, the land behind one terrace. The land under the stadium itself
stayed with the football club company. Shareholders approved it; the two men
held 92 per cent of the shares. It was presented as enabling development for the
club's benefit.

Then the stadium followed. Agreed with **South Somerset District Council in
November 2020** and **completed in May 2022**, Huish Park was **sold to the
council for £2.8m** to cover costs run up during the pandemic, with a **thirty-
year lease back** and an **option to repurchase the freehold at £2.789m subject
to indexation**, sized so that the council recovers its outlay. Rent began at
**£195,000 a year** and rose at an indexed review in May 2025 to **£229,130** —
up 17.5 per cent in one step.

Three details make this more than a sale-and-leaseback. **The council that
bought it no longer exists** — South Somerset was abolished into the unitary
Somerset Council in 2023, so the counterparty changed mid-option. **The buy-back
option is held by Yeovil Town Holdings, not by the football club** — the same
holding company created in 2010 to hold land away from the club. And **the
option has already been extended once**, from 17 May 2026 to **17 May 2028**, by
a council executive decision in September 2025.

Exeter's page on this site records a council buying a ground and leasing it
back after a sale to a housebuilder. Yeovil's is the variant: **sold by a
private owner rather than lost in an insolvency, with the right to buy it back
sitting in a company that is not the football club.**

The ownership itself has turned over four times in six years — bought from the
long-standing owners in 2019, sold in **May 2023** to a locally-run group after a
supporter-led bid failed to complete, and sold again in **May 2025** to a
Dubai-based businessman through **Inflection Holding Ltd**. Accounts filed in
March 2025 showed the football club **lost almost £2.8m** in the 2023/24
promotion season, kept afloat by the then owner selling assets from a property
portfolio, with a stated **material uncertainty over going concern**. The
company also carries a Sport England loan of up to £801,538.

**Yeovil have never been in administration.** The insolvency that appears in
searches belongs to Yeovil Town Ladies, a separate entity, in 2019.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The old **Huish Athletic Ground**, used from 1920 to 1990, had a pitch that
sloped from one touchline to the other. The figure most often given is **eight
feet**, though some accounts specify six feet across the halfway line and eight
corner to corner; no primary measurement has surfaced, and the honest version
gives both. On it, on **29 January 1949**, Yeovil beat **Sunderland 2–1** in the
FA Cup fourth round in front of a record 17,000 — the most famous result in
non-league history for a generation.

**Huish Park** opened on 4 August 1990 and is a different kind of object: a
9,565-capacity ground with **5,212 seats in two side stands and terracing behind
both goals**, one of which is still **uncovered**. The only significant
structural change in thirty-six years was a roof over the home terrace in 2001.
A 2011 scheme for a 3,500-seat stand tied to a retail park was dropped over
funding, and no relocation proposal has ever followed it. This is a ground that
has been finished since the year it opened.

The geography is real but needs stating precisely. **In the 2013/14 Championship
Yeovil were the most remote club in the division**, at a mean 155 miles from
their twenty-three opponents — though only narrowly, less than a mile ahead of
Bournemouth, and despite having Bournemouth 39 miles away. In the 2025/26
National League the same 155-mile figure puts them **fifth**, behind Truro,
Carlisle, Gateshead and Hartlepool. Yeovil are a long way from most of English
football, and they are not the furthest.

One thing that follows from it: **Yeovil are Truro City's nearest divisional
opponent**, 117 miles away, which is a fact about Truro's isolation and a fact
about Yeovil's too.
