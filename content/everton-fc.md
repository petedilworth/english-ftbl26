## Origins

Everton was founded in 1878 as St Domingo's F.C., formed by members of St Domingo's Methodist New Connexion Chapel in the Everton district of Liverpool; the club renamed itself Everton the following November as membership grew beyond the chapel congregation.

The club moved into Anfield in 1884, renting the ground from local brewer and club president John Houlding. Rent rose from £100 to £250 a year by 1892, and Houlding pushed to convert the club into a limited company with broadly issued shares, terms a majority of directors — led by George Mahon — rejected as a threat to members' control; the board voted Houlding out in March 1892 and left to build Goodison Park nearby. Houlding kept Anfield and founded Liverpool F.C. that same year. How much of the split was a straightforward landlord-tenant dispute over rent and control, and how much reflected friction between Houlding's brewery business and Everton's Methodist, temperance-minded membership, is still debated among club historians; what's certain is that Everton's new ground banned alcohol sales and the club refused brewery financing as a matter of policy from that point on.

The "Toffees" nickname has two competing folk explanations, neither confirmed by primary records: Ye Ancient Everton Toffee House, a sweet shop predating the move to Goodison, and Mother Noblett's toffee shop near the new ground, credited with starting the "Toffee Lady" tradition of throwing sweets to fans before kick-off — a ritual that continues today, and about the only concrete evidence either story left behind.

## Trajectory

Chronic underinvestment through the Bill Kenwright chairmanship gave way, after Farhad Moshiri's 2016 takeover, to heavy debt-funded spending without matching results: the club recorded losses in effectively every season from 2016 to 2023, reported cumulatively at well over half a billion pounds. That overspend caught up with the club directly in 2023-24. An independent commission docked Everton 10 points in November 2023 — then the largest sporting sanction in Premier League history — for breaching Profitability and Sustainability Rules in the assessment period to 2021-22; an appeal in February 2024 found the original sanction hadn't properly weighed comparable precedent and reduced it to 6 points. A second, overlapping breach for the period to 2022-23 brought a further 2-point deduction that April. Even with an 8-point handicap across the season, Everton finished 15th — a margin several financial analysts read as the deductions coming close to costing the club its Premier League place outright, on top of a 2022-23 season that had already gone to the final day of the campaign to confirm survival.

## Ownership & Finance

Bill Kenwright bought a controlling 68% stake in 1999 and chaired the club from 2004 until his death in October 2023; under his tenure Everton was widely characterised as chronically undercapitalised relative to its rivals, reliant on player sales and short-term financing rather than sustained investment. Farhad Moshiri took a 49.9% stake in February 2016 for £87.5m and went on to invest an estimated £750m in loans and equity over the following seven years, funding transfers and wages that produced record annual losses rather than sustained improvement — the financial pattern behind the PSR breaches above.

Moshiri agreed to sell his roughly 94% stake to 777 Partners in September 2023 for a reported £550m, but the deal collapsed by mid-2024 after 777 repeatedly failed to provide the audited financial statements required by the Financial Conduct Authority and the Premier League's ownership test, amid wider reporting that the firm's acquisition spree had been funded through its insurance and reinsurance businesses in ways regulators and reporters were separately scrutinising. The Friedkin Group — the American ownership group behind Italian club Roma, led by Dan Friedkin — completed a takeover in December 2024, acquiring a 98.8% stake for a reported £400m-plus. TFG paid off Moshiri's costlier existing stadium loan and converted roughly £450m of his shareholder loans into equity, then arranged a fresh £350m refinancing package through JP Morgan for the stadium debt in January 2025.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Goodison Park opened in 1892 and is often described as the first purpose-built major football stadium in England — Celtic Park in Scotland opened just days earlier, so the claim holds for England specifically rather than Britain as a whole. It served as Everton's home for 133 years.

The club's move to a new stadium on the former Bramley-Moore Dock, a disused dock site on the Mersey waterfront, broke ground in August 2021 and opened in February 2025 ahead of a full relocation for the 2025-26 season, with capacity set at 52,769 after a post-construction compliance audit. Naming rights were sold to law firm Hill Dickinson. Construction cost estimates range from £750m to £800m depending on what is counted, financed in large part by the refinancing package The Friedkin Group arranged after completing its takeover.
