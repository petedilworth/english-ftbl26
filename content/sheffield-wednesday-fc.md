## Origins

The club is named after a day of the week because the men who founded it were
only free on one.

The Wednesday Cricket Club was formed by Sheffield tradesmen whose shops shut
for a Wednesday half-holiday, and on **4 September 1867** it met at the
Adelphi Hotel and resolved to add a football section to keep the players
together through the winter. It is worth resisting the detail usually
attached to this — that they were cutlers. The trade specificity is a later
inference; what the record supports is tradesmen with a particular afternoon
off, which is a more interesting fact anyway, because it means the club's
name is a piece of nineteenth-century employment law.

The Adelphi has a claim to be the most productive building in English sport.
Yorkshire County Cricket Club was founded there in 1863, Sheffield Wednesday
in 1867, and **Sheffield United in 1889** — a county cricket club and both
halves of a football derby, out of one hotel on the corner of Arundel Gate.
It was demolished in 1969 to make room for the Crucible.

Two of the club's identifying details are misreadings, and both are worth
having straight.

The Owls are not named after owls. The ground stands in **Owlerton**, and
"owler" is Yorkshire dialect for the alder tree — Owlerton is an alder-farm,
a piece of botany rather than ornithology. The bird arrived because the
place-name looked like it should mean one, supporters ran with it, and the
club eventually put it on the badge. A folk etymology became an institution.

And **Wednesday were the Blades before Sheffield United were**. "Blade" meant
a Sheffielder rather than a footballer, in a city that made most of Britain's
steel, and it belonged to whichever club was in front. When Wednesday moved
north to Owlerton and became the Owls they left it lying about, and the club
founded by their former landlord picked it up.

## Trajectory

On 15 April 1989, ninety-seven people were unlawfully killed in a crush in
the central pens of the Leppings Lane terrace at Hillsborough, during an FA
Cup semi-final between Liverpool and Nottingham Forest. The 2016 inquests
found that **the behaviour of supporters did not cause or contribute to the
disaster**, and the account of it on this site is set out on
[Liverpool's page](/team/liverpool-fc/), because the consequences that
followed — the Taylor Report, all-seater grounds, and a thirty-year fight
over what had been said about the dead — belong there.

What belongs here is narrower and rarely discussed: what the same jury found
about the ground itself, and therefore about the club that owned and ran it.
Asked whether there were features of the design, construction and layout of
the stadium that they considered dangerous or defective and which caused or
contributed to the disaster, the jury answered **yes**. The findings were
specific. Crush barriers in Pens 3 and 4 did not comply with the Green Guide.
Barrier 144 had been removed. There were no dedicated turnstiles for
individual pens, so the number of people in each could not be monitored.
There were too few turnstiles at the Leppings Lane end, and the signage
directing supporters was inadequate. The jury further found that the club's
consulting engineers, **Eastwood & Partners, should have done more** to
detect and advise on those defects.

Behind all of it sat a document. The ground's **safety certificate had been
issued in 1979 and was never amended**, through a decade in which the
terracing behind the Leppings Lane goal was subdivided into pens and the
layout materially changed. Evidence to the inquests put the safe capacity of
that end at 5,425 against the 7,200 the certificate allowed. **Graham
Mackrell**, the club secretary and safety officer, was convicted in 2019 of a
health-and-safety offence over the turnstile arrangements and fined £6,500 —
the only conviction of any kind arising from the disaster.

The rest of the club's trajectory is a more ordinary story told at an
unusual scale. Wednesday were a First Division club when the Premier League
formed, finished seventh in its first season, and have not returned since
2000. The two relegations that matter here were both administered rather
than played. In **2020/21** the club went down from the Championship carrying
a six-point deduction for the accounting treatment of its own ground; the
margin at the bottom was such that the deduction was the difference. And in
**2025/26**, with eighteen points removed across an administration and a
separate finding on unpaid wages, the club finished the season **on zero
points** — two wins from forty-six games and a deduction larger than the
total it had earned. In the seasons this site records, from 1958/59 onwards,
no other club has finished a league season on nothing at all.

## Ownership & Finance

The single transaction that explains the last decade is the sale of the
ground by the club to its own owner.

Dejphon Chansiri bought Sheffield Wednesday in 2015 for a reported £37.5m.
In 2018, facing profitability and sustainability limits that the club's
losses had comfortably breached, **Hillsborough was sold for around £60m to
Sheffield 3 Ltd, a company Chansiri also owned**. The effect on the accounts
was decisive: a £35.4m loss for 2017/18 became a £2.5m profit. The EFL did
not object to a club selling its ground to a related party as such — the
practice was, at the time, unremarkable enough that several Championship
clubs had done it. What it objected to was the timing. An independent
commission found the profit had been booked in the wrong accounting period,
deducted twelve points, and an appeal board reduced that to six, to be
applied in 2020/21. On the more serious allegation — that the club had
concealed the true nature of the transaction — Wednesday were **found not
guilty**.

The consequence is structural and outlives the owner. The club plays in a
stadium it sold, and the sale that saved a set of accounts in 2018 removed
the only substantial asset it had.

The end came in **October 2025**, when unpaid wages, unpaid tax and a
transfer embargo converged and the club entered administration for the first
time in its history — twelve points, and six more in December for the
payment breaches themselves. Chansiri was banned from involvement in an EFL
member club for three years. A takeover by **Arise Capital** completed in May
2026, on terms under which the EFL waived the further fifteen-point deduction
that would normally follow an exit from administration without paying
creditors in full. Several other clubs objected publicly, which is the
predictable result of a rule that exists to deter and is then set aside.

One correction, because it is repeated confidently and often: **Wednesday did
not enter administration in 2010**. HMRC issued a winding-up petition, it was
settled, and Milan Mandarić's takeover pre-empted the hearing. The club came
close and did not go in. 2025 was the first time.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Hillsborough is not called Hillsborough because of a hill. The ground opened
in 1899 as the **Owlerton Stadium**, and was renamed in 1914 after the
parliamentary constituency the district had been absorbed into — so a
stadium now synonymous worldwide with a single afternoon took its name from
a boundary review.

It has been one of English football's principal neutral venues for most of
its existence, hosting twenty-seven FA Cup semi-finals and matches at the
1966 World Cup and Euro 96, and its capacity — 39,732 — remains among the
largest outside the Premier League. The Leppings Lane end has been rebuilt.

In July 2025 Sheffield City Council served a **prohibition notice closing the
North Stand**, after corrosion was found in the steelwork, removing several
thousand seats weeks before a season began. The remedial works were specified
and verified by **Eastwood and Partners**. That is set down here as a matter
of documented fact, and no inference is drawn from it.

The wider point about this ground is one the club did not choose and cannot
put down. Most stadiums accumulate meaning slowly, out of results. This one
has a meaning fixed by a single day, held by people who mostly support
another club four hours' drive away, and any question about the North Stand,
the Leppings Lane end or a safety certificate at Hillsborough is heard in
that context — permanently, and correctly.
