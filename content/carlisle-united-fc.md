## Origins

Carlisle United was not formed by a merger, and the merger story is the most
widely repeated thing about the club.

What happened on **17 May 1904** was a vote. **Shaddongate United** held its
AGM, and its members resolved to change the club's name to Carlisle United.
There had been a merger proposal — Shaddongate had approached the shareholders
of **Carlisle Red Rose** at the end of the previous season — and Red Rose had
taken it public, said Shaddongate were in serious financial trouble and about
to fold, and dismissed the offer. The renaming was what Shaddongate did
instead.

The documentary disproof of the merger story is a football match. **Carlisle
United beat Carlisle Red Rose 3–0 in the FA Cup in 1905/06**, eighteen months
after the two clubs are supposed to have become one. Red Rose folded in 1906,
independently. The merger that never happened is probably the reason the club
settled on 1904 as its founding date at all.

Election to the Football League came in **1928**, into the Third Division
North, at Durham City's expense, and lasted seventy-six unbroken years.

## Trajectory

**Carlisle United have played one season in the top flight, and for three days
of it they were top of the league.**

Promoted in third place in 1973/74, they won their first three matches of
1974/75 in eight days — at Chelsea, at Middlesbrough, then at home to
Tottenham — and stood first in the First Division on 24 August 1974. They lost
at home to Middlesbrough on the 27th and were never top again. They finished
**twenty-second of twenty-two on 29 points**, and the champions, Derby County,
took one point from four against them.

The other end of the club's range is where the real distinction is.
**Carlisle were the first club to have played in all five of the tiers this
site covers**, completing the set in the Conference in 2004/05; only six clubs
in the whole of the data have done it, and the other five all did it later.
The route down and back up is worth stating in order, because in both cases the
mechanism is not a football one.

**8 May 1999.** Needing to win to stay up, and having had no available
goalkeeper three games from the end — one injured, the other recalled by his
parent club — Carlisle had signed **Jimmy Glass on loan from Swindon, after the
transfer deadline, because the Football League permitted the deal**. In the
last minute of the last match he came up for a corner and scored. Carlisle
finished twenty-third on 49 points; Scarborough went down on 48. He played
three games for the club.

**2003/04.** Twenty-third of twenty-four on 45 points, relegated out of the
League alongside York City. **2004/05**: third in the Conference on 73 points,
and promoted through the play-off final against Stevenage; **2005/06**: League
Two champions on 86.

**2023/24 and 2024/25.** Relegated twice in succession — twenty-fourth in
League One on 30 points, then twenty-third in League Two on 42, out of the
Football League for the first time since 2004. Third in the National League in
2025/26 on 95 points was not enough.

Across all of it, in sixty-eight completed seasons, **Carlisle have never had a
point deducted**.

## Ownership & Finance

Michael Knighton bought Carlisle United in 1992, having left the Manchester
United board to do it, and said the club would be in the Premier League within
ten years. The first half of the decade did not obviously contradict him:
Third Division champions on **91 points in 1994/95**, promoted again in third
place in 1996/97. In 1997/98, after dismissing the manager, he took first-team
affairs himself as part of a three-man coaching group, and the club was
relegated.

Two disqualifications are routinely run together and should not be. In
**September 2000 Michael Knighton was disqualified as a company director for
five and a half years, and his wife for two — over a Yorkshire company that
owed £288,000 to the Inland Revenue when it was wound up in 1997**. That
proceeding had nothing to do with Carlisle United. The Carlisle disqualification
was a different person's: **his son Mark, appointed to the board in 2001, was
later disqualified for three and a half years for allowing the club to trade
while insolvent**.

The exit was forced from below. The club went into **administration in late May
2002** on a winding-up petition from HM Customs & Excise, after three failed
takeover attempts, and supporters boycotted the following season's tickets —
**seven season tickets sold by late July**. Knighton sold his 93 per cent that
month to a consortium led by the Irish businessman John Courtenay. A local
board — Andrew Jenkins as chairman, with two fellow owner-directors — ran the
club for the next two decades.

In **November 2023 Castle Sports Group completed a takeover**, taking 90 per
cent of the holding company. It is owned by the **Piatak family of
Jacksonville, Florida**, whose business is transport logistics. The accounts
since show what buying a lower-division English club costs: turnover of
£8.31m and a pre-tax profit of £2.8m for 2023/24, then **a £5.1m loss in
2024/25**, the first full year under the new owners, with football expenditure
rising from £3.8m to £5.4m and half a million in transfer fees on top. Turnover
and commercial income both rose. The team was relegated twice.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Brunton Park sits on Warwick Road between two rivers, the Petteril and the
Eden, and the club owns it. Everything else about the ground follows from where
it is.

The wooden grandstand burned down on the night of **10–11 March 1953**, and the
rebuild was paid for by **selling Geoff Twentyman to Liverpool for £12,500** —
a transfer fee converted directly into a stand, which is how ground
improvements were financed before grants existed.

Then the water, twice. In **January 2005** the Eden, Caldew and Petteril all
burst their banks; more than 1,800 properties flooded, three people died, and
Brunton Park went under. Carlisle, then in the Conference, played their home
matches at **Morecambe's Christie Park for about six weeks**, fifty-eight miles
away, and went up that May. In **December 2015 Storm Desmond** put water nearly
ten feet deep on the lower concourses — staff could not get in for about three
days — and the club played three "home" fixtures at **three different borrowed
grounds**: Deepdale, Ewood Park and Bloomfield Road. They were back at Brunton
Park on 23 January 2016, seven weeks after the flood. The exile was short; the
restoration ran on for most of a year.

The lasting cost was not the repairs. The insurers paid, the club reported a
record profit for 2015/16, and the value of its property rose by £1.62m. What
changed was **insurability**: the chief executive said flood cover was costing
£50,000 a year more than before, with a large excess and a reduced cap, and
described the club at renewal as almost uninsurable. A ground can be repaired
in a year. A flood plain cannot.

A relocation to a new 12,000-seat stadium north of the city was announced in
2011 and **shelved in 2016**; since 2024 the plans have been for redeveloping
Brunton Park where it stands.

And then the distance, which is the club's permanent condition. **Carlisle's
nearest Football League neighbour is Newcastle United, 51.7 miles away** as the
crow flies — measured from this site's own coordinates, half as far again as
the next most isolated club in the 92 — and the journey to Plymouth is 389
miles by road, 316 in a straight line. But the isolation has a date and an
off-pitch cause. **Until 1977 Workington were thirty miles away and in the same
division**; they finished bottom in 1976 and 1977 and lost their League place
at the re-election vote. Carlisle did not become the most remote club in
English league football by moving. Their neighbour was voted out.
