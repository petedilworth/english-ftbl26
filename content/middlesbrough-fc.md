## Origins

Middlesbrough Football Club was formed in 1876 by members of Middlesbrough
Cricket Club, who wanted something to do in the winter. The meeting was held
in a gymnasium behind the Albert Park Hotel on Linthorpe Road, and the first
recorded match was a 1-1 draw with Tees Wanderers at the old Archery Ground
in Albert Park the following February.

That is an ordinary enough beginning — cricketers needing exercise — but it
determined the club's politics for the next fifteen years, because a club
founded by gentlemen cricketers was a club with opinions about being paid to
play. Middlesbrough's committee was firmly against professionalism. In
October 1889 the members who disagreed walked out, met at the Temperance
Hall, and founded a rival professional club in the same town.

They called it **Middlesbrough Ironopolis**, after the town's own Victorian
boast about its iron and steel trade, and the choice was pointed: one club
named after the place, the other after what the place did for a living.
Ironopolis were the working man's team and drew from the works; Middlesbrough
drew from the middle and upper classes. Middlesbrough turned professional
within a week of their rivals doing so, which tells you how much the
principle was worth under competition. Ironopolis reached the Football
League's Second Division in 1893, resigned after a single season, and folded
in 1894 when the receipts would not cover wages and travel. Middlesbrough
went back to being amateurs in 1893 and won the FA Amateur Cup twice before
turning professional for good in 1899.

The nickname is the plainest part of the club and the town's second one is
the interesting part. "Boro" is simply the end of Middlesbrough, and no
competing story stands up — this is one of the rare cases where a football
nickname means exactly what it looks like. **"Smoggies"** is the one with a
history. It began in the 1990s as an insult from visiting supporters, who
called Teessiders "smog monsters" for the air coming off the ICI works and
the steel plants, and it has been taken up and worn as identity: on shirts,
in fanzines, by people describing themselves. The coinage is generally
credited to Sunderland's support, which puts a rivalry inside the word.

## Trajectory

Twice the club has come close to ending, and each time the mechanism was
institutional rather than footballing.

The first was in 1910, and it is the strangest thing in the club's records.
Chairman Thomas Gibson Poole was the Conservative candidate for Middlesbrough
in that December's general election and believed that beating Sunderland days
before the poll would help him. Manager Andy Walker approached Sunderland's
captain, Charlie Thomson, and offered a bribe to throw the match. Thomson
refused and reported it. Middlesbrough won anyway, 1-0; Poole lost the
election by around three thousand votes; and on 16 January 1911 the FA
suspended both chairman and manager from football for life. Some twelve and
a half thousand Middlesbrough supporters signed a petition asking the FA to
reconsider Walker's ban on the grounds that he had been following his
chairman's instructions. It was refused. A football club had been used as an
instrument of a parliamentary campaign, and the town's response was to argue
that the employee should not carry the employer's sentence.

The second was 1986, and it is the reason a set of gates now stands outside
the Riverside. Relegated to the Third Division and roughly £2m in debt, the
club called in a provisional liquidator that May. The Inland Revenue
petitioned over £115,156 of unpaid tax and a winding-up order followed. On 2
August the manager and twenty-nine other non-playing staff were sacked and
**the gates of Ayresome Park were padlocked** — players trained in local
parks and on the beach, and the first home fixture of the season was played
at Hartlepool's ground because their own was still locked. A consortium
including a twenty-eight-year-old local businessman named Steve Gibson,
backed by ICI and Scottish & Newcastle, put together the bond the Football
League required and registered the club in time to play. The club has since
made the padlock its own iconography: the original Ayresome Park gates were
re-erected outside the Riverside's main entrance in 2005.

The third near-miss was arithmetic. On 21 December 1996 Middlesbrough did not
travel to Blackburn, saying a virus had left them unable to field a safe side.
The commission fined them £50,000 and deducted three points — the first
points deduction in the Premier League's history, and nothing in this
database precedes it. They finished the season nineteenth on 39 points, and
lost both cup finals for good measure. The counterfactual is unusually clean:

| | | on the pitch |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | Southampton | 41 |
| 17 | Coventry City | 41 |
| 18 | Sunderland | 40 |
| **19** | **Middlesbrough** | **42** |

Forty-two would have been sixteenth. The deduction did not contribute to the
relegation; it caused it.

## Ownership & Finance

Steve Gibson came in as part of the 1986 rescue at twenty-eight, bought out
Scottish & Newcastle's holding in 1993, and succeeded ICI's Colin Henderson
as chairman in 1994 with around ninety per cent of the club. He has held it
since, which makes him one of the longest-serving owners in English football
and the club one of the few where ownership has been a settled question for
three decades.

What that has meant in practice is subsidy. The holding company, Gibson
O'Neill, has put a reported £200m and more into the club over its ownership,
overwhelmingly to cover operating losses rather than to buy players, and at
one point converted £149m of debt into equity — cutting gross debt from
around £154m to around £24m at a stroke. The 2024/25 accounts show a loss of
£11.4m even after more than £20m of profit on player sales, with net assets
down to £2m. This is a club that has been kept in the second tier, at a
standard the second tier does not fund, by one man's balance sheet.

The club has also been an unusually persistent litigant. Middlesbrough
claimed against Derby County from 2019 over Derby's financial fair play
breaches, arguing they had cost Boro a play-off place in 2018/19. An
arbitration panel ruled Middlesbrough had no standing to appeal the dismissal
of one EFL charge and could not intervene in the EFL's own appeal; when Derby
entered administration the EFL classed the claim as a football debt; and in
February 2022 the two clubs announced a resolution on private terms.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Ayresome Park was built for 1903/04 to designs by Archibald Leitch — an early
commission, and the plans survive in Teesside Archives — for a reported
£11,957, with the South Stand dismantled and carried over from the club's
previous ground at the Linthorpe Road cricket field. In July 1966 it staged
North Korea's entire World Cup group, including the 1-0 win over Italy, and
the town adopted a team that played in red and had come further than anyone;
surviving players were given a civic reception when they returned in 2002.

The move to the Riverside was driven by the Taylor Report's all-seater
requirement. Teesside Development Corporation offered a derelict dockside
site at Middlehaven on the Tees, and the stadium was built by Taylor Woodrow
in around nine months, opening in 1995 at 30,000 and expanded to 35,100 in
1998 by filling in the corners. The club owns it. What is not in the public
record, and this page will not guess at, is the split of who paid for what
between the club, the development corporation and any grant funding.

The best thing about the move is what was done with what was left. Ayresome
Park was demolished in 1997 and built over with housing, its streets named
Midfield Drive and Promotion Close. The artist Neville Gabie overlaid the old
ground plan on the new estate and marked what had been where: bronze casts
and texts at the centre spot, the penalty spots, the touchlines, the gates. A
pair of bronze boots sits outside somebody's front door on the old centre
circle. A bronze puddle with stud marks in it marks the place Pak Doo-ik shot
from in 1966. It is a football ground remembered as an archaeology rather than
a monument, in a housing estate people actually live in.
