## Origins

Ipswich Town was founded in 1878 and did not turn professional until 1936 —
fifty-eight years of amateur football, ending with election to the Southern
League and then, on 30 May 1938, to the Football League Third Division South
in place of Gillingham. The first League match at Portman Road was played
that August. The club had already been there for fifty-four years by then,
having moved to the ground in 1884, which gives it one of the longest
continuous tenancies in English football.

The reason a club could take that long to turn professional, and then run for
another half-century without much upheaval, is the family that chaired it.
The Cobbolds were Suffolk brewers: Thomas Cobbold established a brewery at
Harwich in 1723, the family moved brewing to Ipswich in 1746 and built the
Cliff Brewery beside the Orwell, and five members of the family chaired the
football club, the last two consecutively from 1957 to 1991. Captain Ivan
Cobbold was in the chair when Ipswich turned professional and was killed in
1944 when a bomb hit the Guards Chapel in London. John Cobbold joined the
board in 1948 at twenty-one, reportedly the youngest director in the League,
chaired the club from 1957 to 1976 and stayed a director until his death;
Patrick Cobbold followed him from 1976 to 1991. Thirty-four unbroken years of
two brothers, inside a family association stretching from 1936.

What that chairmanship is remembered for is a line about wine. Some version
of "there is no crisis at Ipswich until the white wine runs out in the
boardroom" is attributed to a Cobbold chairman, most often John — but the
wording is unstable across tellings, the attribution varies between the
brothers, and no contemporaneous source for it has surfaced. It is a good
anecdote and should be labelled as one. The nearest documented fact beside it
is the recollection of David Rose, club secretary for forty-five years, that
keeping the boardroom stocked with drink was among his duties.

The nickname is more recent than the club's manner suggests, and its origin
is genuinely disputed. "The Tractor Boys" began as an opposition supporters'
taunt about agricultural Suffolk and was then reclaimed, in the standard
English pattern, as self-deprecating humour. Where accounts diverge is the
occasion: one has it coined at Birmingham away in 1998, an Ipswich fanzine
editor recalling that "we were being taunted, so we responded with 'one-nil
to the Tractor Boys' — we may be rural country cousins but we were actually
1-0 up"; the other has it originating at Leeds in 2000-01, from a chant that
Leeds supporters were being beaten by a bunch of tractor drivers. What is not
in dispute is that it entered national use during the Premiership seasons of
2000 to 2002, that it started as an insult, and that it is a twenty-five-year-
old name for a club that is 148 years old. Inside Suffolk the club is Town, or
the Blues.

## Trajectory

Ipswich have the longest unbroken run any club has had in the second tier in
this database's thirty-three seasons: seventeen consecutive Championship
campaigns from 2002/03 to 2018/19. The run begins with a relegation and an
insolvency four months apart, and understanding it means starting with a
television company.

ITV Digital had contracted to pay the Football League £315m over three years
for its broadcast rights. It entered administration on 27 March 2002 with
roughly £178.5m of that unpaid. The League sued Carlton Communications and
Granada, arguing that they had guaranteed their subsidiary's bid; the High
Court ruled that neither had any liability, the League's commercial director
conceding in court that there were no written guarantees and that he had
assumed they existed, and the judge describing the League as naive for
entering the agreement without securing them. Sky picked up replacement
rights for around £95m — a fall of more than £75m a season across the
Football League. Somewhere around sixteen clubs entered administration in
2002-03.

Ipswich was one of them, and its position was worse than most because it had
been relegated from the Premier League the season before and had built for
staying up. An administration order was made over the club company on 10
February 2003. The contingency plan had been to sell players: three departures
raised roughly £7m, further sales fell through, and players and staff took
wage deferrals. The administrators, from Deloitte & Touche, exited through a
company voluntary arrangement approved on 2 May, running four years, under
which some creditors received as little as five pence in the pound. The order
was discharged on 30 May.

No points were deducted, and this is the part that makes Ipswich's case
instructive rather than merely unfortunate. The Football League's automatic
ten-point penalty for insolvency did not exist in February 2003. It was
introduced for 2004/05, in direct response to the wave of administrations
that followed the ITV Digital collapse — the wave Ipswich belonged to —
and Wrexham became the first club docked under it in December 2004. The
penalty was raised to twelve points from 2015/16. A club that had done
nothing structurally unusual by the standards of its peers was nearly
destroyed by a broadcast contract failing in London, and escaped a sanction
only because the rule written in response to that failure came eighteen
months too late to catch it. Its absence from the deductions in this database
is a true record, not a missing row.

Since the run ended the club has moved faster than at any point in its
history: relegated to the third tier in 2018/19, four seasons there, then
promoted as runners-up in 2022/23 with 98 points and again in 2023/24 with
96, a single Premier League season in 2024/25 that ended nineteenth with 22
points, and immediate promotion back as runners-up in 2025/26. Four
divisional changes in four seasons, at a club that had managed one in the
previous seventeen.

## Ownership & Finance

Marcus Evans completed his purchase on 18 December 2007 in two parts that are
usually reported as one. He injected £12m for new shares, taking 87.5% of the
club, and separately bought the club's £32m of bank debt for a reported
twenty pence in the pound. That debt had a specific origin: it financed the
rebuilding of the North and South stands at Portman Road and a new training
centre in the early 2000s. Buying it at a discount converted a structural
liability into a shareholder's claim, which is why the club could operate at
all through the following decade.

The sale to American investors completed on 7 April 2021, at a reported £40m,
through a vehicle named Gamechanger 20 Ltd. The ownership behind it is
routinely garbled, and it is worth setting out precisely, because three
different entities get merged into one. ORG Portfolio Management, which held
around 90% of Gamechanger, is an investment manager based in Cleveland, Ohio.
The pension fund whose money it manages is the Arizona Public Safety
Personnel Retirement System, the fund for that state's police officers and
firefighters. And the Phoenix connection that gets attached to the pension
fund actually belongs to three consortium members who sit on the board of
Phoenix Rising FC and invest through a fund called Three Lions. An Ohio
manager, an Arizona pension fund and an Arizona football club are not the
same investor. Mark Ashton arrived as chief executive on 1 June 2021 and
later became chair as well.

In December 2025 the structure changed again: Portman Holdings LLC became
majority shareholder following a transfer of shares from ORG, which remains a
stakeholder, with further investment from Three Lions and Clara Vista
Partners. The restructure was reported at a valuation of around £350m —
roughly ten times the 2021 price, at a club that had been promoted twice and
relegated once in between. Precise post-restructure percentages have been
reported inconsistently and are not stated here.

The accounts of the promotion and the Premier League season are the clearest
illustration in this database of what going up actually costs and pays. The
year to June 2024, the Championship promotion season, shows turnover up 71%
to £37.3m and a pre-tax loss of £39.3m — a loss larger than the entire
turnover. The single biggest driver was a £16.3m exceptional charge for
promotion bonuses, booked in the year the promotion was won rather than the
year the money arrived, with the wage bill more than doubling from £19.8m to
£44.5m. The following year, in the Premier League, turnover reached a club
record £155.4m and the club posted a pre-tax profit of £4.0m. League
distributions and televised games went from £10.3m to £107.6m; commercial
income from £11m to £29m; wages from £44.5m to £77.1m; player acquisitions
cost £112.8m. Profit on player sales was £32.2m, around £15.4m of it from a
single transfer completed just before the year end — without player trading
the club would have posted a small loss rather than a profit.

The regulatory arithmetic underneath is worth separating from the headline
figures, because the £39.3m accounting loss is not the loss the rules
measure. Profitability and Sustainability thresholds are weighted by the
division a club was in each season, so promotion raises the allowance
sharply; the great majority of that £112.8m of transfer spending is amortised
across the length of the contracts rather than charged in one year; and owner
money arriving as equity to be converted into shares is not an expense at
all. The club published its own position for the cycle ending 2023/24 —
eligible losses of £34.9m against a £39m threshold, more than 10% of
headroom. Auditors on the 2024/25 accounts noted net current liabilities of
£29.3m and a letter of support from the parent company. From 2026/27 the
Premier League replaces the regime entirely with a squad cost ratio, so the
mechanics Ipswich navigated will not be the ones the next promoted club
faces.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Portman Road has been Ipswich's ground since 1884 and was among the first in
the country to install goal nets, in 1890. The first stand went up in 1906
and a second in 1928. In 1914 the British Army commandeered the ground as a
training camp and did not return it until two years after the war ended.

The ownership is split in an unusual way, and the split is stable rather than
contested. Ipswich Borough Council owns the land; the football club owns the
stands and every building on it. The club signed a new ninety-nine-year lease
in 1968, and the council has declined the club's attempts to buy the
freehold, its stated reason being that its interest keeps the club in the
town centre near the railway station. There is a widely repeated story that
the club sold the ground to the council and leased it back; no evidence for
any such transaction exists, and it appears to be a garbled account of the
lease.

The Taylor Report converted all four stands to all-seater in the early 1990s.
The North and South stands were then rebuilt in the early 2000s, taking
capacity from about 22,600 to roughly 30,000 — the work that produced the
£32m of bank debt Marcus Evans later bought. In September 2013 the council
listed Portman Road as an Asset of Community Value following a nomination by
the Ipswich Town Independent Supporters Trust, which means the council must
be notified of any disposal and a community group gets a window to prepare a
bid.

The recent work has followed promotion rather than preceded it. Safe standing
was installed in the Cobbold Stand in summer 2024, and the away allocation was
raised from 2,000 to 3,000 to meet Premier League away-percentage
requirements. In October 2024 the chief executive announced an agreement in
principle to buy a large plot of land behind the Cobbold Stand, confirmed by
the borough council, with a long-term ambition of a two-tier stand and a
ground of around 40,000; separate plans for the West Stand concourses have
been submitted. As of late 2025 the surface car parks were reported as
needing replacement before the Cobbold Stand work could proceed.

It is a very Ipswich sequence: one ground for a hundred and forty-two years,
a landlord that will not sell and will not evict, and an expansion that waits
on somewhere to put the cars.
