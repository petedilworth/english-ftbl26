## Origins

Cheltenham Town was founded in **1887**, and it knows that because it went
looking. The club's own research, published in 2010, moved the founding date
back five years from the 1892 that every reference work had carried — so any
source older than 2010 will disagree with the club about when it began.

It was formed after trials at the East Gloucestershire cricket ground by
**Albert Close White**, a Cheltenham man who had taken up association football
at college in London and introduced it on returning to a teaching post at home.
A spa town with a racecourse, and a schoolmaster bringing a game back from the
capital.

**The Robins came from a shirt, and later than you would think.** Ruby shirts
in 1896 made them the Rubies. Turning professional in 1932 brought red and
white hoops and no new nickname. It was the **July 1938 kit** — white shirts, red
collar and sleeves, and a red robin embroidered on the pocket — that produced
the name they have now.

They have been at Whaddon Road since 1932, on a ground built in 1927 by the
Cheltenham Original Brewery on the site of the Berkeley Hunt kennels.

## Trajectory

This site has spent a good deal of these pages on clubs refused promotion
because their ground was not good enough — Kidderminster in 1994, Macclesfield
in 1995, Stevenage in 1996. **Cheltenham Town are the other side of that
ledger.**

In **1996/97 Gresley Rovers won the Southern League Premier Division and their
ground failed the required standard, so the runners-up went up in their place.
The runners-up were Cheltenham Town.** Everything that follows — the Conference
title, the Football League, twenty-seven years of it — begins with a promotion
the club did not win.

What follows is compressed. **Steve Cotterill** was appointed player-manager in
February 1997, aged 32, a Cheltenham local, with the club still in the Southern
League. Inside five years he took them up three times: into the Conference in
1997, into the **Football League as Conference champions in 1998/99** — 80 points
to Kettering's 76 — and into the third tier through the play-offs in 2002. He
won the FA Trophy at Wembley in 1998 at 33, and came back as manager in
September 2025, twenty-three years after leaving.

**1999/2000 was the club's first season in the Football League in its history**,
and it was in the fourth tier — a correction worth making, because the claim
that Cheltenham went up to the third tier in 1999 circulates and is wrong by two
divisions and three years.

On the grading question there is a negative finding worth stating. **No record
exists of Whaddon Road ever failing a grade or of Cheltenham being refused a
promotion.** The timeline argues against it: the ground's three modern stands
were built in 2001, 2001 and 2005 — *after* both the 1997 and 1999 promotions —
so whatever Whaddon Road was in 1999, it passed as it stood.

Relegation out of the League came in 2015 and lasted one season: **National
League champions in 2015/16 on 101 points**, twelve clear of Forest Green. The
club won its **first Football League title in 2020/21** and had its highest
finish, fifteenth in the third tier, the year after. Relegation from League One
followed in 2023/24, twenty-first on 44 points.

**Cheltenham have never had a point deducted.**

## Ownership & Finance

**For most of its time in the Football League, Cheltenham Town had no owner.**

Not a benefactor, not a majority shareholder — an array of small shareholders
and a board appointed by them, running the club on what it earned. Four larger
holdings and a long tail, and no one in control. That is a rare arrangement at
this level and it produced the number that makes the point: **Cheltenham broke
even in 2023/24, in a division where the average club lost about £5.2m and the
losses across League One were covered by roughly £120m of owner money.** Their
total wage bill was around £1.49m.

It has to be said with one qualification. **Simon Keswick**, a Cheltenham
supporter and a director of Jardine Matheson, was the largest shareholder,
gave significant input to building a stand in 2006, and **funded the purchase of
the training ground in 2022** — a twenty-two-acre site the club had used since
2002 but only leased, and which, as the chairman put it, they could have been
asked to vacate at any time. So the defensible claim is not "no wealthy
backer". It is that no owner was covering the operating losses, because there
were barely any.

The model ended because it ran out of road. **In October 2024 three of the four
biggest shareholders wanted out**, the chairman saying the club needed
substantial new investment, particularly for the stadium and training ground.
**On 23 August 2025 Mike Garlick, the former Burnley chairman, acquired a
controlling interest.**

**The Robins Trust holds a stake and did not sell.** Formed in 2005, it has put
over £300,000 into the club, has had a director on the board since 2018, held
around 16% as the third-largest shareholder, and after the takeover is the
second-largest. **Cheltenham is not a fan-owned club** and never was — but the
supporters own more of it now, relatively, than they did before somebody bought
it.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Cheltenham Borough Council owns the land Whaddon Road stands on, and a
land swap the club proposed in 2012 has still not happened.**

The ground holds 7,066 with a little over half of that seated, and its record
crowd — 8,326, against Reading in the FA Cup — dates from 1956. The oldest
structure is a main stand from 1963, and the reason it is still there is the
land. The club proposed swapping part of its car park for a council plot next
door, to improve access and free up the main stand for redevelopment. It has
been unresolved since 2012, complicated by a community association that holds
leases on surrounding land and once sought nearly £200,000 from the club over
an access agreement struck when the 2001 stand was built.

There was an alternative and it was costed and abandoned. **From 2008 the club
pursued a joint venture with Cheltenham Racecourse**, at one point discussing a
double-sided grandstand shared between football and National Hunt racing.
**It was shelved in January 2012**, the chairman saying plainly that the costs of
the move were prohibitive.

The name on the ground changes about every three years — Abbey Business, then
World of Smile for a single season of a three-year deal, then LCI Rail, then
Jonny-Rocks, then Completely-Suzuki, and since 2024 a Gloucestershire electric-
vehicle charger installer. Whaddon Road is what it is called.

One tenancy is worth recording, because it closes a loop. **Gloucester City,
Cheltenham's rival of more than a century, played at Whaddon Road from 2010
after their own ground flooded under eight feet of water**, paying around
£40,000 a year, and stayed seven years. The club Cheltenham's supporters had
spent a hundred years hating became the club they charged rent — and the
rivalry itself had already been killed off in 1999, not by either club, but by
Cheltenham being promoted out of reach of it.
