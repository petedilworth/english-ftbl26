## Origins

There have been two York City football clubs and they do not overlap. The
first was founded in 1908, played in the Northern League and then the Midland
League, turned professional in 1912 and **folded in 1917**. The present club is
a **new limited company formed in 1922** by members of the old one, to fill the
gap the war had left; the record-keepers file them separately, and the League
place came in 1929.

Two things about the city are usually got wrong. The first is the
confectionery. York's great employers were Rowntree's and Terry's, and neither
founded, funded or ran the football club: both were Quaker firms with, as
*When Saturday Comes* put it, little time for sport. Rowntree's had its own
works team. The only documented connection is a venue — Fulfordgate was not
ready for the start of 1922/23, so **the club's first two home league matches
were played at Mille Crux, Rowntree's ground**. The second is the railway.
York's other industry was the railway works, later nationalised, and it plays
no founding role either. This is a rare English club that owes nothing to a
factory, a church or a pub.

The move that mattered was demographic. Fulfordgate was on the wrong edge of
the city, and in **1932 the club moved to Bootham Crescent** because the
population within a mile of it was thirty thousand rather than three.

## Trajectory

**In 1983/84 York City became the first club in Football League history to
reach a hundred points**, winning the Fourth Division with 101 from 31 wins,
8 draws and 7 defeats. In the whole of this site's data only two fourth-tier
totals have ever beaten it. It remains the club's only league title.

It is routinely merged with the other thing everyone remembers. The
**1–0 win over Arsenal in the FA Cup fourth round was 26 January 1985**, a
different season, with York eighth in the Third Division and about a hundred
and fifty supporters clearing three inches of snow off the pitch with spades
so the tie could be played at all.

The fall took two forms, and this site can only show one of them. York
finished bottom of the Third Division in **2003/04**, ending seventy-five
years of Football League membership, and spent **eight seasons in the
Conference** — not four, as is often written — before going up through the
2012 play-off final, having won the FA Trophy at Wembley three weeks earlier.
Then bottom of League Two in 2015/16, and relegated again from the National
League in 2016/17, **into regional football for the first time since 1929**.
That second drop is invisible here: this site's data stops at the fifth tier,
so **York simply has no rows between 2018 and 2022**, and any derived figure
for the lowest level they ever reached will read tier five when the answer is
tier six. Five seasons in National League North ended with a play-off final
win over Boston United in May 2022.

They came back at the top of the table. **The National League title in 2025/26
was won on 108 points**, a total beaten only once in this data, by Wrexham's
111, and settled on the final day by a 103rd-minute equaliser at Rochdale, who
were chasing the same title.

**York have never had a point deducted, in any season, at any level** — which
in a club that went into administration is a matter of timing rather than
virtue, and is dealt with below.

## Ownership & Finance

York City bought the Bootham Crescent freehold in **1948 for £4,075**, having
leased it from the cricket club since 1932. What happened to that asset is one
of the most completely documented cases in English football of a ground being
separated from the club that played on it, and then recovered.

**July 1999.** The chairman wrote to shareholders proposing that the club and
the ground be transferred into a new company, **Bootham Crescent Holdings**,
noting that he and three fellow directors held 94 per cent of the shares and
had already approved it. Land Registry records show **BCH paid York City
£165,000 for the ground**.

**December 2001 to March 2002.** The club was put up for sale, BCH was priced
at £4.5m, and the club — now BCH's tenant — was given notice to quit by June.
The supporters' trust was launched at the York Barbican on 1 February 2002. On
30 January the city's MP had already raised the case in the Commons, arguing
that **FA Rule 34**, written to stop clubs selling their grounds and leaving
their communities, was not doing it. In March the football club was sold to a
new owner **for £1**, with a £400,000 payment attached and a twenty-five-year
lease torn up. BCH kept the ground and contracted to sell it to **Persimmon for
£3.5m on vacant possession**; in July the developer applied to demolish it and
build ninety-three houses.

**December 2002.** The club went into administration within a year of the sale.
Because the Football League's automatic points penalty for insolvency did not
begin until 2004/05, no deduction followed.

**March 2003.** The trust **raised £600,000, formed a new company and bought
the club's assets from the administrator**. That company, incorporated on
6 March 2003, is still the one that operates York City today.

**February 2004.** A **£2m low-interest loan from the Football Stadia
Improvement Fund** let the club acquire a majority shareholding in Bootham
Crescent Holdings — recovering the ground by buying the company that owned it,
rather than the land. The loan came with relocation conditions attached and was
later converted into a grant once the club moved. The site did become housing
in the end, but on the club's terms: **sold to Persimmon for £7m** and handed
over in April 2022, with the proceeds repaying a £2m council loan and the
FSIF's accrued interest.

The ownership since has moved through every model this site records.
**Fan-owned outright from 2003 to February 2007**, when the trust's members
approved selling 75 per cent to Jason McGill's JM Packaging in return for
further funding, keeping 25 per cent and a right of first refusal. That right
was exercised in **July 2022**, making the club **fully fan-owned again for
about four months** before 51 per cent was sold on; that owner's year ended in
open conflict with the trust. Since **June 2023** the majority holder has been
**394 Sports Ltd**, owned by Julie-Anne and Matthew Uggla, mother and son. The
trust sold a further 24 per cent in November 2023 and completed a **golden
share** process at the same time; a 24 per cent minority now sits with a silent
American partner. The structure is 51 / 25 / 24.

So: York City is **not** a fan-owned club, whatever the 2003 story suggests.
The trust holds a quarter of it, a seat at the table and a veto over the things
a golden share protects — which, after 1999, is the part that matters.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Bootham Crescent was built in four months and opened on 31 August 1932. It
ended at a capacity of 8,256, and one of its stands carried the name of a York
player who died during a match there in 1990. Demolition was completed in early
2023. **Part of the pitch had to be dug up so that supporters' ashes scattered
on it could be recovered** — a detail that says more about what a ground is
than any valuation of the site.

The replacement inverts the club's whole history. The **LNER Community
Stadium** at Monks Cross is **owned by the City of York Council**, holds 8,500,
and is shared with York City Knights rugby league club; the football club is a
**tenant**, and the trust had to give its consent before the club could enter
the lease. Having spent twenty years recovering a ground it owned, York now
plays in one it does not, inside a development that also contains a leisure
centre, a library, an NHS drop-in and a cinema.

Getting there took fifteen years. Planning began in 2006; there was a Sport
England holding objection, a Highways Agency delay, great crested newts, a
procurement re-run, the main contractor withdrawing in December 2016 as costs
rose, a judicial review brought by a cinema operator, drainage problems in
autumn 2020 — and finally a **lease dispute with the council landlord that
pushed the first match from October 2020 to January 2021**, settled over a
weekend with both sides saying their public statements had been misrepresented.
The ground opened in February 2021. The club's own contribution was a £2m loan
from the council, repaid out of the sale of the ground it had fought to keep.
