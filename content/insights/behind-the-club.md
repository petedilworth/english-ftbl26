Above the fifth tier, English football is owned from abroad. Of the
clubs the register can be matched to, twelve of nineteen in the Premier
League answer to someone outside the United Kingdom, and fourteen of
nineteen in League One – the division with the highest share of all.
In the seventh tier it is two of sixty-two. Twenty-six of the fifty-six
foreign controllers are American: more than every other country put
together.

The register shows how long the line can be. West Ham United's company
is owned by a company, owned by a company, owned by a company, and
three steps up stands Daniel Kretinsky, of the Czech Republic, with
between a quarter and a half. Manchester City's line runs through three companies to
Sheikh Mansour. Liverpool's runs through one to John Henry.
Where a chain reaches a company registered abroad – a Delaware
corporation, a Cayman fund – the register stops, and so does this page.
Manchester United's stops sooner: five holders sit at the top, none
with enough to be the controller.

A hundred and four clubs have borrowed against themselves. Not all of
it is debt: the most common names on the charges register are the
Football Stadia Improvement Fund, Sport England and the Football
Foundation, which take a charge over a ground when they pay for a stand
or a pitch, so the money comes back if the ground stops being a ground.
The borrowing proper is the banks and specialist lenders – and one name
recurs at the top: Macquarie holds charges at eight clubs in the top two
divisions, Brentford, Hull, Forest, Sunderland, Middlesbrough,
Sheffield United, Watford and Leicester, lending against the television
money still to come. Manchester United's company carries seventeen
charges, more than any other.

Boards churn fastest where owners change most. Nottingham Forest have
appointed sixteen directors and lost eighteen in five years, and have
two today. And the register keeps the scars: twenty-two clubs' companies
have an insolvency on record, most of them administrations or rescue
deals from the 1990s and 2000s that the clubs survived. One is live. Sheffield Wednesday went into
administration in October 2025 and their accounts are overdue.

Below the National League, the picture turns over. Two in three clubs in
the seventh tier are controlled by one person living in the UK, and eighteen of
sixty-two by nobody at all: members' clubs, where no one holds a
quarter.
