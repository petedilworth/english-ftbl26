The richest neighbourhoods in England have no Football League club.
Kensington, Richmond, St Albans and the Surrey commuter belt – the
dark blue on the map below – are served by Hampton & Richmond Borough,
St Albans City and Corinthian-Casuals, and the richest catchment of any
club in the country belongs to a side in the sixth tier. At the other
end, the poorest catchments with a club in the top two divisions belong
to Blackburn, Liverpool, Burnley, Sunderland and Everton: five clubs,
every one of them a former champion of England.

That is the finding, and the numbers do not soften it. Across this
season's clubs the correlation between the income of the people a club
draws and the tier it plays in is almost exactly zero. The number of
people it draws explains nine-tenths of the difference in tier on its
own, and adding income to the sum changes almost nothing. A club is as
big as its town, not as rich as it.

Which is why the money map is worth looking at twice. Once as a map of
England – the south-east dark, the Pennine towns and the coast pale –
and once as a map of football, where the pattern is close to the
reverse: the big clubs sit in the pale, because the pale is where the
big towns are, and the big towns are big because of the industries that
made them poor.

The spread inside a catchment is a different story. Chelsea and Fulham
draw from Kensington and from the estates beside it; the gap between
the richest tenth and poorest tenth of the people they reach is over
£22,000 a year, among the widest in the country. Burnley's is about
half that; Workington's a third. There is no measure of income inside a neighbourhood
in the data, so this is the spread between neighbourhoods, weighted by
how many of each a club draws – and it says which clubs sit on a fault
line and which sit on a plain.
