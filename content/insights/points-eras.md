Several tables on this site stop at 1981/82 and say so in a line of small
print: *from 1981/82 only, when three points for a win came in — earlier
totals are not comparable.* This page is why.

The Football League gave two points for a win until 1980/81 and three from
1981/82, which is well enough known. The consequence is less often thought
through: a points total is not a measurement of anything on its own. It is
a number produced by a rule, and ranking totals from either side of that
change is not a like-for-like comparison. Queens Park Rangers took 18
points from 42 games in 1968/69. The same record under three points for a
win would have been 22, which is why they do not belong in a table of the
lowest totals ever recorded — they would sit above clubs whose seasons were
genuinely worse.

The sharper case is a division that did something no other English national
league has done.

For three seasons from 1983/84 the Alliance Premier League — the first
national fifth tier, and the competition that became the National League —
paid **two points for a home win and three for an away one**. The reasoning
was straightforward enough: winning away is harder, so it should be worth
more, and rewarding it might discourage the cautious away performance that
a flat rule makes rational. No other national division in England has ever
valued the two differently.

It was not a technicality. In 1984/85 it decided the title.

Wealdstone won the championship with the best away record in the division —
twelve away wins, more than anyone — and eight at home. Bath City won
fifteen at home, more than anyone, and six away. Under the rule as it stood,
Wealdstone finished first and Bath City fourth. Count the identical results
three points for a win, as every season since has been counted, and Bath
City are champions and Wealdstone third. Nothing about what happened on the
pitch changes. Only the arithmetic does.

That is the argument for the small print, and the reason this site computes
points from the rule in force for the season rather than applying one
number to seventy years of football. The table below reads its figures from
the same function the pipeline uses, so what is described here and what was
actually applied cannot drift apart.

It is also a reminder about league tables in general. A table looks like a
record of what happened, and it is really a record of what happened passed
through a scoring rule that somebody chose and could have chosen
differently.
