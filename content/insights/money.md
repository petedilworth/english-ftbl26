The median Premier League club turned over £222m in its latest accounts.
The median Championship club turned over £30.4m, about a seventh of that,
and paid out £1.09 in wages for every pound of it. In League One the
figure is £1.18. Below the third tier most clubs stop filing a profit and
loss account at all, and the comparison carries on with the balance sheet
alone.

The balance sheet has a cliff of its own. The median Premier League club
with one on file is worth £92.1m. The median Championship club owes £18.2m
more than it owns, and the median League One club £4.8m. Eighty-one of the
142 clubs whose balance sheet could be read are worth less than nothing.
In football this is a common condition rather than a final one, and the
next page is about the clubs for whom it was final.

The parachute shows plainly. The six Championship clubs relegated from the
Premier League in the three seasons before had a median turnover of
£69.3m. The other seventeen had £28.0m.

A Premier League point cost a median £4.1m in wages. Brentford's cost
£2.3m. Southampton paid £116m in wages and won twelve points, which comes
to £9.7m each. In the Championship, Burnley and Leeds United both reached
a hundred points, at £820k and £1.0m a point. Coventry City's points cost
£384k each, and Sheffield Wednesday's £411k, shortly before the club went
into administration.

The ratio of wages to turnover moved furthest at clubs that changed
division. Burnley's went from 70% to 114% in their season back in the
Championship. Leicester City's fell from 102% to 82% in their season back
in the Premier League, which is what promotion is for.

Most of the pyramid files very little. Of the 252 clubs in the top seven
tiers this season, 86 file full accounts. Another 111 file a balance sheet
and nothing else, 45 of them stating that they are entitled to withhold the
rest under section 444 of the Companies Act. Eleven are on the register
with nothing readable yet, and 44 could not be matched to a company at all.
Many of those are community benefit societies, which file their accounts
somewhere else.
