A football club is, legally, a company that plays football somewhere.
Whether it owns the somewhere is a separate question, and the answer is
"no" more often than the badge on the gate suggests.

Of the clubs in this season's top seven tiers whose landlord could be
established, about a third own their ground. Half are on council land.
The rest rent from a former chairman, a property company, a pension
fund, a rugby club or, in eleven cases, from the people who own the
club – which is not the same thing as the club owning it.

The pattern by tier is not the one money would predict. The
Championship has the highest share of clubs owning their ground, higher
than the Premier League, because the biggest modern stadiums were built
on public land: Manchester City, Brighton, West Ham and Tottenham all
play on council or public freehold, on leases long enough to outlive
everyone reading this. Below the Football League the council is the
landlord for most clubs, usually on terms that are kind until the
council needs the money.

The cases to watch are at both ends. **Sell the club, keep the
ground**: the owner holds the stadium in a separate company, often after
a sale-and-leaseback to get past the spending rules, and a buyer of the
club buys a tenant. **Leases running out**: Bradford City's lease from a
former chairman's family pension fund ends in 2028; Oxford United have
been licensees of a former owner since he sold them the club and kept
the stadium. A short lease is not a crisis until the landlord decides it
is one.
