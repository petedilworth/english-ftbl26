English football has no fixed number of divisions, and that is the first
thing worth understanding about it. It has a pyramid: a set of leagues
connected by promotion and relegation, narrow at the top where one division
covers the whole country, widening as it descends into parallel regional
divisions, until at the bottom it stops being centrally administered at all
and becomes a matter for county football associations. There is no floor
you can point to. A team of colleagues playing on a public pitch on Sunday
morning is, in principle, connected to the Premier League by an unbroken
chain of promotions.

The top four levels are the settled part. The Premier League runs 20 clubs;
the three divisions of the English Football League run 24 each. Those 92
clubs are what people usually mean by "professional football" in England,
and their membership is closed in a way the rest of the pyramid's is not —
the EFL is an association of 72 member clubs, and you join it by winning
promotion into it, not by applying.

Below them sits the National League System, which the Football Association
administers in six steps covering levels 5 to 10. For 2026/27 the FA
allocated **996 clubs across 48 divisions in 19 leagues**. Add the 92 above
and the top ten levels of English football contain 1,088 clubs — and that
is a count, not an estimate.

Past that, honesty requires vagueness. The eleventh level is a set of
regional feeder leagues run by county associations, and below those the
structure varies by region: some parts of the country run to sixteen
levels, others to twenty or more. The figure often quoted is that there are
around 7,000 clubs across roughly 20 levels. Both halves of that need care.
The 7,000 is an estimate arrived at by multiplying a division count by an
assumed average, not a count of anything; it refers to *teams* rather than
clubs, and a single club can enter several; and 20 is a regional maximum
rather than a national depth. What can be said plainly is that nobody
publishes an authoritative total, which is itself a fact about how the
lower pyramid works.

## Where the divisions multiply

The shape of the thing is set out in the first table at the foot of this
page. One division at level 5, two at level 6, four at level 7, eight at
level 8, sixteen at level 9, seventeen at level 10. Each split is
geographic: North and South at level 6; the Northern Premier, Southern and
Isthmian leagues carving up the country at levels 7 and 8; and
progressively tighter regions below that, until a division is a county or
two.

The reason is travel. A national fifth-tier division is affordable because
the clubs in it take enough money at the gate to pay for a coach to the far
end of the country on a Tuesday. A national twelfth-tier division would not
be, and the players would have to be back at work on Wednesday morning.

## Are the players paid?

Yes, and no, and it depends where — but not in the way the phrase
"professional or semi-professional" suggests. That phrase implies a line,
with full-time professionals above it and part-timers below. What the
accounts show is a gradient, and it is very steep long before anyone stops
being full-time.

All four Football League divisions are full-time in practice. Every one of
the 92 clubs employs its players to play football and nothing else. But the
second table below covers those four divisions and no others, and the
average wage bill falls by a factor of forty from top to bottom. A Premier
League squad and a League Two squad are both professional in exactly the
same sense, and they are not remotely the same job.

Level 5, the National League, is where the two models genuinely overlap.
It is constitutionally a semi-professional competition, and most of its
clubs nonetheless operate full-time; promotion into it is a common moment
for a club to convert, hiring players who until then combined football with
other work. Below that, part-time becomes the norm and then the only
option. Exactly where players stop being paid meaningfully is not something
this page will put a number on, because no reputable source publishes one —
the figures that circulate for wages at each step trace back to betting
blogs and forum posts rather than to the leagues, the FA or the players'
union.

What can be shown precisely is the contractual difference, which is
sharper than the wage difference and much less discussed. Players in the
Football League sign the standard Premier League and EFL contract. Below
it, across all six steps of the National League System, clubs use the FA's
Non-League Player Contract, a materially weaker instrument — and the
National League System's own rules define a "non-contract player" as one
paid no more than expenses, which is the formal mechanism by which a
semi-professional player is registered at all. When that contract was
rewritten for 2023/24, the proposed terms would have limited full injury
pay to twelve weeks in the National League and six weeks below it, after
which a club could reduce a player to statutory sick pay. The players'
union refused to support it, Step 1 club captains sought talks, strike
action was discussed, and the FA board approved an amended version with the
long-term-injury termination clause removed. That is the practical
difference between the two halves of the pyramid: not simply how much a
player earns, but what happens to them when they get hurt.

## The two boundaries that matter

Ask where non-league football begins and the answer is level 5 — that is
where EFL membership ends. Two clubs go down into it and two come up each
season, and the change is not only in status. A club promoted into League
Two must bring its ground up to a certified 4,000 capacity with 500 seats,
rising to 5,000 with 2,000 seats by its third season, and terracing must
meet the prescribed standard or be taken out of use. Ground grading has
denied promotion to clubs that won it on the pitch, which is why this site
records it as a category of its own.

But there is now a second boundary, and it falls a level lower. The
Football Governance Act 2025 created an independent regulator for English
football, and its licensing regime covers the top five tiers — 116 clubs,
the Premier League down to and including the National League. For questions
of ownership tests, financial sustainability and who may buy a club, the
meaningful line is now between levels 5 and 6, not 4 and 5. Anything
written about who oversees English clubs before 2025 predates it.

*The tier names above are the neutral ones. Sponsored names — Sky Bet for
the EFL divisions, and a sponsor for the National League that has changed
more than once — are what appear on the badges and in the broadcasts, and
they date faster than anything else on this page.*
