English football's finances swing harder between divisions than in almost
any other major league, because the gap in television income is enormous
and the move between divisions happens in a single season rather than
gradually. The Premier League's current broadcast deal (domestic and
international rights combined) runs to around £13.2 billion across four
years. Even a club finishing bottom of the table banks roughly £109
million in central distributions in a season — while a Championship club
with no parachute payments typically receives around £10–11 million. That
is the shape of the cliff: promotion and relegation aren't a step up or
down, they're closer to a tenfold change in central income, decided by
results over nine months.

**What promotion buys a club** is mostly that central distribution,
whatever a club's own finish inside it — the difference between 1st and
20th in the Premier League narrows the money considerably less than the
difference between being in the league at all and not. It also buys a
repricing of everything around the club: sponsorship, matchday pricing,
and the transfer value of the same players it already had.

**What relegation risks** is a mismatch that television income alone
doesn't fix. Wage bills built for Premier League survival — multi-year
player contracts, mostly — don't shrink the moment a club goes down, even
though the income funding them does, immediately. Parachute payments exist
specifically to soften that landing: relegated clubs currently receive
roughly 55% of a Premier League "equal share" payment in the first year
after relegation, 45% in the second, and 20% in the third, before central
income drops to the ordinary Championship level. That cushion is real —
recently relegated clubs have received on the order of £49 million in
their first season down — but it's also a source of competitive
distortion the EFL itself has flagged: clubs with parachute money can
outspend Championship clubs without it, which is part of why relegated
clubs often bounce straight back up, and why the clubs squeezed hardest
are the ones who've been down for a while rather than the ones who just
arrived.

**The regulatory response** is the EFL's Profitability and Sustainability
(P&S) rules, which cap how much a Championship club can lose over a
rolling three-year period — a figure most consistently cited at £39
million, with a modest upward adjustment reported for 2025/26. The
Premier League runs a comparable but separately administered system with
a substantially higher threshold, commonly cited at £105 million over
three years (reduced for clubs that spent part of that window in the
EFL). When a club breaches its threshold, the penalty is usually a direct
points deduction — turning an accounting outcome into a league-table one.
Deloitte's Annual Review of Football Finance has put the Championship's
aggregate wages-to-revenue ratio at 93–94% in recent seasons — meaning,
in effect, that most of what the division earns is already spoken for
before a ball is kicked, which is exactly the condition P&S rules exist
to contain.

**Why it's a cycle, not just a shock.** Parachute payments plus an
inflated squad often push a relegated club straight back up, which resets
the whole exposure for the next relegation rather than resolving it. This
site's own [yo-yo clubs](../yo-yo/index.html) and
[natural level](../natural-level/index.html) pages exist to
surface exactly that pattern: clubs whose level oscillates rather than
settles, because the financial incentives on both sides of the
promotion/relegation line reward overreach more than caution.
