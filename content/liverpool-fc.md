## Origins

Liverpool Football Club was formed in 1892 out of a boardroom defeat.
Anfield had been built in 1884 for Everton F.C., who rented it from local
brewer John Houlding; as the tenancy grew successful — Everton became
founder members of the Football League in 1888 and champions in 1891 —
rent and tension with their landlord rose alongside it. When Houlding
pushed to convert the club into a limited company on his terms, the board,
led by George Mahon, voted to leave and build Goodison Park instead,
taking the Everton name and most of the playing squad with them. Houlding
was left holding an empty ground and tried to keep the identity that had
just walked out on him: he first attempted to register his existing
company as the continuing Everton F.C., and when the Football Association
refused to recognise it as such, tried registering the new side as
"Everton Athletic." The FA rejected that too, unwilling to sanction two
Evertons, and Houlding was forced to a new name — Liverpool F.C., formally
constituted on 15 March 1892 — to play at a ground eight years older than
the club itself.

The new club inherited Everton's leftover kit along with the ground,
playing in blue-and-white shirts for its first four seasons before
switching to red in 1896, a change made explicitly to stop being confused
with the blue Everton had by then settled into at Goodison Park — "the
Reds" as a deliberate point of difference from the club it split from,
not an original identity.

## Trajectory

Two disasters, four years apart, defined the club's off-pitch history more
than anything on the pitch has.

On 29 May 1985, before the European Cup final against Juventus at
Brussels' Heysel Stadium, Liverpool supporters charged through a fence
into a nominally neutral section that was in practice occupied largely by
Italian fans; the resulting crush caused a concrete retaining wall to
collapse, killing 39 people, most of them Juventus supporters, and
injuring around 600. An ageing, poorly maintained stadium, inadequate
segregation and an under-resourced police presence all contributed, and
the match was played later that evening regardless. UEFA banned English
clubs from European competition indefinitely; most were readmitted after
five years, but Liverpool served a longer ban, not returning to Europe
until 1991-92. Twenty-six Liverpool supporters were extradited to Belgium
and tried; fourteen were convicted in April 1989 on a charge commonly
rendered in English as manslaughter, each sentenced to three years with
half suspended. Two Belgian officials — the Belgian FA's general secretary
and a police captain — were separately convicted of negligence over the
policing and organisation of the match, with suspended sentences of their
own.

On 15 April 1989, a crush developed in overcrowded central pens at
Hillsborough, Sheffield Wednesday's ground, during an FA Cup semi-final
against Nottingham Forest, after the police match commander ordered an
exit gate opened to relieve congestion outside without redirecting the
resulting inflow away from pens that were already full. Ninety-seven
people died — the final figure confirmed only in 2021, when Andrew Devine,
who had survived the disaster in a permanently vegetative state, died and
a coroner ruled his death, too, had been caused by it. In the disaster's
immediate aftermath, some police accounts falsely blamed fan behaviour for
what had happened; The Sun's front page five days later, headlined "THE
TRUTH," repeated claims that fans had picked victims' pockets and assaulted
police officers giving first aid, provoking a boycott of the paper on
Merseyside that has lasted decades. Those claims were established as false
by Lord Justice Taylor's 1990 report into the disaster, which found the
main cause to be a failure of police control and led directly to the legal
requirement for all-seater stadiums across the top two English divisions
by 1994-95. The original 1991 inquest verdicts of accidental death were
quashed after the Hillsborough Independent Panel's 2012 report found that
police statements had been altered to remove criticism of the force and
shift blame onto supporters; fresh inquests concluded in April 2016 with
verdicts of unlawful killing and an explicit finding that fans bore no
responsibility for the disaster. Subsequent criminal prosecutions produced
one conviction, of the Sheffield Wednesday club secretary for a
health-and-safety offence; the police match commander was tried twice for
gross negligence manslaughter and acquitted, and charges against other
officers and officials were dropped or resulted in acquittal — an outcome
bereaved families have continued to publicly contest, even as the 2016
inquest's core findings about the disaster's cause and the fans' innocence
stand.

A different, structural kind of off-pitch story runs through the Bill
Shankly era. Arriving in December 1959 to a Second Division club with
Anfield and the Melwood training ground both in disrepair, Shankly rebuilt
more than the playing squad: the informal staff meetings that became known
as "the Boot Room" — Shankly alongside Bob Paisley, Reuben Bennett, Joe
Fagan and Ronnie Moran — created a coaching culture and management
succession pipeline that outlasted him, with Paisley, Fagan and others
promoted from within it across the following two decades.

## Ownership & Finance

Tom Hicks and George Gillett completed a £218.9m takeover in February
2007, against existing club debts of £44.8m, financed substantially
through debt secured against the club itself rather than the buyers' own
capital — a structure comparable to the Glazer family's contemporaneous
takeover of Manchester United. That debt had grown to £350m by the end of
the 2009-10 season; a later restructuring shifted some of it onto a Hicks
holding company registered in the Cayman Islands, a move financial
reporting at the time characterised as reducing the figure on the club's
own books rather than genuinely reducing what was owed.

The crisis came to a head when a Royal Bank of Scotland loan to the
ownership group's holding company matured on 15 October 2010; failure to
repay it would have pushed the club into administration, carrying an
automatic points deduction and materially reducing its value. The
Liverpool board, chaired by Martin Broughton, voted to approve a sale to
New England Sports Ventures over Hicks and Gillett's objections; Hicks and
Gillett attempted to block it by trying to replace board members and
pursuing action through a Texas court. RBS obtained an injunction
preventing the board's removal, and Mr Justice Floyd ruled in the English
High Court that Hicks and Gillett did not hold an absolute veto over the
sale, reinstating the board's authority and nullifying the Texas
proceedings. New England Sports Ventures — renamed Fenway Sports Group the
following year — completed the £300m purchase on 15 October 2010.

## Infrastructure & Environment

Anfield predates Liverpool F.C. by eight years, built in 1884 for
Everton's tenancy and inherited when Everton left for Goodison Park. The
Spion Kop terrace, built in 1906, took its name from a Boer War hill where
a disproportionate number of soldiers from Liverpool regiments died in
January 1900 — a naming convention shared with several other English
grounds' standing ends of that era. It was converted to all-seating in
1994, its last standing match played that April, as a direct consequence
of the Taylor Report's requirement following Hillsborough.

Rather than relocate — an option considered and abandoned under Hicks and
Gillett, who had proposed a new stadium at nearby Stanley Park before that
plan was shelved — the club expanded Anfield in place instead: a Main
Stand rebuild opened in September 2016 added around 8,500 seats, and a
further expansion of the Anfield Road end completed for the 2023-24 season
brought total capacity to just over 61,000.
