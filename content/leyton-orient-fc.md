## Origins

The club began in 1881 as a **cricket** club — **Glyn Cricket Club**, formed by
old boys of Homerton College on waste ground off Glyn Road in Hackney, and named
for Glyn's Bank, then a byword for cricket patronage. It became Eagle Cricket
Club in about 1886, and on **3 March 1888**, at a meeting at 86 Dunlace Road, it
added a football section and changed its name to **Orient**.

The name is the club's best-known story and it deserves an honest label. The
account, given identically by the club, the supporters' club and everything
downstream of them, is that a committee member and player named **Jack R.
Dearing**, an employee of the **Orient Steam Navigation Company**, proposed it.
The shipping line certainly existed — constituted in 1878, running Australia
liners — and a Hackney clerk working for it in 1888 is entirely plausible. What
does not appear anywhere is a contemporary document: no minute-book entry, no
newspaper report. This is the account the club has always given, and it is
consistent rather than corroborated.

The name then moved with the money. **Clapton Orient** from 1898, adopted
explicitly to widen the club's catchment. **Leyton Orient** from October 1946,
after the club narrowly avoided bankruptcy. Back to **Orient** in 1966, and back
to **Leyton Orient** in 1987. A sixth change was proposed in 2012 — **London
Orient**, conditional on winning a share of the Olympic Stadium — and died with
the bid.

## Trajectory

**In December 1914 Clapton Orient's captain was the first footballer onto the
stage at a recruiting meeting in Fulham Town Hall, and nine of his teammates
followed him immediately.**

The meeting raised the 17th (Service) Battalion of the Middlesex Regiment — the
Footballers' Battalion — and the initial ten Orient players grew to **forty-one
players, staff, officials and supporters**, the largest contingent any single
club sent. That figure is worth stating carefully, because it is routinely
rendered as forty-one *players*, which it was not. The other qualification is
that Hearts had done it first, into a Scottish battalion in 1914, and McCrae's
was the explicit model for the Middlesex; Orient were the first **English
Football League** club to enlist together.

Three of them did not come back. **William Jonas**, killed at Delville Wood on
27 July 1916, aged 26, no known grave. **George Scott**, wounded on the Somme,
taken prisoner, died of wounds on 16 August. **Richard McFadden**, Military
Medal, company sergeant major, fatally wounded near Serre on 22 October and
died the next day, aged 27. Orient supporters paid for and unveiled a memorial
at Flers on the Somme in 2011, and a version of it stands at the National
Memorial Arboretum.

The rest of the club's trajectory is a study in institutions deciding things.

**In 1930 the Football League barred Orient from their own ground.** Money had
forced them out of Millfields Road to Lea Bridge Stadium, and weeks into the
season the League ruled the gap between the pitch and the speedway fence too
narrow and stopped them playing there until it was fixed. So **Clapton Orient
played two ordinary Second Division home fixtures at Wembley Stadium** — beating
Brentford 3–0 on 22 November 1930 in front of 10,300, and Southend 3–1 on 6
December in front of 2,500. Not a cup run. An administrative sanction that put
a struggling club into the national stadium and drew two and a half thousand
people.

**Their single season in the top division came in 1962/63**, after promotion as
Second Division runners-up, and it was comprehensive: bottom of twenty-two, six
wins, **21 points from 42 games, twelve behind safety**. It remains the only one.

And in **2016/17 they were relegated out of the Football League after 112 years**,
bottom of League Two on 36 points, relegation confirmed at Crewe on 22 April.
What surrounds that date is the subject of the next section. They came back as
**National League champions in 2018/19** on 89 points, three ahead of Solihull
Moors, and won League Two in 2022/23 on 91.

The only points deduction in the club's record is three, in 1997/98 — and our
own data carries it with the note that no independent source establishes what
it was for. They finished eleventh; it changed nothing.

## Ownership & Finance

**Barry Hearn sold Leyton Orient in July 2014 and kept the ground.** That
sentence explains most of what has happened since.

Hearn had taken control in March 1995 from an owner in financial difficulty and
chaired the club for nineteen years. In 2014 Matchroom Sport's ninety per cent
shareholding passed to **Francesco Becchetti** for a reported £4m — but the
stadium stayed with Matchroom, and the club became its tenant. Hearn said
publicly in 2017 that he regretted the sale.

The Becchetti period is best measured in institutional findings. On Boxing Day
2015, after a home win, he came onto the pitch and aimed a kick at the assistant
manager; an FA regulatory commission found him guilty of **violent conduct** and
banned him from six first-team matches with a £40,000 fine. He got through **ten
managers in under three years**. A separate Albanian extradition request over an
alleged hydropower fraud was **refused** — a district judge stayed the case,
finding a document submitted by the Albanian government "totally misleading",
and Albania did not appeal, so nothing has been established against him there
and this page says no more about it.

The end came through the courts. **HMRC petitioned to wind the club up on 2
February 2017** over roughly £250,000 of unpaid tax. In March the High Court gave
Becchetti until **12 June** to pay or sell. Staff and players went unpaid in
March; employees reported no communication from the owner and difficulty making
rent. The tax and wages were cleared in late April; relegation was confirmed on
22 April; and on **12 June the petition was dismissed** because the petition
debts — HMRC plus four other creditors who had joined it — had been paid, with
other creditors understood to remain outstanding. That is what kept it dangerous
for another ten days. **The sale completed on 22 June 2017.**

The buyer was **Eagle Investments 2017 Limited**, headed by **Nigel Travis**, a
lifelong supporter who was then chairman and chief executive of Dunkin' Brands,
with the Dallas businessman **Kent Teague** as principal investor — one man, not,
as often written, a family. The price was not disclosed; the club was stated to
be debt-free on completion. **A supporter representative was appointed to the
board.** The supporters' trust had raised £100,000 in a fortnight towards a
fighting fund during the crisis.

**David Gandler**, co-founder of the streaming company FuboTV, became majority
shareholder in April 2025 with 78.55 per cent; Travis remains chairman.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**Brisbane Road has flats in its corners, and the interesting part is who
bought the land.**

Orient sold **Samuel Beadie (Leyton) Limited** a 999-year lease on the West
Stand for £1.5m in 2005, leasing the stand back for £1 a year, and at an EGM on
**8 May 2006** shareholders approved selling strips of land behind two more
stands to the same company for **£1.25m**, for residential development — the
proceeds funding the North Stand, which opened for 2007/08. Samuel Beadie
(Leyton) Ltd was a consortium of which the club's own chairman, Barry Hearn,
was a half partner. So this was not a club selling corners to a developer; it
was a club selling corners to its chairman's company, to pay for a stand whose
construction depended on that company getting planning permission — a
prerequisite the supporters' trust recorded that shareholders were not told
about at the meeting.

The flats are ordinary private leasehold homes, sold and resold on the open
market, some with balconies over the pitch and marketed on exactly that basis.
Residents watch from them. The club owns none of it.

The ground is still not the club's. **Matchroom is the landlord**, the lease was
extended by twenty years in 2023, and Waltham Forest granted the stadium **Asset
of Community Value** status on a supporters' trust application — which gives a
community group the right to register interest and time to bid if it is ever put
up for sale. That listing is the only real protection the supporters have over a
ground they do not own, and they had to apply for it.

Then the Olympic Stadium, which is the other reason Orient's landlord matters.
The club fought West Ham's move to Stratford from 2011, first alongside
Tottenham on the argument that a local-authority contribution amounted to
unlawful state aid, then by judicial review. Hearn's case was that the bidding
body's own rules had not been followed; **Orient's bid was to groundshare, not
to take the stadium**. In 2013 Mr Justice Lewis refused all three of Orient's
applications, holding that none was arguable and that the decision was not
irrational, and refused permission to appeal. Orient dropped the objection in
2014.

A club a mile and a half from an 60,000-seat stadium built with public money,
playing in a ground it rents from its former chairman, with flats in the
corners owned by strangers. There is no tidier illustration on this site of what
it means to not own the thing you play in.
