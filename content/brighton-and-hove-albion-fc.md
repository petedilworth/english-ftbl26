## Origins

Brighton & Hove Albion was founded on 24 June 1901 at a meeting in the Seven
Stars Inn on Ship Street. It was the third attempt at senior football in the
town in as many years: the professional Brighton United had collapsed, and
the amateur Brighton & Hove Rangers, formed in 1900, handed over its Southern
League place to the new club and was not heard of again. Albion was
registered as Brighton & Hove United, then renamed before a ball was kicked
after objections from Hove F.C. The club moved to the Goldstone Ground in
1902 and was elected to the Football League in 1920. Its "Seagulls"
nickname is much more recent: a short-lived "Dolphins" identity in the
mid-1970s gave way to a terrace chant war with Crystal Palace fans — their
"Eagles, Eagles" answered with "Seagulls, Seagulls" — officially adopted,
badge included, by 1977.

## Trajectory

Albion spent ninety-five years at the Goldstone. The tenure ended on 26 April
1997, and the season it ended in was existential rather than merely bad: the
club sat bottom of the bottom division, at one point twelve points adrift,
carrying a two-point deduction imposed that December for a pitch invasion —
itself a protest against the sale of the ground. Survival came with a draw at
Hereford United on the final day, sparing Albion the distinction of being the
first former top-flight club and FA Cup finalist to drop into the Conference.

From there the club climbed to the Premier League and to European
qualification. That arc is almost entirely a story about a stadium: the
fourteen years without a permanent home, and what happened once one existed.

## Ownership & Finance

In 1993 a group led by Bill Archer acquired a controlling stake for £56.25,
undertaking to provide the £800,000 needed to see off a winding-up order. A
clause in the club's constitution preventing shareholders from profiting from
asset sales in the event the club folded was removed. In July 1995 the board
announced the sale of the Goldstone freehold to a property company for £7.4
million, most of which serviced debt. The club was left without a ground and
without a replacement.

The Football League's two-point deduction is the only formal sanction on the
public record arising from this period; no FA commission finding or court
judgment against the directors appears in it, and none should be inferred.
Their involvement ended in 1997, when a consortium led by lifelong supporter
Dick Knight took control with liquidation imminent.

The modern era begins in 2009 with Tony Bloom, a Brighton-born professional
gambler who took majority control of his boyhood club and put over £90
million of his own money into building its stadium. Bloom's fortune comes
from Starlizard, a private sports-betting analytics syndicate he founded in
2006, and the same statistical approach runs through Brighton's recruitment:
proprietary models are used to identify undervalued players in
under-scouted leagues and positions, sign them cheaply, develop them, and
sell at a large profit — Moisés Caicedo (bought for £4m, sold to Chelsea
for £100m), Alexis Mac Allister (£6.9m, sold to Liverpool for £35m) and
Marc Cucurella (sold to Chelsea for £56m) among the clearest examples.
Brighton are not unique in this approach — Brentford, under a similarly
betting-derived ownership model, runs a close parallel — but the two are
the most prominent examples of it in English football.

Bloom holds football interests elsewhere, and the distinction matters:
these are his personal shareholdings, not the club's, structured to stay
within multi-club ownership rules rather than build an integrated network.
He held a minority stake in Belgian side Royale Union Saint-Gilloise,
reduced when both clubs were on course for the same UEFA competition; in
2025 he paid £9.86m for a 29% non-voting stake in Scottish club Heart of
Midlothian, and separately agreed a stake in Australian side Melbourne
Victory. Player pathways sometimes cross the stakes directly — winger
Simon Adingra was loaned to Union Saint-Gilloise to develop before joining
Brighton's first team, following the same route as Kaoru Mitoma via a loan
in Belgium.

## Infrastructure & Environment

The fourteen years between the Goldstone and the Amex define the modern club.
For 1997–98 and 1998–99, Albion played "home" matches at Gillingham's
Priestfield Stadium, over seventy miles away — the longest such exile in
modern English league football, and one that gutted attendances. From 1999 to
2011 the club played at the Withdean Stadium, a municipal athletics ground in
a residential Brighton suburb, with a running track between crowd and pitch,
temporary stands and a capacity around 6,900.

Withdean was always a stopgap, and the search for a permanent home became one
of the most protracted planning battles in English sport. After a public
inquiry, the Deputy Prime Minister granted permission for a stadium at Falmer
in October 2005. In April 2006 he acknowledged approving it on a mistaken
understanding of how much of the site fell on the neighbouring district's
side of the boundary, and in November 2006 the permission was quashed
following a legal challenge on that ground of factual error. It was
re-affirmed by a different Secretary of State on 25 July 2007. The completed
stadium was handed over on 31 May 2011.

Unlike many clubs that endured comparable exiles, Albion owns its ground
outright — which is precisely why the fourteen years were survivable and why
the two decades since look so different.
