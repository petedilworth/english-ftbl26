## Origins

Shrewsbury Town was founded in 1886, after Shropshire Wanderers had folded and
Castle Blues had faded — a successor to them rather than a continuation of
either, and the club records no lineage from them.

Its route to the Football League ran through three separate non-league
competitions: founder members of the Shropshire & District League in 1890,
into the Birmingham & District League from 1895, and the Midland League from
1937, which they won three times. **They were elected to the Football League in
1950**, when it expanded from 88 clubs to 92 — the same expansion that took
Colchester and Gillingham into the southern section, both of whom have pages
in this project. Shrewsbury polled thirty votes, comfortably the most of any
applicant.

The detail worth keeping is that election followed the campaign rather than the
form. **1949/50, the season that got them in, was their worst Midland League
finish: tenth.**

They were then moved. After one season in the Third Division North they were
**transferred to the Third Division South for 1951/52**, a Shropshire club
reassigned by administrative convenience, and stayed there until the divisions
went national in 1958.

## Trajectory

**Shrewsbury Town won the Welsh Cup six times and it never once took them to
Europe.**

The wins came in 1891, 1938, 1977, 1979, 1984 and 1985 — more than any other
English club has managed. And the Welsh Cup carried a place in the European Cup
Winners' Cup, **unless the winner was English**, in which case it passed to the
last surviving Welsh club. That happened five times in the competition's
history and **four of them were Shrewsbury**.

So the club's record reads: six national cups, no European tie, ever. In 1977
the place went to Cardiff City, whom they had just beaten in the final. In 1984
it went to Wrexham, whom they had just beaten in the final — and Wrexham took
it to Portugal and knocked out Porto on away goals.

This site's Cardiff City page records the other end of the same rule: from
1995/96, UEFA held that the Welsh Cup could only feed a European place if clubs
playing in England were excluded, which barred Cardiff, Swansea, Wrexham and
Newport from a competition that had been their route into Europe. The same
sweep caught the English border clubs — Shrewsbury, Hereford, Chester. The
difference is what it cost them. **Cardiff lost a door. Shrewsbury lost an
entry to a competition whose prize they had never been allowed to collect.**

The rest of the trajectory is a club that has never played in the top
division. Ten seasons in the second tier, 1979/80 to 1988/89, with a best
finish of eighth. **Relegated out of the Football League in 2003** after
fifty-three years, twenty-fourth of twenty-four — and back at the first
attempt, third in the Conference and up through the play-offs, beating
Aldershot on penalties. The financial mechanism of that relegation is worth
recording: the central League payment halved, from £200,000 to £100,000.

**Shrewsbury have never had a point deducted and have never been through an
insolvency process.** A sanction in July 2026 — a one-window transfer-fee
restriction for a late payment to another club under a loan agreement, which
the club settled on discovery — is a registration embargo, not a deduction,
and this page does not upgrade it.

## Ownership & Finance

**Roland Wycherley** bought the club in July 1996 and has owned it ever since —
thirty years in 2026, one of the longest single ownerships in the English game.
He inherited £750,000 of debt, and has said the sale of a defender to Crewe for
£500,000 in October 1997 is what kept the club alive. He and his family trusts
hold **65.42%** of the ordinary shares; the club publishes no other
shareholder.

The finances are those of a club living close to the line. An operating loss of
£3.1m reported in 2024, turnover around £6.8m against a £1.6m loss to June that
year, narrowed to £600,000 the following year on higher income and lower costs
— and **£96,000 in the bank** at one point, with the chairman and his
vice-chairman putting in £900,000 of interest-free loans across a season.

Wycherley has been trying to sell since September 2024. Two deals collapsed, in
March 2025 and again six months later. As of mid-2026 an American consortium
had agreed terms and was going through both the EFL's Owners' and Directors'
Test and the new Independent Football Regulator's assessment — further than any
previous bid, and not completed.

**Shrewsbury is not a fan-owned club**, and the supporters' contribution is
something more specific and more interesting than a shareholding. **In the
summer of 2018 Shrewsbury installed the first rail seats at an English league
ground** — 555 of them at the back of the South Stand — and they were paid for
largely by **a crowdfunding campaign run by the club's Supporters' Parliament**,
which passed its revised £62,500 target in October 2017. The supporters did not
buy a stake in the club. They bought a stand.

## Infrastructure & Environment

**For most of a century, balls kicked out of Gay Meadow landed in the River
Severn, and a man went after them in a coracle.**

He was **Fred Davies**, a Shrewsbury coracle maker, and this is documented
rather than folklore: he was filmed by the BBC in 1975, the Coracle Society
records him, and the Davies family are described as the last traditional
coracle family in England. He was paid **five shillings a ball** — twenty-five
pence — still no more than fifty pence by the mid-1980s. It ran for around
forty-five years, from the 1930s or 1940s, and he was succeeded by his nephew.
The sources disagree on when it ended, giving 1979, 1986 and 1987 while all
citing the same forty-five years, so this page will not pick one.

Two corrections to the popular version. **The coracle did not last until the
ground closed** — in Gay Meadow's later years the boat was given up for a long
pole with a net. And the two Fred Davieses of Shrewsbury Town are different
men: the coracle maker, and the manager who won the Third Division title in
1994.

The river was not only picturesque. **Gay Meadow sat on low ground inside a
loop of the Severn and flooded** — matches postponed in 1998 and 2000 when the
pitch, the dressing rooms and the offices went under, and the club eventually
built its offices **on stilts**. That is the infrastructure story, and it is
the reason a club leaves a ground it has had for ninety-seven years.

The last match there was on 5 May 2007. The site is now a housing development
called The Old Meadow, and one of the Davies coracles — having spent time at the
National Football Museum — came home to Shrewsbury in January 2020 and is
displayed at the new ground. The club's official podcast is called *The
Coracle*.

**The new stadium opened in July 2007 on a greenfield site at Oteley Road**,
holding 9,875, built for **£11.2m** from a mixture of club resources, grants,
loans and the sale of Gay Meadow. **The club owns it.** After eight batches of
these pages, that sentence has become the exception rather than the rule — and
one of its stands is named after the man who has owned the club for thirty
years. The name on the ground itself is sold and resold: Greenhous, then
Montgomery Waters, and since 2023 a Shrewsbury marketing agency.
